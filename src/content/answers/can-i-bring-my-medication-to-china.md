Yes, for ordinary medicines in quantities that are plainly for your own use. The complication is that **some medicines that are routine prescriptions in Europe, North America and Australia are controlled substances in China**, and for those there is a specific rule naming specific documents.

This page is orientation, not legal or medical advice. It tells you which regime your medicine falls under and what the Chinese text actually says, so you can check your own drug before you fly rather than at a customs desk.

## Ordinary medicines: personal use, reasonable quantity

For everything that is not a controlled substance, the operating principle at Chinese customs is **self-use and reasonable quantity**. A course of blood-pressure tablets, an inhaler, an antibiotic, a contraceptive, an antihistamine, an EpiPen — these travel in as personal effects.

What [this site already tells you six times over](/plan/china-packing-list/) is right, and worth repeating because it is what makes the reasonable-quantity judgement easy: **original labelled packaging, plus a copy of the prescription.** A box with a pharmacy label and your name on it is self-evidently personal. A bag of loose tablets is not.

## The category that is regulated separately

China's **麻醉药品和精神药品管理条例** (*májuì yàopǐn hé jīngshén yàopǐn guǎnlǐ tiáolì*, Regulations on the Administration of Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances) was issued as **State Council Order No. 442** on 3 August 2005 and took effect on 1 November 2005. It has been amended three times, most recently by a State Council decision of **6 December 2024**, in force since **20 January 2025**.

**Article 44** is the provision that concerns a traveller, and its number survived that amendment:

> 因治疗疾病需要，个人凭医疗机构出具的医疗诊断书、本人身份证明，可以携带单张处方最大用量以内的麻醉药品和第一类精神药品；携带麻醉药品和第一类精神药品出入境的，由海关根据自用、合理的原则放行。

Where treatment requires it, an individual may carry narcotic drugs and first-category psychotropic drugs **within the maximum quantity of a single prescription**, on the strength of **a medical diagnosis certificate issued by a medical institution** and **proof of their own identity**. For carrying such drugs across the border, **customs releases them on the personal-use and reasonable principle.**

Two things follow that are easy to miss.

**The ceiling is a prescription, not a number.** The regulation caps you at one prescription's maximum quantity — and the 2024 amendment rewrote **Article 40** so that the maximum quantity of a single prescription is what the State Council health department prescribes. The regulation sets the ceiling by reference. It does not publish a figure you can look up and apply to your own drug, so do not plan around one.

**A diagnosis certificate is not the same as a prescription.** The article names 医疗诊断书 (*yīliáo zhěnduàn shū*) — a doctor's diagnosis document — alongside identity proof. If your medicine may be in this category, ask your prescriber for a signed letter stating the condition, the drug, the dose and the duration, not just a repeat-prescription slip.

## What to actually do

- **Check your specific drug before you book**, not after. Ask your own pharmacist or prescriber whether it is a controlled substance, and if it is, ask your nearest Chinese embassy or consulate. Common prescriptions abroad that fall under control in China include stimulant medicines and several strong painkillers and sedatives.
- **Declare it.** If your medicine is in the controlled category, go through the declaration channel and present the papers. Carrying a controlled drug in without meeting the conditions is treated as smuggling, not as a paperwork slip.
- **Carry it in your hand luggage**, in its box, with the documents in the same bag.
- **Do not plan to replace it locally.** Chinese pharmacies are excellent and cheap, but [the labels are in Chinese and formulations differ](/guides/health-and-safety-in-china/), and a foreign prescription is not dispensable.
- **Traditional remedies run the other way too.** Preparations containing protected wildlife ingredients are prohibited to carry, and that is a rule about what you take *out* as much as in.

Hong Kong and Macau administer their own dangerous-drugs and import law, so none of the above describes bringing medicine into either of them.
