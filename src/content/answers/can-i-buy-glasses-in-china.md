**Yes.** Prescription spectacles are made and dispensed at scale in China, and a finished pair is a routine purchase rather than a specialist errand. What is worth knowing is what stands behind the counter legally, because it changed recently and it is not the same for glasses and for lenses.

## The standard for a finished pair stopped being compulsory

Until this year a dispensed pair of glasses in China was governed by **GB 13511.1-2011**, *配装眼镜 第1部分：单光和多焦点* — Assembled Spectacles, Part 1: Single-vision and multifocal. The **GB** without a **/T** mattered: it was a **compulsory** national standard.

The national standards catalogue now records that standard as **废止**, abolished, with an abolition date of **1 March 2026**. It was replaced in full by **GB/T 13511.1-2025**, retitled *配装眼镜 第1部分：单焦和多焦定配眼镜*. The standard plan behind the revision records its 标准性质 — the nature of the standard — as **推荐性**: recommended.

The subject changed hands as well. The 2011 standard was administered by the **Ministry of Industry and Information Technology**; the revision that replaced it is administered by the **National Technical Committee on Optometry and Optics Standardization**. An industrial product became an optometric one. Part 2, covering progressive lenses, made the same move to GB/T on the same day. So since **1 March 2026**, the technical rules for a finished pair of glasses in China are advisory rather than mandatory, and the responsibility for a shop meeting them shifted from law to practice. [What GB and GB/T mean, and why a standard can change its force](/food/chinese-wine/) is set out elsewhere on this site; this is a live instance of it.

## Contact lenses are in a different legal universe

Spectacles are goods. Lenses that sit on your eye are not.

:::warn A shop selling contacts needs a licence a spectacle shop does not
The market regulator in Tongliao puts the classification plainly in a consumer notice of **18 December 2023**:

> 隐形眼镜（美瞳）及其护理产品在我国属于第三类医疗器械

**Contact lenses, including cosmetic coloured ones, and their care solutions are Class III medical devices in China** — the highest of the three risk classes. Two consequences follow, and the same notice states both.

**The seller needs a licence.** 经营此类产品的企业需要取得《医疗器械经营许可证》 — a business selling these products must hold a **Medical Device Operating Licence**. That is a permission applied for and granted, not a filing.

**The box needs a number.** The notice tells consumers to check whether the packaging carries a **医疗器械注册证编号**, a medical device registration certificate number, along with the manufacturer, production date and expiry.
:::

So the same shopfront can sell you spectacles under a recommended standard and contact lenses under the strictest device regime China operates. Nothing on the sign distinguishes the two.

## What to do

- **Bring your current prescription**, or expect a refraction on the spot. A written prescription travels better than a guess.
- **Ask what standard the lenses are made to.** The answer is now a claim rather than a legal requirement, which makes asking more useful, not less.
- **For contacts, look for the licence on the wall and the registration number on the box.** Both are things the regulator tells its own citizens to check.
- **Keep the receipt and the prescription together.** [If the goods turn out not to be what they were sold as](/guides/sold-a-fake-in-china/), that pairing is the claim.
- **Do not buy cosmetic lenses from a stall or a general shop.** A seller without the operating licence is outside the regime described above entirely.
- **Hong Kong and Macau regulate optical goods and medical devices under their own law**, so neither the abolished standard, its replacement nor the device classification governs a purchase made there.

The short version: glasses in China are cheap, quick and now formally governed by advice; contact lenses are cheap, quick and governed as medical devices. Treat the two counters as two different shops.
