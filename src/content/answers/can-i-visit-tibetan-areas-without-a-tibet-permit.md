The permit requirement is administrative rather than cultural, and the administrative boundary is not the cultural one. This is the most useful thing a traveller can know about visiting Tibetan China.

## What the permit actually covers

The **Tibet Travel Permit** is required for the **Tibet Autonomous Region** — Lhasa, Shigatse, the Everest north side, Ngari. Inside that boundary a foreign passport holder cannot travel independently: you need the permit, a licensed agency, a booked itinerary and a guide.

Outside it, none of that applies.

## Where the Tibetan areas outside the TAR are

Historic Tibet extended well beyond the modern autonomous region, and the areas that were **Kham** and **Amdo** now sit inside neighbouring provinces:

- **Qinghai** — the northeastern plateau, including the great monastery complexes and the grasslands around Qinghai Lake.
- **Western Sichuan** — the Garzê and Ngawa prefectures, high, monastery-dense, and reachable by road from Chengdu.
- **Northwest Yunnan** — around **Shangri-La** at 3,200 m, the gentlest introduction to the plateau and the easiest to add to an existing route.

These are not consolation prizes. They hold working monasteries, Tibetan-language communities, and landscape at genuine altitude — and you reach them on an ordinary Chinese visa or visa-free entry, with a bus ticket.

## What you give up, and what you gain

**You give up** the Potala Palace, the Jokhang, and Everest Base Camp on the north side. Those are in the TAR and there is no way around it.

**You gain** independence. No fixed itinerary, no guide at your shoulder, no agency timeline, and no risk of the region closing to foreigners the week you planned to fly — the TAR shuts periodically, most consistently around March.

You also gain cost. A guided Tibet trip is expensive by necessity; Qinghai and western Sichuan are ordinary Chinese travel prices.

## Altitude still applies

The permit is the only thing that changes. **Shangri-La sits at 3,200 m** and much of western Sichuan and Qinghai is higher, so acclimatisation, hydration and a slow first two days matter exactly as much as they would in Lhasa. Check that your travel insurance covers activity above 3,000 m — many standard policies do not.

## If Lhasa is the point

If the Potala is what you came for, there is no substitute and the permit is the price. It is arranged only through a licensed Tibetan agency as part of a booked tour, with roughly 15–20 days of processing — see [how to get a Tibet Travel Permit](/answers/how-do-i-get-a-tibet-travel-permit/).
