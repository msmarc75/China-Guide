This one is worth stating plainly because the mistake is so common, and because getting it wrong is more awkward than doing nothing at all.

## What people actually do

**A handshake** in any formal or business setting. Chinese handshakes tend to be lighter and held slightly longer than the firm Western grip — do not squeeze, and do not read softness as disinterest.

**A nod and a smile** in casual settings. Greeting a shopkeeper, a hotel receptionist or someone you pass needs nothing more.

**Nothing physical at all** is completely normal. China is not a greeting-by-contact culture; hugging or cheek-kissing someone you have just met will be endured rather than enjoyed.

## Where a slight bow does appear

A small forward inclination — more of a deep nod than a bow — shows up as a marker of respect or thanks, often paired with a handshake toward someone notably senior or elderly. It is a modest gesture, not the formal bow of Japanese practice.

Follow rather than initiate. If someone inclines toward you, return it. Do not open with one.

:::warn Business cards get both hands
Offer and receive a card with both hands, Chinese side facing the recipient. When you receive one, read it properly before putting it away, and never write on it or push it into a back pocket in front of the person. This ritual is observed carefully and noticing it earns you credit.
:::

## What to say

**你好** (*nǐ hǎo*) works universally. **您好** (*nín hǎo*) is its respectful form, worth using with anyone older or senior.

You will be asked **吃了吗** — "have you eaten?" — which is a greeting rather than an invitation. Answer yes and it has done its job.

## The gestures to avoid instead

Beckoning with an upturned finger is rude; wave with your palm down and fingers together. Pointing at people with a finger is impolite — use an open hand. Neither is likely to cause offence coming from an obvious foreigner, but both are easy to get right.
