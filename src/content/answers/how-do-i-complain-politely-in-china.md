Complaining works in China. It just runs on different rules from the ones a Western visitor arrives with, and the usual escalation — get louder, get firmer, ask for a manager — is precisely the wrong direction.

## Why volume fails

**Face** (面子, *miànzi*) is the standing you have in front of other people. A public dressing-down takes it away, and the person you have embarrassed now has two problems: your complaint, and their own position in front of colleagues and other customers.

The predictable result is that they become less able to help, not more. What looks like stubbornness is usually someone with no way to concede without a further loss.

## What works instead

- **Describe the situation, not the person.** "The room is very noisy" solves more than "you gave me a bad room". The first is a problem you and they can look at together; the second is an accusation.
- **Say what you want.** Chinese service culture responds much better to a specific request than to expressed displeasure. "Could I move to a quieter room?" beats "this is unacceptable".
- **Lower your voice rather than raising it**, and move the conversation somewhere with fewer onlookers if you can. Quiet and private is where problems get solved.
- **Give them a way to say yes.** Offering an out — "I know it may not be possible, but…" — lets someone act without appearing to have been wrong.
- **Be patient with the first no.** Front-line staff frequently have no discretion. A refusal often means "I am not authorised", not "no".

## When you are told nothing can be done

This is usually true of the person saying it. Systems in China are rule-bound, and [very little discretion sits at the counter](/culture/how-china-works/) — a rule that does not fit your case is a rule that does not fit your case.

Two things help:

- **Ask who can decide**, politely and without implying the person in front of you is failing. "Is there someone who can approve this?" is a normal question, not an escalation.
- **Ask in writing, through an app.** Hotels, airlines and platforms handle written complaints through WeChat or their own apps, and the written channel often has more latitude than the desk.

## Where you genuinely have recourse

- **Hotels** booked through an international platform: raise it with the platform as well as the property.
- **Taxis**: use DiDi rather than a street cab, because the trip is logged, the fare is fixed in the app and there is a complaint route attached to a record.
- **Shops and markets**: bargaining disputes are not complaints. Walk away instead — it is the strongest position you have.

## The one thing not to do

Do not threaten, and do not invoke your embassy over a service problem. It reads as wildly disproportionate, it will not work, and it ends the conversation you were trying to have.

Almost everything a traveller runs into is a small logistical problem, and small logistical problems in China get solved by calm, specific, unembarrassing requests.
