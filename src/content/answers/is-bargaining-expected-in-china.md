The advice to "always haggle in China" is a decade out of date and was never true of most transactions. Modern urban China runs on fixed, displayed, QR-scanned prices.

## Where haggling is normal

Tourist-facing markets are the main case — the silk and pearl markets in Beijing, the fabric and antique markets in Shanghai, souvenir stalls at scenic sites, and street vendors selling clothing, bags, jewellery or trinkets without marked prices.

The tell is simple: **no price label means negotiation is expected.** A displayed price means it is not.

## Where it is not

Supermarkets, convenience shops, department stores, branded outlets, pharmacies, bookshops, restaurants, cafés, ticket counters and anything with a till. Metered taxis and DiDi are fixed too. Trying to negotiate in these places produces polite confusion, not a discount.

## How it actually works

The opening price at a tourist market bears little relation to what the item sells for. Name a figure far below it, expect to meet somewhere in between, and stay good-humoured throughout — this is a routine and slightly theatrical exchange, not a confrontation.

The one genuinely effective move is walking away. If the vendor lets you go, you were near the real price. If they call you back, you were not.

Two practical helpers: type numbers into a phone screen and pass it back and forth, which removes the language problem entirely; and decide before you start what the object is worth *to you*, then hold to it.

:::warn Do not win too hard
Grinding a street vendor down over a trivial sum to prove a point is unpleasant to watch and reflects badly on you. Get to a fair price, pay it, and enjoy the object.
:::

## The scam-adjacent cases

Be wary of anyone who approaches *you* — outside major tourist sites, invitations to a tea ceremony, an art student's exhibition or a nearby bar are a well-documented setup ending in a wildly inflated bill. That is not bargaining, and the defence is declining to go rather than negotiating.
