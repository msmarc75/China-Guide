China is not a backpacker-cheap country in the way parts of Southeast Asia are, but it delivers far more for the money than its economic size suggests.

## Where China is cheap

**Transport.** This is the standout. High-speed rail costs a fraction of the equivalent journey in Europe, runs to the minute, and connects almost everywhere you want to go. City metros are priced by distance and are among the cheapest of any major world city. Taxis and DiDi are inexpensive by Western standards.

**Food.** You can eat extremely well without spending much. Noodle shops, street stalls, dumpling counters and canteen-style restaurants serve full meals for a small fraction of a Western restaurant bill. Eating badly in China takes effort.

**Domestic flights.** Competitive and often cheap, particularly outside holiday periods.

## Where the money actually goes

**The international flight.** For most visitors this is the single largest line item, frequently more than everything else combined.

**Hotels in tier-one cities.** Beijing and Shanghai are priced like major world capitals. Mid-size cities cost dramatically less for equivalent quality — one of the strongest arguments for going beyond the obvious three or four stops.

**Attraction tickets.** Individually modest, but China charges for things that are free elsewhere: temples, gardens, old towns, park sections, and often a separate ticket for the interesting building inside the site you already paid to enter. Over a fortnight these add up more than people budget for.

:::warn The expensive mistake is the itinerary, not the spending
Cramming five cities into ten days means five sets of intercity transfers and five hotel check-ins. Fewer cities with longer stays costs less and shows you more.
:::

## Timing changes the price

Chinese New Year and the October National Day week send domestic transport and hotel prices to their annual peak while half the country is on the move. Avoiding those two windows is the largest single saving available to you.
