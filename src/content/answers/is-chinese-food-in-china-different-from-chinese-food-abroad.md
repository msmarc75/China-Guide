The honest framing is not that food abroad is fake. It is that you have been eating one dialect of a language and are about to hear the rest of it.

## Where the food abroad came from

The great waves of Chinese emigration in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries left disproportionately from **Guangdong and Fujian** on the southern coast, so the restaurants that followed cooked what those emigrants knew. Cantonese cooking is the base layer of Chinese food in Britain, the United States, Australia and much of Europe.

Then it adapted, because it had to. Ingredients were unavailable and were substituted. Palates expected sweetness and thicker sauces. Some dishes were invented outright for the new market and have no Chinese ancestor at all. The result is a real cuisine with its own history — it is just not a sample of what people eat in China.

## What actually changes when you arrive

**Sweetness drops sharply.** The thick, sweet, glossy sauce is largely an export adaptation. Chinese cooking uses sugar, but as one note among several rather than the dominant one.

**Texture becomes a stated goal.** Chinese diners talk about texture — springy, crisp, slippery, bouncy — with the seriousness Western diners reserve for flavour. Dishes that seem to have little taste often have texture as the point.

**Restaurants specialise.** The long menu covering every region is an export format. In China a restaurant usually does one region, and frequently one dish, better than a generalist ever could.

**Familiar dishes taste different.** Kung pao chicken (宫保鸡丁, *gōngbǎo jīdīng*) exists and is excellent, but the Sichuan original is drier, more savoury and carries the numbing tingle of the peppercorn rather than a sweet-sour glaze.

## What you have almost certainly never eaten

This is the exciting part. Whole traditions are effectively absent abroad:

- **Hunan** (湘菜, *xiāngcài*) — chilli heat without the numbing element, built on chopped salted chillies and smoked meats.
- **Guizhou** — sour and fermented rather than simply hot.
- **Chaoshan** — eastern Guangdong's own cuisine, distinct from Cantonese, with extraordinary seafood and beef hotpot.
- **Uyghur and northwestern** cooking — hand-pulled noodles, cumin lamb, breads baked in a tandoor-like oven.
- **Yunnan** — mushrooms, rice noodles, herbs and dairy, closer to Southeast Asia than to Beijing.

## What this means for a trip

Two habits get you most of the value. **Eat the local speciality wherever you are** rather than ordering what you already know — the thing a city is famous for is famous locally, not just in guidebooks. And **treat the first week as recalibration**: some things will taste under-seasoned or strange for a few days, and then stop.

Some dishes genuinely are better abroad for a particular palate, and there is nothing wrong with saying so. But the range on offer inside China is a different order of magnitude, and it is the reason to eat adventurously rather than safely.
