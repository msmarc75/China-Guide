For anyone avoiding dairy, China is one of the easier countries in the world to eat in — and the reason is structural rather than a matter of substitutions being available.

## Why it is barely there

Han Chinese cooking developed without a dairy tradition. Fat comes from pork, oil and, in Buddhist cooking, from sesame and peanut. The role cheese and cream play elsewhere is filled by fermented soy — soy sauce, fermented bean paste, fermented tofu — which delivers savoury depth by a different route.

The practical consequence is that a dish is rarely made non-dairy by request, because it never contained dairy to begin with. Stir-fries, braises, noodle soups, dumplings, roasts and almost the whole of the street-food repertoire are dairy-free as standard.

**Milk itself is now widely drunk**, and supermarkets are full of it. That is a modern development and it has not worked its way into savoury cooking.

## Where dairy genuinely appears

**Yunnan** is the real exception, and it is worth seeking out rather than avoiding. The Bai people of Dali make **乳扇** (*rǔshàn*), a fan-shaped sheet of cheese made by curdling fresh cow's milk with a fruit-acid whey — usually from quince or smoked plum — then stretching it and drying it on canes. It is recorded as early as the Ming dynasty and is one of the province's celebrated oddities: *milk sold as fans*. Eaten grilled, deep-fried, raw, or shredded into the sweet second cup of the Bai three-course tea.

**乳饼** (*rǔbǐng*) is its cousin — a pressed block, made in Yunnan from goat's or cow's milk by the same acid-curdling principle, close enough to tofu in method that it is sometimes called milk tofu.

**Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang and Tibet** all have real dairy cultures: yoghurt, dried curd, milk tea, and in Tibet the butter that goes into both the tea and the temple lamps.

**Modern cafés, bakeries and Western chains** are everywhere in Chinese cities and use dairy exactly as they would anywhere. A bakery pastry is not a safe assumption.

## If you are lactose intolerant

You will find this easy, with two small habits:

- **Milk tea is milky.** The bubble-tea shops on every corner use dairy or a dairy-derived creamer by default. Ask, or choose a plain tea.
- **Watch the bakery counter and the breakfast buffet**, not the restaurant menu.

## If you are vegan

Dairy is the least of your problems in China. The things that quietly make a vegetable dish non-vegan are **lard, meat stock, oyster sauce and dried shrimp**, none of which anyone considers meat when you ask. That is a different question and worth reading about separately.

The reliable answer in both cases is a **Buddhist vegetarian restaurant**, which excludes dairy and eggs along with everything else.
