English is taught throughout the Chinese school system, so a great many people have studied it. Far fewer use it, and confidence drops sharply outside international-facing jobs. A hotel receptionist in Shanghai will manage; the person selling you noodles will not, and neither will your taxi driver.

Assume no English in Chengdu, Xi'an, Guilin and everywhere smaller — including at train stations, which is the one that surprises people most.

## What actually closes the gap

This matters far less than it did a decade ago, and the fixes are practical rather than linguistic.

**Screenshots of addresses in Chinese characters.** The highest-value five minutes of preparation on the whole trip. Save your hotel's name and address in characters before leaving in the morning. It works offline, works on a dying battery, and solves the taxi problem completely.

**Photo translation.** Pleco's camera OCR reads a menu or a sign in real time and works entirely offline. Google Translate's camera mode does the same if your connection reaches Google. Baidu Translate and Youdao work on any Chinese connection.

**Pointing.** Point at a photo menu, a display case, or a dish on someone else's table and say **这个** (*zhè ge*, "this one"). This is not a workaround — it is what Chinese diners do themselves, and it works in every restaurant, market and shop in the country.

**DiDi.** Ride-hailing removes pronunciation from the equation entirely, because you type the destination rather than saying it. It has a full English interface.

## Where signage helps

Better than the spoken situation suggests. Metro systems in every city a visitor is likely to reach are signed and announced bilingually. High-speed railway stations have English on departure boards and platform signs. Major museums have English labelling. Motorway and street signs carry pinyin.

The gap is conversational, not navigational.

## The ten words worth learning

你好 (nǐ hǎo, hello) · 谢谢 (xiè xie, thank you) · 多少钱 (duō shao qián, how much) · 这个 (zhè ge, this one) · 洗手间 (xǐ shǒu jiān, toilet) · 买单 (mǎi dān, the bill) · 不要辣 (bú yào là, not spicy) · 一个人 (yí ge rén, one person) · 对 (duì, correct) · 我不懂 (wǒ bù dǒng, I don't understand).

Learning these changes how you are treated more than it changes what you can accomplish, which is a reason to bother rather than not.
