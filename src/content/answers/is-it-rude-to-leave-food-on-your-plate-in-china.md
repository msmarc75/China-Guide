This is one of the etiquette questions where the old rule and current practice pull in opposite directions, and the honest answer sits between them.

## The traditional logic

Chinese hosting works on abundance. A host orders more than the table can eat, deliberately, because running out would mean they had been mean. A guest who empties every dish creates the implication that there was not enough — which is precisely the impression the host was spending money to avoid.

Leaving a little in the shared dishes therefore reads as *you gave us plenty*, which is the compliment.

## What has changed

The Clean Plate Campaign has pushed hard against food waste for over a decade, with genuine cultural traction — restaurants display reminders, and ordering far more than a table can eat now attracts mild disapproval rather than admiration.

The result is a narrower target. Leave a modest amount. Do not leave dishes barely touched.

## The practical version

Take small portions from the shared dishes rather than filling your bowl. You can always take more, and unfinished food in *your* bowl is more conspicuous than unfinished food in the communal dish.

If you genuinely cannot eat more, say so and stop. Nobody expects a guest to eat past comfort, and continued refusal is understood — expect your host to press once or twice as a matter of form, and decline warmly each time.

Taking leftovers home is completely normal and carries no stigma at all. Asking to pack up the remains of a good dish is unremarkable, including at a fairly formal meal.

:::warn The chopstick rule matters more
Never stand your chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice. It mirrors incense burned for the dead and is genuinely unsettling to see. Lay them across the bowl or on the rest.
:::

Watch your host and follow. This holds for pace, for when to start, and for how much is left at the end — and it is a more reliable guide than any rule you memorise in advance.
