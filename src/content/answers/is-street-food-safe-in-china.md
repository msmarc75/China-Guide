The reflex to avoid street food entirely costs you the best breakfast in the country and does not meaningfully reduce your risk — a busy street stall with constant turnover is frequently safer than a quiet restaurant with a fridge.

## The three rules

**Follow the queue.** Turnover is the best hygiene proxy available at a stall, though at a sit-down restaurant it is not the only instrument — [there is a graded food-safety face on a board by the door](/food/restaurant-hygiene-ratings-in-china/). A stall with ten people waiting is selling food that was raw twenty minutes ago. An empty one is not.

**Cooked to order, in front of you.** Wok, griddle, tandoor, charcoal, deep fryer — high heat applied while you watch. Jianbing is made on a hot griddle in ninety seconds. Lamb skewers come off charcoal. That is the safe category.

**Be careful with the cold and the lukewarm.** Pre-cooked trays sitting at ambient temperature, cold dressed dishes, and pre-cut unpeeled fruit are the higher-risk items anywhere in the world, and China is no exception.

## What is genuinely fine

- **Ice** in mainstream restaurants and chains, which is made from purified water
- **Cooked meat and vegetables** from a hot wok or grill
- **Steamed items** — baozi, dumplings — straight from the basket
- **Deep-fried anything**, by definition

## What to avoid

- **Tap water**, everywhere, always. Not potable anywhere in mainland China
- **Pre-cut fruit** sitting unrefrigerated
- **Cold dishes from an unbusy stall**
- **Raw fish outside a proper cold chain** — [what makes farmed salmon safe to eat raw in China is a national standard, not the price](/food/salmon-in-china/)

## The adjustment period is not food poisoning

A lot of visitors feel off in their first days and blame hygiene. Frequently it is the food itself: quantities of chilli, Sichuan peppercorn and rendered oil that a stomach has not met before. Sichuan peppercorn produces genuine physical numbness, which is disconcerting rather than dangerous.

Carry loperamide and rehydration salts, ease into the spice, and give yourself a mild first day.

## Practical hygiene

Street stalls rarely have anywhere to wash your hands, so carry sanitiser. Carry tissues too — public toilets in China frequently have neither paper nor soap.

Payment is by Alipay or WeChat QR at essentially every stall, including pushcarts. A few very small vendors use a personal-account code that foreign cards cannot pay, which is one of the few times cash genuinely helps.

## Where to eat it

Breakfast, not dinner. The best street food in China is served between 06:00 and 10:00 outside residential compounds and then packs up. Night markets are fun and increasingly aimed at tourists — the scorpions on sticks are a photo prop, not food anyone in China eats.
