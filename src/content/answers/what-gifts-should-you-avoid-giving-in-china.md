Nobody expects a foreign visitor to know these, and getting one wrong will be forgiven instantly. But they are easy to learn and the reasoning is genuinely interesting.

## The homophone taboos

- **A clock or watch.** 送钟 (*sòng zhōng*), to give a clock, sounds exactly like 送终 (*sòng zhōng*), to attend someone's deathbed or see them to the grave. This is the strongest taboo on the list and the one most likely to be given innocently by a visitor. If a clock is genuinely the right gift, the recipient can hand over a token coin so that it counts as a purchase rather than a gift.
- **Pears.** 梨 (*lí*) sounds like 离 (*lí*), to part or separate. Avoid especially with couples — and note that sharing a pear by cutting it in half is avoided for the same reason.
- **An umbrella.** 伞 (*sǎn*) sounds like 散 (*sàn*), to scatter or break up.
- **Shoes.** 鞋 (*xié*) is a near-homophone of 邪 (*xié*), evil, and the phrase 穿小鞋 (*chuān xiǎo xié*) — "to wear tight shoes" — means to be quietly persecuted by someone.
- **Anything in fours.** 四 (*sì*) sounds like 死 (*sǐ*), death. Give six or eight of a thing, never four.

## The non-pun ones

- **Knives, scissors and letter openers.** Cutting implements suggest severing the relationship.
- **A green hat.** 戴绿帽子 (*dài lǜ màozi*) — "to wear a green hat" — means a man's wife is unfaithful. Nothing green for a man's head, ever.
- **Chrysanthemums**, and white or yellow flowers generally. These are funeral flowers.
- **White or black wrapping paper.** These are mourning colours. Red and gold are the celebratory ones.

## What to give instead

Something from where you come from is close to unbeatable, because the point is that it travelled. Local specialities, regional food or drink, well-made small objects, and anything with a story attached all work. Branded items are appreciated more openly than in many cultures.

**Practicalities that matter more than the object:**

- **Offer and receive with both hands.** This applies to gifts, business cards and payment alike.
- **Expect a polite refusal, twice.** Declining before accepting is courtesy, not rejection. Offer again.
- **Do not expect it opened in front of you**, and do not open one you are given unless invited to. Opening immediately can read as valuing the object over the gesture.
- **Reciprocate at roughly the level you received.** A wildly expensive gift creates an obligation rather than pleasure.

## The one that catches business travellers

Red envelopes (红包, *hóngbāo*) of cash are normal between family, at weddings, and at New Year, and are entirely ordinary in that setting. Cash from a foreign visitor to a business contact is a different thing altogether and should not be offered.
