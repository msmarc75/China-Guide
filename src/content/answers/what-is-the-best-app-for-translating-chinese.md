The right answer is a pair of apps with different jobs, plus one piece of preparation that matters more than the choice between them.

## Pleco, for characters

**Pleco** is the dictionary. It looks up characters by handwriting, by pinyin, or by camera, and its OCR reads a menu or a sign and tells you what each character means rather than producing a guess at the whole phrase.

The decisive feature is that it works **entirely offline**. No signal, no VPN, no data plan — which matters on a train through Gansu or in a restaurant with concrete walls.

## Google Translate, for sentences

**Google Translate** handles conversation, whole sentences and the "show this to the driver" case that a dictionary cannot.

The catch is that Google is blocked in mainland China, so **the app only works if you downloaded the offline Chinese language pack before you arrived**. Do that at home, alongside your offline maps. With the pack installed, camera and text translation both work without a connection; without it, the app is a blank screen on the day you need it.

## Baidu Translate or Youdao, behind the firewall

If you are on a **Chinese SIM without a VPN**, Google will not reach the internet at all. **Baidu Translate** and **Youdao** are the domestic equivalents, both good, both with camera translation, and both entirely unaffected by the filter.

This is worth installing even if you plan to roam on a foreign SIM, because it costs nothing and covers the case where your data arrangement fails.

## The preparation that beats all three

**Screenshot your hotel name and address in Chinese characters.** An image works with a dead signal, a dying battery and a driver who does not read Latin script. It turns the hardest daily problem — getting home — into a two-second exchange.

Do the same for anywhere you must reach at a fixed time: a station, a clinic, a tour meeting point. This single habit removes more friction than any app.

## What the apps are bad at

**Menu translation produces nonsense.** Chinese dish names are frequently poetic or historical, so an app will hand you "Ants Climbing a Tree" (minced pork with glass noodles), "Lion's Head" (a pork meatball) or "Husband and Wife Lung Slices" (which contains no lung). The translation is literal and correct; the dish is not what it sounds like.

**Spoken translation struggles in noise**, which is most restaurants. Typing is more reliable than speaking.

**Medical and administrative situations** need precision an app cannot guarantee. That is an argument for an insurance assistance line rather than a better app.
