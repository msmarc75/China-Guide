China is a very safe country for travellers — violent crime against visitors is rare and walking anywhere at night is normal. The risk here is entirely financial, and it works by being pleasant rather than threatening.

## How it runs

**The approach.** One or two people, usually in their twenties, usually presenting as students or as visitors from another Chinese city. Their English is good and their opening is charming: they would like to practise, they ask where you are from, they are delighted by the answer.

**The build.** Ten or twenty minutes of genuinely enjoyable conversation. Photographs together. Recommendations. Nothing is being sold and nothing feels wrong, which is the whole design — by the time the invitation comes you are talking to someone you like.

**The invitation.** A traditional tea ceremony just around the corner; a small art exhibition their college is running; a bar a friend has opened. Always a specific venue they name, always nearby.

**The bill.** The ceremony is real, and unhurried, and pleasant. The price list, if there is one, is not shown until afterwards, and the total runs to thousands of yuan for a few small cups. Staff appear. The door is watched. Card machines materialise. The pressure is social and physical at once, and it is calibrated to be just short of anything you would call the police about.

The art-gallery variant substitutes overpriced "student" scrolls for tea. The bar variant substitutes drinks. The structure is identical.

## Where it operates

Around the highest-traffic sights, where a foreign visitor standing still with a map is easy to find:

- **Beijing** — Tiananmen, Wangfujing, the Forbidden City exits, Qianmen
- **Shanghai** — Nanjing Road and the Bund, most persistently of anywhere
- **Xi'an** — the Bell Tower and Drum Tower square
- **Guangzhou, Chengdu, Guilin** — the main pedestrian streets

## The one rule that defeats it

**Never go to a venue chosen by someone who approached you in the street.**

That is the entire defence, and it works because the scam cannot function anywhere except a venue that is in on it. You do not need to be rude, suspicious of Chinese people, or closed to conversation — talking to strangers in China is one of the pleasures of travelling there. You only need to control the destination.

If you genuinely want tea with someone you have just met, name a place yourself: a café you can see, or somewhere from a map. A real acquaintance will shrug and agree. A scammer will produce a reason why their place is better, and that reason is the tell.

Other signals worth knowing:

- Approached in English first, near a major sight
- A specific venue proposed within the first fifteen minutes
- Reluctance to be redirected to a place you choose
- No visible prices at the venue, or a list produced only at the end

## If you are already sitting down

Ask for the price list before anything is poured. If it is refused or vague, stand up and leave — the pressure is theatrical and the venue is not going to detain a foreigner who walks out. If you have already been charged, pay by card rather than cash where you can, keep the receipt, and dispute it with your bank; then report it to the police on **110** and to the tourism hotline **12301**, which operates in English.

Do not let embarrassment stop you reporting it. This is a professionally run operation and the people it catches are not fools; they are people who were treated warmly by someone good at their job.
