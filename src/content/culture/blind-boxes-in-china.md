A **盲盒** (*máng hé*, blind box) is a sealed box holding one figure from a known series. You are told the series; you are not told which figure. Somewhere in the run there is a 隐藏款 (*yǐncáng kuǎn*), a hidden edition, at long odds.

The regulator itself names the three channels the trade runs on. Article 6 of the guidelines below addresses anyone selling blind boxes 通过互联网、实体店、自助销售设备 — **over the internet, in physical shops, and through self-service vending equipment** — and requires each of them to display the business licence and the operator's identifying details in a conspicuous position. That is the same duty [the licence on any Chinese shop wall carries](/guides/business-licences-in-china/), extended to a machine. One Chinese brand in the category, **泡泡玛特** (*Pàopào Mǎtè*), trades internationally under the English name **POP MART**.

[This site's shopping guide](/guides/shopping-in-china/) is about tea, silk, porcelain and seals. This is the other half of Chinese retail, and it comes with a rulebook.

## The instrument, and what kind of instrument it is

The national text is the **盲盒经营行为规范指引（试行）** (*máng hé jīngyíng xíngwéi guīfàn zhǐyǐn*, Guidelines for Regulating Blind Box Business Conduct, trial), issued by the **State Administration for Market Regulation** as **国市监稽发〔2023〕39号**, dated **8 June 2023** and published on **15 June 2023**. It runs to twenty-nine articles.

:::note A guideline is not a rule, and the difference shows
Read the verbs. Some articles say 应当 (*yīngdāng*), *shall* — those restate duties that already exist in the Civil Code, the Consumer Rights Protection Law and the pricing rules. Others say 鼓励 (*gǔlì*), *encouraged* — and those are wishes.

Article 11 is the clearest case. It **encourages** operators to build a 保底机制 (*bǎodǐ jīzhì*), a floor mechanism, by setting a draw time, a spending ceiling and a limit on the number of draws; and it **encourages** them to promise not to hoard, not to hype and not to feed stock straight into the resale market. Encouraged. Not required.

So when a shop tells you something is "the rule", the question worth asking is which kind. [The same ladder decides whether a Chinese standard is mandatory or advisory](/food/salmon-in-china/), and it is worth carrying into any Chinese shop.
:::

## Article 9: what has to be on display before you pay

This is the article that changes a blind-box wall from a lottery into a purchase you can assess. It requires the operator to publish, **conspicuously**, a specific list:

> 盲盒经营者应当将商品名称、商品种类、商品样式、抽取规则、商品分布、限量商品投放数量、抽取概率、商品价值范围等关键信息以显著方式对外公示。

Item by item: the **product name**, the **product category**, the **styles**, the **draw rules**, the **distribution of goods**, the **quantity of any limited-edition item released**, the **draw probability**, and the **range of product values**.

The same article forbids the two things that would make all of it meaningless: setting **empty boxes**, and manipulating the outcome or adjusting the probability from the back end.

And Article 10 puts a paper trail behind it. Operators must keep their probability settings; where the draw happens live online, they must keep a **complete record** of the results and run a tracking system so that what you drew actually reaches you. The records are to be held for **not less than three years**.

:::key What to look for on the wall
- The **series name** and how many figures are in it
- The **odds on the hidden edition** — this is the number people buy for
- **How many** of any limited item were made
- The **value range** of what is inside
- A **price** on the box, [displayed the way every Chinese price has to be](/guides/marked-prices-in-china/)

If the odds are not posted anywhere, that is the thing Article 9 asks for and it is missing.
:::

## The return rule reverses itself, and only here

The general position in Chinese consumer law is that opening the box does not cost you the seven-day no-reason return. [The State Council settled that in 2024](/answers/can-i-return-something-i-bought-in-china/): a consumer who unseals goods to inspect them, without affecting their original quality, function and appearance, is still entitled to return them. The law's own closed list of exceptions does contain one unsealing case — audiovisual products and software — but that is a fixed category set by statute.

The blind box is not in that list. It gets there by a different route, and one the seller has to actively take.

Article 17 sets it out in two sentences that face opposite directions:

:::warn Unsealing forfeits it — unless you bought the whole set
> 盲盒经营者通过充分告知提示，并经消费者单次购买时确认后，以互联网形式销售的盲盒商品拆封后可以不适用七日无理由退货。

Where the operator has **given full notice** and the consumer has **confirmed at the moment of that single purchase**, a blind box sold online **may be taken outside** the seven-day no-reason return once it is unsealed.

Now the second sentence:

> 以全包形式销售整套系列商品，该系列内商品清楚确定的，经营者应依法执行网购七日无理由退货规定。

Where a **complete set** is sold as a whole and the items in that series are **clearly determined**, the operator **shall** apply the seven-day online return rule as normal.

Read the pair together and the logic is exact. The right is removed by the **randomness**, not by the box. Buy the whole series and there is nothing random left, so the ordinary rule comes back.
:::

Two practical consequences follow. The exclusion is not automatic — it depends on the seller actually having told you and on your having confirmed it, at that purchase. And it is written for **online** sales; a box handed to you across a counter was never inside the seven-day right in the first place, because [that right only covers distance selling](/answers/can-i-return-something-i-bought-in-china/).

## The ¥200 figure that is not a national rule

You will see it quoted as though it were: a blind box may not cost more than ¥200. It is real, and it is **Shanghai's**.

**《上海市盲盒经营活动合规指引》** (Shanghai Blind Box Business Compliance Guidelines), issued by the **Shanghai Municipal Administration for Market Regulation** as **沪市监竞争〔2022〕14号**, printed on **10 January 2022** and released on **14 January 2022** — seventeen months *before* the national text — says at its Article 8:

> 单个盲盒商品的售价一般不高于人民币200元。盲盒商品实际价值应与其售卖价格基本相当。

The selling price of a single blind box **generally should not exceed ¥200**, and the actual value of what is inside should be **broadly equivalent** to what you paid.

The national guideline does not carry that cap. What it does instead, at Article 8, is set two comparisons:

> 同一套系商品或者服务的成本差距不应过大。盲盒商品价格不应与相同非盲盒销售商品价格差距过大。

The cost gap **within one series** should not be too large, and the blind-box price should not differ too much from the price of **the same item sold openly**. Vaguer than a number, and aimed at the same thing.

:::note A city figure travelling as a national one
This is worth noticing beyond blind boxes. Chinese regulation is often piloted by one municipality and then generalised, and the pilot's specific numbers do not always survive the trip. Shanghai wrote a price ceiling; the national text kept the principle and dropped the figure. Anyone quoting ¥200 outside Shanghai is quoting a municipal guideline.
:::

## Children: a hard floor and a consent step

Article 23 is the one clause here with no softness in it at all:

> 盲盒经营者不得向未满8周岁未成年人销售盲盒。向8周岁及以上未成年人销售盲盒商品，应当依法确认已取得相关监护人的同意。

Operators **may not sell a blind box to a minor under 8 years of age**. For minors of **8 and over**, the operator must confirm that the relevant guardian's consent has been obtained. Shanghai got there first here too, with the same age floor in its Article 18.

For a family, the practical reading is simple: the purchase is the adult's, at every age below eighteen. [Travelling in China with children](/plan/china-with-children/) has enough age and height thresholds in it already; this is one more.

## What may not be sold this way at all

Article 5 draws a boundary around the whole model. Blind-box selling is to stay in the territory of everyday life and entertainment; anything needing a licence still needs the licence; anything the law bars from sale is barred here too. Then it names categories:

- **Medicines, medical devices, toxic or hazardous substances, flammables and explosives, and live animals** — goods with strict requirements on conditions of use, storage, transport, inspection or quarantine — **may not** be sold as blind boxes.
- **Food and cosmetics** should not be, where the conditions to guarantee quality, safety and consumer rights are absent.
- And one that names a specific trade:

> 无法投递又无法退回的快件，不得以盲盒形式销售。

**Express parcels that can be neither delivered nor returned may not be sold as blind boxes.** That is the "unclaimed parcel" mystery-box trade, written out of the category by name.

Article 21 closes the other door: blind-box goods may not carry content the law forbids, and an operator **may not conduct gambling, or disguised gambling, under the name of a blind box**. There is a lawful way to sell a chance in China and it is a narrow one — [two state-licensed lotteries, and nothing else](/culture/lotteries-in-china/).

## In practice

- **Look for the odds before you look at the figures.** Article 9 makes the probability a disclosure, not a secret. A wall with no posted odds is a wall that has skipped the main obligation.
- **Assume an opened box bought online is yours to keep.** The exclusion is available to the seller and most will have taken it. If it matters, the whole-set purchase is the version that keeps the return right.
- **In a shop, the seven days were never there.** Buying in person puts you outside the distance-selling regime entirely, which is [the distinction that decides most Chinese return questions](/answers/can-i-return-something-i-bought-in-china/).
- **The price on the box is a marked price.** Nothing may be added at the till, and that is a legal duty rather than a shop policy.
- **The ¥200 ceiling is Shanghai's.** Elsewhere the test is whether the price is out of line with the same item sold openly.
- **Children under 8 cannot buy one**, and 8 to 17 needs a guardian's agreement.
- **The resale market is outside all of this.** Article 11 only *encourages* operators to stay out of it; what happens between collectors afterwards is not what this guideline governs.
- **Hong Kong and Macau regulate consumer trade under their own law**, so neither the national guideline nor Shanghai's applies to a purchase made there.

## Frequently asked questions

### What information must a Chinese blind box shop display?

Article 9 of the 2023 national guidelines requires conspicuous publication of the product name, the category, the styles, the draw rules, the distribution of goods, the released quantity of any limited item, the draw probability and the range of product values. The same article forbids empty boxes and any back-end adjustment of the odds.

### Can I return a blind box in China?

Rarely, if you bought it online and opened it. Article 17 allows the seller to exclude an unsealed blind box from the seven-day no-reason return, provided it gave full notice and you confirmed at that purchase. Buying the complete set is different: where the whole series is clearly determined, the seven-day rule applies as normal. Bought in a physical shop, the seven-day right never applied at all.

### Is there a price limit on blind boxes in China?

Not nationally. The ¥200 ceiling widely quoted comes from Shanghai's municipal compliance guidelines of January 2022, which say a single blind box generally should not exceed ¥200 and that the contents should be broadly worth the price. The national guidelines instead say the price should not differ too much from the same item sold openly.

### Can children buy blind boxes in China?

Not under the age of 8 — operators are barred from selling to them. From 8 to 17, the operator must confirm that the guardian has consented. Shanghai set the same age floor in 2022 and the national guidelines adopted it in 2023.

### Are blind boxes considered gambling in China?

No, and the guidelines are explicit that they must not become it: an operator may not conduct gambling or disguised gambling under the name of a blind box. The disclosure and record-keeping articles exist to keep the draw a purchase with known odds rather than a wager.

### What cannot be sold as a blind box in China?

Medicines, medical devices, toxic or hazardous substances, flammables and explosives, and live animals are barred outright. Food and cosmetics should not be sold this way without the conditions to guarantee safety. Express parcels that can be neither delivered nor returned are named and excluded, which rules out the unclaimed-parcel mystery box.
