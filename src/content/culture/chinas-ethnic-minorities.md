Travel in Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi, Xinjiang, Ningxia, Inner Mongolia or the Tibetan areas and you will meet a China that does not match the one in most guidebooks — different languages, different food, different architecture, different religions. This page explains the framework behind that, and how to be a decent visitor inside it.

## Where the number 56 comes from

The People's Republic recognises **56 nationalities (民族)**: the Han majority and 55 minorities. The figure is everywhere in Chinese public life, on banknotes and in school textbooks, and it is usually presented as simply a fact about the country.

It is more interesting than that. When the first national census was taken in 1953, people returned **more than four hundred** different ethnic names for themselves. The state then ran a decades-long **ethnic identification project (民族识别)**, sending linguists and ethnographers to determine which of those constituted distinct nationalities. Thirty-eight were recognised in the first phase to 1954, fifteen more by 1964, and the process continued until the Jino of Yunnan were recognised in **1979** — the last addition, and the point at which the list closed at 56.

So the categories are administrative decisions taken within living memory, not descriptions that were simply found. Groups were merged where officials judged them variants of one another, and boundaries were drawn that people on the ground did not always recognise. Scholars inside and outside China have written extensively about this; you do not need the debate to travel, but it explains why a "single" nationality can contain communities whose languages are mutually unintelligible and whose dress, architecture and customs differ completely from one valley to the next.

At the 2020 census, minorities were **around 8.9 per cent** of the population — a small share of a very large number, and growing faster than the Han population. The **Zhuang**, mostly in Guangxi, are the largest group; the Uyghur, Hui, Miao and Manchu each also exceed ten million.

## What "autonomous" means on the ground

The [state pillar](/culture/how-china-works/) covers the administrative hierarchy. The ethnic layer sits inside it at three levels: **five autonomous regions** at province level (Inner Mongolia, Guangxi, Tibet, Ningxia, Xinjiang), about **thirty autonomous prefectures**, and around **a hundred and twenty autonomous counties** — roughly a hundred and fifty-five such areas in total, covering something like 64 per cent of China's land area. Forty-four of the 55 minorities have an autonomous area somewhere.

What a traveller actually notices is narrower than the constitutional language suggests: bilingual signage, sometimes a bilingual announcement on the metro, local holidays, and in some places religious infrastructure that is absent elsewhere. Autonomy in the administrative sense does not mean the region operates under different everyday rules for a visitor.

## Where you actually encounter this

**Yunnan** is the most ethnically diverse province and the one most travellers meet first — Bai around Dali, Naxi around Lijiang, Dai in Xishuangbanna near the Laos border, Hani on the Yuanyang terraces, and many more. The Bai are also why [Yunnan is the one part of China with a cheese tradition](/answers/is-dairy-used-in-chinese-cooking/). See the [Yunnan guide](/destinations/yunnan/).

**Guizhou** has large Miao and Dong populations and far fewer foreign visitors than Yunnan, which is much of its appeal.

**Guangxi** is the Zhuang autonomous region, though the Zhuang are sufficiently integrated into surrounding Han society that visitors to Guilin and Yangshuo often do not register that they are in a minority region at all.

**Ningxia and the northwest** are Hui heartland — Chinese-speaking Muslims whose visible difference is religious rather than linguistic. The halal cooking described in the [food pillar](/food/beyond-the-eight-cuisines/) is theirs, and it reaches every large Chinese city. See [Yinchuan](/destinations/yinchuan/).

**Xinjiang** is Uyghur, Kazakh and several other Turkic-speaking populations, with a food culture and built environment closer to Central Asia than to eastern China. The Tajik towns along [the Karakoram Highway](/answers/do-i-need-a-permit-for-the-karakoram-highway/) are a different world again, and need a border permit.

**Tibetan areas** extend well beyond the Tibet Autonomous Region — into Qinghai, western Sichuan and northwest Yunnan — and those provincial Tibetan areas are reachable without the permit that Tibet itself requires.

**Inner Mongolia and the northeast** have Mongol and Manchu populations, though Manchu language use has declined to near-extinction and the visible distinctiveness is much reduced.

:::warn Tibet requires a permit and an organised tour
Foreign passport holders cannot travel independently in the Tibet Autonomous Region. You need a **Tibet Travel Permit**, [arranged through a travel agency](/answers/how-do-i-get-a-tibet-travel-permit/) rather than applied for directly, and you must be on an organised tour; the permit is checked before you board the train or flight. Border areas such as Everest and Ngari need a further permit on top.

Requirements and processing times change, and the agencies that publish them have a commercial interest, so treat any timeline you read — including this one — as a starting point and confirm with an operator well before booking flights. [Tibetan areas in Qinghai, Sichuan and Yunnan](/answers/can-i-visit-tibetan-areas-without-a-tibet-permit/) are not covered by this and need no permit.
:::

## Staged culture and living culture

This is the practical distinction that determines whether a visit is worth making, and it is not subtle once you know to look.

**Staged**: a gated site with an admission fee, scheduled performances several times daily, staff in costume who change into ordinary clothes at the end of a shift, a fixed route, and a souvenir hall at the exit. These exist across the whole country and are honest enough on their own terms — a job is a job, and some of the performers are genuinely skilled. But they are theatre, and reviewing them as a window into how people live is a category error.

**Living**: a village where people are doing their own work, where you are a visitor rather than an audience, where there is no performance schedule, and where much of what is interesting is not arranged for you at all. The tell is usually whether anything would still be happening if you were not there.

Both can be worth your time. Confusing the first for the second is what produces the disappointed traveller who concludes a whole culture is fake.

:::note Festivals are the exception worth planning around
Real festivals — the Dai water-splashing festival in April, Tibetan and Mongol seasonal gatherings, Miao festivals across Guizhou — are events communities hold for themselves, which outsiders may attend. They are not scheduled for tourism, and dates often follow lunar or local calendars rather than fixed ones.

Check dates locally and well ahead, expect accommodation to be full, and remember you are a guest at something that is not about you.
:::

## Photographing people

The single most common way visitors get this wrong is the camera, so it is worth a real rule rather than a platitude.

**Ask first, and accept a no.** A gesture towards the camera and a questioning look works without shared language. If someone declines, that is the end of it — not an invitation to take the picture from further away.

Beyond that:

- **Do not photograph inside homes, or during religious activity, without being invited.** This applies in mosques and Tibetan temples as much as anywhere.
- **Children need a parent's agreement**, not the child's.
- **Where photographs are transactional**, someone in elaborate dress in a tourist area may expect payment. That is a fair arrangement, but it is a transaction rather than an encounter, and it is worth knowing which one you are having.
- **Elderly people in rural areas are frequently the most photographed and least consulted.** If you would not do it at home, the fact that you are travelling does not change the calculation.

The underlying test is simple: a person is not scenery. If your photograph requires someone to be an object in a landscape rather than a person you have dealt with, take a different photograph.

## Buying things

Buy from the person who made it where you can. In craft-producing areas — Miao silver, Dong and Miao textiles, Uyghur instruments and knives, Tibetan carpets — the difference between a workshop and a souvenir counter is usually visible, and the maker will tell you about the object if you ask.

Two practical notes. First, a great deal of what is sold as handmade minority craft is factory-produced, sometimes elsewhere entirely; this is not unique to China. Second, antiques face genuine export restrictions, so anything genuinely old needs care — the [shopping guide](/guides/shopping-in-china/) covers that.

## Language

Many minority languages are not related to Chinese at all. Uyghur and Kazakh are Turkic, Tibetan belongs to a different branch of Sino-Tibetan, Mongolian is Mongolic, and the Dai languages are related to Thai. Others, including the Hui, speak Chinese as a first language.

For a visitor this mostly means signage is often bilingual, and that Mandarin is the shared language almost everywhere — younger people generally speak it fluently, older rural people sometimes less so. Learning a greeting in the local language is disproportionately well received, and the [Mandarin phrases guide](/culture/mandarin-phrases-for-travellers/) will not help you with it.

## Subjects to be careful with

Xinjiang and Tibet are politically sensitive in China, and both are subjects on which international and Chinese accounts diverge sharply.

The practical position for a traveller is the same as the [etiquette guide](/culture/chinese-etiquette/) sets out for sensitive topics generally: the risk to you as a tourist is negligible, and the risk to the Chinese person you are talking with — particularly in public, or on Chinese platforms — is not. If someone raises a subject, listen. Do not press it, and do not put a stranger in the position of answering.

That is a rule about other people's exposure, not about what you are permitted to think.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many ethnic groups are there in China?

The People's Republic officially recognises 56 nationalities: the Han majority and 55 minorities. The 1953 census returned more than four hundred self-reported ethnic names, which a state identification project consolidated over the following decades. The list reached its final form in 1979.

### Which is the largest ethnic minority in China?

The Zhuang, concentrated in Guangxi, with a population above nineteen million at the 2020 census. The Uyghur, Hui, Miao and Manchu each also number more than ten million. Minorities together were around 8.9 per cent of the population in 2020.

### What does an autonomous region mean in China?

It is a province-level administrative unit where a designated minority is recognised in the region's structure — Inner Mongolia, Guangxi, Tibet, Ningxia and Xinjiang. There are also autonomous prefectures and counties at lower levels. For a visitor the visible effects are mostly bilingual signage and local holidays.

### Can I visit Tibetan areas without a Tibet permit?

Yes — the permit requirement applies to the Tibet Autonomous Region only. Substantial Tibetan areas lie outside it, in Qinghai, western Sichuan and northwest Yunnan, and those need no permit and no organised tour. The [visa guide](/guides/china-visa-guide/) covers the permit itself.

### Is it okay to photograph people in minority villages?

Ask first and accept a refusal. Do not photograph inside homes or during religious activity uninvited, get a parent's agreement for children, and be aware that some photographs in tourist areas are paid transactions. A person is not scenery.

### Where is the best place to see minority cultures in China?

Yunnan is the most diverse and the most accessible, Guizhou has large Miao and Dong populations with far fewer visitors, and the northwest is Hui and Uyghur country. What matters more than the destination is distinguishing a ticketed performance site from a place where people actually live.
