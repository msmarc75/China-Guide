After a week in China most visitors have seen a dozen temple halls and can no longer tell them apart. The problem is not the buildings; it is that Chinese architecture communicates through a system nobody explains at the gate.

Once you know what the system encodes, the buildings stop being interchangeable. A Chinese visitor reads a roof the way a European reads a coat of arms.

This page gives you the apparatus. The [religion pillar](/culture/religion-and-philosophy-in-china/) covers who is inside a temple and what the statues mean; this one covers the building itself, the garden around it, and the art on the walls.

## Four things to check on any building

**1. Count the little animals on the roof ridge.** At the lower end of each sloping ridge you will see a line of small figures, led by a man riding a bird. The count is not decorative whim — [it signals rank](/answers/what-do-the-small-animals-on-chinese-roofs-mean/). The numbers run in odds: one, three, five, seven, nine, with more meaning more important.

Nine is the maximum. There is exactly one building in China with ten, and it is the Hall of Supreme Harmony in the Forbidden City. Within the palace the gradient is legible: the Palace of Heavenly Purity has nine, the Palace of Earthly Tranquility seven, the consorts' palaces five, minor side halls one. Learn to count them and you can rank any imperial or temple building at a glance.

**2. Look at the roof's shape.** Roof forms are a formal hierarchy, and the Qing codified who could use which — commoners and lower-ranking officials were restricted to the simplest kinds.

| Roof form | Chinese | Where it sits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Double-eaved hip | 重檐庑殿顶 | The highest; main halls of palaces and great temples |
| Double-eaved gable-and-hip | 重檐歇山顶 | Second rank |
| Single-eaved hip | 单檐庑殿顶 | High status |
| Single-eaved gable-and-hip | 单檐歇山顶 | Common on important halls |
| Overhanging gable | 悬山顶 | Ordinary buildings |
| Flush gable | 硬山顶 | The plainest; houses and service buildings |

The distinction worth learning first is hip versus gable-and-hip: a hip roof (庑殿顶) slopes on all four sides in unbroken planes, while a gable-and-hip roof (歇山顶) has a small vertical gable panel high on each end. Once you see it you cannot unsee it.

**3. Measure the bracket set against the column.** The **bracket set (斗拱)** is the cluster of interlocking wooden blocks and arms where a column meets the roof. It carries the eaves outward and transfers their load down.

This is the best dating cue available to a non-specialist, because bracket sets shrank steadily. In Tang buildings they are massive — commonly cited as around half the height of the column below. By the Song they are roughly a third to a quarter. By the Ming and Qing they are perhaps a fifth or less, packed in rows, and doing far more decorative than structural work. Liang Sicheng, the architectural historian who established the field, divided the tradition into a vigorous early period, a mellow middle one and a rigid late one, largely on this evidence.

So: big, sparse, obviously load-bearing brackets suggest something early. Small, dense, fussy ones suggest late.

**4. Count the bays.** A hall's width is measured in **bays (间)** — the spaces between columns, not the columns themselves. Bay counts are odd, so the building has a central axis, and more bays mean more importance. Nine is imperial; eleven, at the Hall of Supreme Harmony, is exceptional.

:::note Almost nothing you see is as old as it looks
Only four timber buildings generally counted as Tang survive, all in Shanxi, and the dating of some is debated. The oldest is the main hall of **Nanchan Temple (南禅寺)**, dated 782 — seventy-five years earlier than the more famous east hall of **Foguang Temple (佛光寺)** of 857, which English sources sometimes wrongly call the oldest.

Everything else is later, rebuilt, or reconstructed, often several times. That is not a scandal; timber burns and China is large and old. But it means a "Tang temple" is almost always a Tang foundation with much later buildings on it. The [economy pillar](/culture/chinese-economy-for-visitors/) covers why so much of what looks historic is recent, and the [Kaifeng](/destinations/kaifeng/) and [Wuhan](/destinations/wuhan/) guides say so about specific famous sites.

The eye test above is not the only one available. Protected sites carry a plaque stating their grade and the year they were listed, and [reading it takes ten seconds and settles what the brackets can only suggest](/culture/chinese-heritage-protection/).
:::

## Why everything is arranged the way it is

Chinese monumental building is not about the single object. It is about a **sequence along an axis**.

You enter at the south. You pass through a gate, into a courtyard, through another gate, into another courtyard, and the halls get progressively more important as you go north. Side buildings are subordinate by position, not just by size. The Forbidden City is this pattern at maximum scale; a small county temple is the same pattern with three courtyards instead of a dozen.

The consequence for a visitor is that **the experience is the walk, not the view**. There is no facade designed to be photographed from across a plaza, because the building was never meant to be seen that way. If a Chinese palace complex feels anticlimactic in photographs, that is why.

A temple hall and a palace hall are built the same way, from the same kit of parts, at ranks set by the same rules. What differs is what is inside and what the courtyards are for.

## Gardens are built to be walked through

The classical gardens of Suzhou operate on the opposite principle to the axial palace: no straight line, no full view, nothing revealed at once.

The devices have names and are worth knowing because you will then spot them constantly:

- **Borrowed scenery (借景)** — incorporating something outside the garden into the composition, so a distant pagoda appears to belong to a garden that does not own it. The Ming garden manual *Yuanye* by Ji Cheng treats this as the central skill of the art.
- **Framed views (框景)** — a doorway or window shaped to compose a specific scene, so a white wall becomes paper and the opening becomes the frame.
- **Lattice windows (漏窗)** — pierced screens that leak a partial view of what is beyond, encouraging you to move rather than stop.
- **Blocked views (障景)** — a rock or screen wall placed to prevent you seeing everything on entry.

Walk a Suzhou garden slowly and stop at every opening, and it works. March through it and it will seem like a small cluttered park. See the [Suzhou guide](/destinations/suzhou/) for which gardens repay this most.

## Looking at a scroll painting

Chinese painting asks for a different habit than European painting. Three things help.

**Scrolls are read, not viewed.** A handscroll was designed to be unrolled a section at a time, right to left, at arm's width — a sequence in time, closer to reading than to standing in front of a canvas. Museums display them fully unrolled, which is convenient and misrepresents them completely. [Move along it from right to left](/answers/how-should-i-look-at-a-chinese-handscroll/) rather than taking it in at once, and read the red seals as a chain of owners rather than as decoration.

**Landscape is not scenery.** In the monumental landscapes of the Northern Song the mountain dominates and the human figures are tiny, often needing a search to find. That relation is the subject, and finding the traveller on the path is part of looking.

**Style broadly tracks the period,** in the way art historians generally characterise it. Song landscape tends towards monumental scale, ink over colour, and atmospheric depth. Yuan painting shifts towards the scholar-amateur, where visible brushwork carries the artist's personality and the picture is closer to handwriting. Ming and Qing painting is more varied, more colourful, more self-consciously in dialogue with earlier masters, and splits between professional court painters and literati.

Treat those as tendencies rather than tests — attributions and datings are contested constantly, which is part of why the field is interesting.

## Calligraphy, without reading Chinese

Calligraphy sits at the top of the traditional hierarchy of arts, above painting. Visitors who cannot read Chinese usually skip it, which is a shame, because much of what is being judged is not the meaning.

What connoisseurs look at is the movement: the speed of the brush, whether the ink is wet or dry and how it changes across a stroke, the rhythm and spacing, the balance of a character as a shape. You can see all of that without knowing a single character.

It helps to know the five broad script types, since a gallery will mix them: **seal script (篆书)**, ancient and used now for seals; **clerical (隶书)**, broad and flat; **regular (楷书)**, the clear standard form; **running (行书)**, fluent and semi-joined; and **cursive (草书)**, so abbreviated that many Chinese readers cannot read it either. If you cannot read a piece and a Chinese visitor beside you also cannot, it is probably cursive, and that is not a failure of either of you.

Film is the other way into this material: several of the best-known Chinese films were shot in buildings you can visit, which [Chinese literature and film for travellers](/culture/chinese-literature-and-film/) maps out.

## In a provincial museum

Provincial museums are free, consistently excellent and under-visited by foreigners. Two categories reward attention.

**Ritual bronzes** from the Shang and Zhou are the objects Chinese museums are proudest of, and they are genuinely astonishing as casting. Look for the stylised animal-mask motif that covers many surfaces, and look for inscriptions — often cast inside the vessel, invisible in a case, and reproduced on the label. The inscriptions are why these objects matter historically as well as aesthetically.

The same free-and-under-visited logic extends past the museums. [A Chinese municipal library is free by law, open far longer, and often the most ambitious modern building in the city](/culture/public-libraries-in-china/).

**Ceramics** offer the clearest visual chronology in Chinese art. Song taste ran to monochrome glazes and restrained shapes. Blue-and-white as most people picture it emerged with Yuan-dynasty production using imported cobalt. Ming and Qing porcelain becomes more colourful, more technically elaborate and more export-driven. Walk a ceramics gallery in order and the sequence is unmistakable even without labels.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do the small animals on Chinese roofs mean?

They indicate the building's rank. The figures run along the lower end of each sloping roof ridge, led by an immortal riding a bird, and are counted in odd numbers — the more there are, the higher the status. Nine is the standard maximum, and the Hall of Supreme Harmony in the Forbidden City uniquely has ten.

### What is a dougong bracket set?

A bracket set (斗拱) is the cluster of interlocking wooden blocks and arms between a column and the roof, carrying the eaves outward and transferring their weight down. Their size relative to the column is the most useful dating cue available: very large in Tang buildings, smaller through the Song, and small and largely decorative by the Ming and Qing.

### What is the oldest wooden building in China?

The main hall of Nanchan Temple in Shanxi, dated 782. The east hall of Foguang Temple, also in Shanxi, dates from 857 and is sometimes incorrectly described as the oldest. Only about four timber buildings are generally counted as Tang survivals, and some datings are debated.

### How should I look at a Chinese handscroll?

Move along it from right to left, a section at a time, rather than taking it in at once. Handscrolls were designed to be unrolled progressively at arm's width, so a museum display showing the whole thing at once is convenient but misrepresents how the painting was meant to work.

### Can I appreciate Chinese calligraphy without reading Chinese?

Yes. Much of what is being judged is the movement of the brush — speed, wetness of ink, rhythm, spacing and the balance of each character as a shape — all of which is visible without reading. Cursive script is frequently illegible to Chinese readers too.

### Why do Chinese palaces and temples all look similar?

Because they are built from a shared kit of parts, at ranks set by shared rules, and arranged as a sequence of courtyards along a north–south axis. The variation is in rank markers — roof form, bay count, roof-ridge figures — rather than in overall form, which is exactly what the system was designed to communicate.
