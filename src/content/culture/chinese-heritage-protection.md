"Is this actually old?" is the question that follows visitors around China, and this site answers it constantly — Mutianyu is restored, Jiankou is not, Datong's old city is a modern rebuild, Kaifeng is reconstruction over a buried Song city.

Two other pages give you two ways to judge. [Chinese art and architecture](/culture/chinese-art-and-architecture/) teaches the eye test: bracket sets, roof beasts, bay counts. [The economy pillar](/culture/chinese-economy-for-visitors/) gives the material reason so little survives. This page gives the third answer, which is administrative and which is printed on a stone at the entrance.

## The designation, and its three grades

China protects immovable heritage by designating individual sites as **文物保护单位** (*wénwù bǎohù dānwèi*, cultural relic protection units). The system runs on three tiers, set out in Article 23 of the **中华人民共和国文物保护法** (*Zhōnghuá Rénmín Gònghéguó Wénwù Bǎohùfǎ*, Cultural Relics Protection Law of the People's Republic of China), comprehensively revised on **8 November 2024** and in force since **1 March 2025**:

- **全国重点文物保护单位** (*quánguó zhòngdiǎn wénwù bǎohù dānwèi*) — major national sites, approved and published by the State Council, selected from the sites already listed at lower levels.
- **省级文物保护单位** (*shěngjí wénwù bǎohù dānwèi*) — provincial, approved and published by a provincial, autonomous-region or municipality government.
- **市县级文物保护单位** (*shìxiànjí wénwù bǎohù dānwèi*) — city or county level, approved and published by the relevant city or county government.

The grade is not a quality rating in the tourist sense. It is a statement about significance and about who is legally on the hook for the site. It is also, usefully, a rough proxy for how carefully the thing in front of you has been treated.

## The plaque at the gate

This is the part worth learning, because it takes ten seconds and it is at almost every site you will visit.

Within a year of a site being designated, the responsible government must demarcate its protection scope, erect a marker, establish a record archive and assign a body or named person to manage it. The marker — the **标志说明** (*biāozhì shuōmíng*, marker and explanatory sign) — is the stone or metal plaque you walk past, and the implementing regulations for the law, State Council Order No. 377, in force since 1 July 2003, specify what it must say:

> 文物保护单位的级别、名称、公布机关、公布日期、立标机关、立标日期等内容

The **grade** of the protection unit, its **name**, the **authority that announced it**, the **date of that announcement**, the **authority that erected the marker**, and the **date the marker went up**.

So the plaque tells you, without any Chinese beyond the numerals: how important the state considers this, and *when it entered the system*. In ethnic autonomous areas the same regulations require the marker to be written in both standard Chinese characters and the local minority script, which is why plaques in [Xinjiang](/destinations/xinjiang-kashgar/) and Tibet carry two scripts.

:::tip The date is the useful number
A listing date long before the site's tourist development usually means the fabric was protected first and presented later. A listing date that arrives after a visible rebuild tells a different story. Neither is disqualifying, but the plaque is the only place that sequence is written down.
:::

The **保护范围** (*bǎohù fànwéi*, protection scope) named on the plaque is a defined area covering the relic itself and a margin around it, sized to the site's type, scale and surroundings. It is the reason a protected building often sits inside a fenced envelope larger than the building.

## What "restored" is allowed to mean

Article 32 of the revised law sets the standard for work on a protected site: repairs must follow the principle of **不改变文物原状** (*bù gǎibiàn wénwù yuánzhuàng*, not altering the original state of the relic), together with **最小干预** (*zuìxiǎo gānyù*, minimum intervention), so that the authenticity and integrity of the relic are preserved.

That is a meaningfully strict test, and it is what separates two things this site describes with the same English word. A restored **protected unit** has been repaired under a rule that forbids changing what it was. A restored **old street** with no designation has been rebuilt to look the part, and no such rule applied.

## The rule that explains the reconstructions

Article 33 is the one that answers the question underneath all the others:

> 不可移动文物已经全部毁坏的，应当严格实施遗址保护，不得在原址重建。

Where an immovable relic **has been completely destroyed**, strict ruins protection must be applied and **it may not be rebuilt on its original site.** Rebuilding on the site for special reasons of heritage protection requires provincial government approval; for a national-key unit, it requires State Council approval with the agreement of the national heritage authority.

Read what follows from that. A full-scale "ancient" complex standing where a destroyed monument used to be is, in the ordinary case, **not a protected relic at all** — because the protection system would not have permitted it. It is a civic or commercial reconstruction, operating outside the heritage regime rather than under it.

That is the legal shape of a pattern this site already describes: [Kaifeng and the Yellow Crane Tower](/culture/chinese-economy-for-visitors/) are not failed heritage projects, they are a different category of thing. Both are worth visiting. Neither is what the word "ancient" on the signage implies.

## What to do with this

1. **Look for the plaque before the ticket gate.** It is usually on the boundary wall or beside the entrance.
2. **Read the grade and the date.** Two pieces of information, no Chinese required beyond recognising the four characters 全国重点 (*quánguó zhòngdiǎn*, national key) that mark the top tier.
3. **No plaque at all is itself informative.** A large, impressive, entirely unlisted "old town" is telling you what it is.
4. **Do not confuse this with the A-grade.** Chinese sites frequently carry two plaques, and they measure completely different things — [the A-grade rates facilities, management and scale](/guides/chinese-scenic-areas/), and predicts crowds; the protection grade rates historical significance, and predicts authenticity. A 5A site can be a reconstruction; a 1A-or-unrated site can be a national-key relic.
5. **Adjust the expectation, not the itinerary.** A reconstruction visited knowingly is a good day out. The disappointment comes from the mismatch, not the building.

## Frequently asked questions

### How can I tell if a Chinese historic site is original?

Look for the stone plaque at the entrance. Protected sites are designated as cultural relic protection units at national, provincial or city and county level, and the marker must state the grade, the name, the authority that listed it and the date. A site with no such designation has not been through the protection system, whatever its signage says.

### What does 全国重点文物保护单位 mean?

Major site protected at national level — the top of China's three-tier heritage designation, approved and published by the State Council from among sites already listed provincially or locally. It is the strongest signal available that the fabric in front of you is significant and has been treated under the protection rules.

### Why are so many Chinese historic buildings reconstructions?

Partly because timber burns and the country is old, which [the economy pillar](/culture/chinese-economy-for-visitors/) covers. Administratively, though, a completely destroyed relic may not legally be rebuilt on its own site except with high-level approval — so most full-scale "ancient" rebuilds are civic or commercial projects standing outside the heritage system, not protected relics.

### Is a restored Chinese temple still authentic?

If it is a designated protection unit, the work was governed by a rule requiring that repairs not alter the relic's original state and follow minimum intervention, preserving its authenticity and integrity. If it carries no designation, no such standard applied, and "restored" may mean rebuilt from scratch in an old style.

### What is the difference between a 5A rating and a heritage listing in China?

They measure different things and often appear on the same site. The A-grade assesses facilities, transport, services and management, and is [the better predictor of how busy and how developed a place will be](/guides/chinese-scenic-areas/). The cultural relic protection grade assesses historical significance and brings legal duties about repair and reconstruction with it.

### When was China's cultural relics law last changed?

It was comprehensively revised on 8 November 2024 and took effect on 1 March 2025, restating the protection principles for immovable relics — including that repairs must not alter the original state and must follow minimum intervention — and keeping the prohibition on rebuilding a wholly destroyed relic on its original site.
