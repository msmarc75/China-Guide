"Chinese opera" is a category, not a work — the way "European theatre" is. China has hundreds of regional operatic forms, distinguished by dialect, instrumentation and singing style, and a Chinese audience hears the difference between them immediately.

Two of them matter most to a visitor, and the order in which they are usually presented is the wrong way round.

## Peking opera is not the oldest

**Peking opera** (京剧, *jīngjù*) is the one everyone has heard of. It coalesced in Beijing, is sung largely in Beijing dialect, combines singing, recitation, acting and martial arts, and was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in **2010**.

**Kunqu** (昆曲, *kūnqǔ*) is older by centuries. It originated in Kunshan in Jiangsu, has a history usually given as more than six hundred years, and is known in Chinese as the ancestor or teacher of a hundred operas — the form the later regional operas grew out of. UNESCO proclaimed it a **Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity on 18 May 2001**, in the very first group of nineteen, and it moved onto the Representative List in 2008 when that earlier programme was folded in.

So the international recognition ran Kunqu first, Peking opera nine years later. If you have room for one performance and you want the deeper tradition rather than the famous one, Kunqu in Suzhou or Nanjing is the better answer than Peking opera in Beijing.

## How to read a performance without the language

This is the part worth knowing before you sit down, because the form is designed to be legible on sight. Every character is assigned to one of four role types, and the costume, the make-up and the movement announce which before anyone sings.

| Role | Character | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sheng** (生) | Male roles | Subdivided by age and bearing; the scholar, the warrior, the old man |
| **Dan** (旦) | Female roles | Historically played by male performers, now overwhelmingly by women |
| **Jing** (净) | The painted faces | Bold, rough or dangerous men; elaborate full-face patterns |
| **Chou** (丑) | The clown | Identified by a patch of white around the nose |

The **jing** face patterns are the ones in every photograph, and they are a code rather than decoration: colours and patterns signal temperament and moral standing, with a small number of basic patterns generating a very large number of character-specific variants. The **chou**'s white nose patch can mean a quick wit or a mean streak, and which one it is becomes obvious within a scene.

Add to that a stylised vocabulary of movement — a whip stands for a horse, a circuit of the stage covers a journey, a raised foot crosses a threshold that is not there — and a plot you have half a chance of following, since most repertoire comes from the classical novels and histories that [are worth reading before a trip anyway](/culture/chinese-literature-and-film/).

## Sichuan opera, and the trick that is a state secret

Sichuan opera is a different tradition with a broader comic register, and it carries the single most spectacular technique in Chinese performance: **face-changing** (变脸, *biànliǎn*), in which a performer's painted mask changes instantly, repeatedly, with a turn of the head or a sweep of a sleeve.

:::note The method is legally classified
Face-changing was designated a **second-level state secret by China's Ministry of Culture in 1987** — reportedly the only opera technique ever given that status. It has traditionally been transmitted within families, and for most of its history only to men. Reporting on the art notes that the secrecy has eroded since a troupe's overseas tour in the 1980s, and versions of the trick now appear well outside Sichuan.

You will not be told how it is done, and asking is not the done thing. Watch the hands.
:::

Chengdu is where to see it, and an evening of Sichuan opera is a standard fixture of a stay there — [the city's own guide](/destinations/chengdu/) covers what else fills an evening.

## Acrobatics is a separate tradition

Chinese acrobatics is not opera and is not a modern tourist invention; it is its own performance lineage with a long history and its own training system. It travels better than opera does for a foreign audience, because it needs no cultural or linguistic decoding at all, and it is often what a hotel concierge is actually recommending when they say "a show".

## The tourist show and the real theatre

Both exist, and it is worth being clear-eyed about which you are buying.

**The tourist version** is shortened, brightly lit, surtitled or narrated in English, often bundled with a meal or a teahouse setting, and typically an hour of highlights rather than a full work. [Beijing's Lao She Teahouse](/culture/tea-culture-in-china/) is the well-known example of the format. There is nothing wrong with it — a compressed, explained hour is a far better introduction than three hours of a form you cannot read — but it is a sampler.

**The real thing** is a full-length work at a working theatre, mostly attended by people who already know the repertoire, usually without English surtitles. It is a much heavier lift for a first-timer and a much better experience for anyone who did the reading.

**And there is the ordinary evening.** If a full work is too heavy a lift and the tourist hour feels thin, the thing most Chinese people actually do with an evening out is [go to the cinema, where the law requires the film to start at the time printed on your ticket](/culture/going-to-the-cinema-in-china/).

**And there is a free version.** Retired Beijingers sing opera in the parks in the morning, in groups, for their own pleasure — the Temple of Heaven park is the classic place to encounter it, at around seven in the morning, along with tai chi and ballroom dancing. It is amateur and it is completely unstaged, and a lot of visitors find it the most memorable performance they see in China.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Peking opera the oldest form of Chinese opera?

No. Kunqu is considerably older — usually dated to more than six hundred years and known as the ancestor of a hundred operas — and UNESCO recognised it in 2001, nine years before Peking opera was inscribed in 2010. China has hundreds of regional operatic forms rather than a single national one.

### Can I enjoy Chinese opera without speaking Chinese?

Yes, because the form is built to be read visually. Four role types — sheng, dan, jing and chou — are signalled by costume, face paint and movement, painted faces encode temperament in colour and pattern, and a stylised movement vocabulary conveys horses, journeys and doorways. Knowing the four roles before you sit down does most of the work.

### What is Sichuan face-changing?

Bian lian, a Sichuan opera technique in which a performer's painted mask changes instantly and repeatedly. China's Ministry of Culture classified the method as a second-level state secret in 1987, reportedly the only opera technique ever so classified, and it has traditionally been passed down within families.

### Where can I see Chinese opera as a visitor?

Peking opera in Beijing and Kunqu in Suzhou or Nanjing for the two main forms, and Sichuan opera with face-changing in Chengdu. Tourist-oriented performances are shortened and explained in English; working theatres stage full-length works without surtitles. Morning parks offer an amateur, unstaged and free version.

### Is Chinese acrobatics the same as opera?

No, it is a separate performance tradition with its own history and training. It is generally easier for a foreign audience than opera because it needs no linguistic or cultural decoding, and it is frequently what is meant when a hotel recommends "a show".
