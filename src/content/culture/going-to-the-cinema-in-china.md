A Chinese multiplex is modern, enormous, comfortable and cheap, and going to one is a completely ordinary evening that almost no visitor thinks to have. The site already tells you [what to watch before you fly](/culture/chinese-literature-and-film/). This is the other half: how to see a film once you are there.

One thing about it is better than what you are used to at home, and it is written into a statute.

## The printed time is the real time

Chinese cinemas are governed by the **Film Industry Promotion Law**, adopted on 7 November 2016 and in force since **1 March 2017**. Article 32 is short:

> 电影院在向观众明示的电影开始放映时间之后至电影放映结束前，不得放映广告。

**A cinema may not show advertising between the start time announced to the audience and the end of the film.**

It is not advisory. Article 51 makes a breach punishable by a warning and an order to correct, and in serious cases a fine of between ¥10,000 and ¥50,000 imposed by the county-level film authority.

The practical consequence is the one worth carrying: **if the ticket says 19:30, the film begins at 19:30.** Advertising runs before that time, not after it. Arriving at the printed time is arriving on time, which is the opposite of the habit most visitors bring with them.

## Booking is an app, and the seat is yours

There is effectively no box-office queue. Article 30 of the same law requires cinemas to install a computerised ticketing system under national rules, and the consequence is that every seat in the country is numbered, allocated and sold through an app.

Shanghai's own English-language guidance for residents names the two platforms: **猫眼** (*Māoyǎn*, Maoyan) and **淘票票** (*Táopiàopiào*, Taopiaopiao). Both work as standalone apps and both exist inside the wallets — Maoyan as a WeChat mini-programme, Taopiaopiao inside Alipay — which is the usual Chinese pattern of [an application living inside the app you already have](/guides/mini-programmes-in-china/) rather than needing its own install.

The flow is the same on either: pick the cinema, pick the showing, pick your seat from the plan, pay, and an e-ticket appears. At the cinema you either show the code or print the paper ticket at a self-service machine in the lobby.

**Choose your seat deliberately.** Because allocation happens at booking rather than at the door, a popular film sells its centre block first and you are choosing from what is left — the same discipline as booking a train.

## Why the board is mostly Chinese films

This is the thing a visitor notices at the listings screen and has no way to explain.

Article 29 requires cinemas to schedule films made by domestic entities reasonably across sessions and time slots, and sets a floor: **their screening time must be no less than two-thirds of the cinema's annual total.**

So the balance you are looking at is not an accident of taste or of what happens to be released that week. It is a statutory minimum, and it means the majority of what is on will be Chinese-language.

## The language problem, honestly

Here is the constraint, and it is the reason to plan rather than wander in.

**Imported films are generally shown in the original language with Chinese subtitles.** For an English-language film that is exactly what you want, and the app shows the language of each individual showing, so check the specific session rather than the film.

**Chinese films carry Chinese subtitles, not English ones.** Domestic releases are subtitled in Chinese as a matter of course — which helps across dialects and in a noisy room, and does nothing for you. Going to see the big domestic release of the moment is a real cultural experience and it is also two hours of a language you may not read.

That is the honest trade. If you want a Chinese film with English subtitles, the reliable place to find one is a festival screening or, later, a streaming release — [and which films are worth the effort is a separate question](/culture/chinese-literature-and-film/).

## What an evening actually looks like

- **Book in the afternoon** for an evening showing, or the day before at a weekend.
- **Arrive at the printed time.** You are not missing twenty minutes of trailers, because there will not be any.
- **The snack counter is not the point.** Chinese cinemas sell popcorn and drinks, but the room is the product and the ticket is the spend.
- **Screens are large and new.** Most Chinese multiplexes were built in the last fifteen years, so premium formats are widely available rather than confined to one flagship in the capital.
- **It pairs badly with a late train and well with dinner.** If the evening is going to continue, [the other default is a rented karaoke room](/culture/ktv-in-china/), which starts when the film ends.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I watch English-language films in China?

Usually yes. Imported films are generally screened in the original language with Chinese subtitles, and the booking apps show the language of each individual showing — check the session rather than the film, because a single title can run in both original and dubbed versions.

### Do Chinese films have English subtitles in cinemas?

No. Domestic releases carry Chinese subtitles as standard and English ones are not part of a normal cinema release. For a Chinese film with English subtitles, look to a festival screening or a later streaming release.

### How do I book cinema tickets in China?

Through an app — Maoyan or Taopiaopiao, both of which also run inside WeChat and Alipay respectively. You pick the cinema, the showing and your seat, pay, and receive an e-ticket, which you can also print at a machine in the lobby.

### Do adverts play before the film in Chinese cinemas?

Before the announced start time, yes. After it, no — the Film Industry Promotion Law prohibits advertising between the time announced to the audience and the end of the film, with a fine for cinemas that do it anyway. The printed time is when the film starts.

### Why are there so few foreign films in Chinese cinemas?

Because the law sets a floor for domestic ones. Cinemas must give films made by domestic entities no less than two-thirds of their annual screening time, so the majority of any listings board is Chinese-language by statutory design.
