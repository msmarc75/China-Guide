If a Chinese colleague or new friend suggests an evening out, there is a good chance it ends in a **KTV** — and if your mental image is a pub with a microphone and a stranger murdering a ballad, put it aside. Nobody sings in front of strangers. You rent a room.

The site already says that in one line and tells you to accept the invitation. It is right. What follows is what nobody explains, including the reason the door has a window in it.

## What you are actually renting

A **包厢** (*bāo xiāng*, private room), by the hour, for your group only. Inside: sofas around a low table, a large screen, a couple of microphones, and a touchscreen where you queue songs. Drinks and food are ordered to the room rather than fetched from a bar.

Everyone sings. That is the part visitors get wrong — it is not a performance with an audience, it is an activity everyone does badly together, and sitting it out entirely reads as withdrawing from the evening rather than as modesty. The song machines carry English-language catalogues, usually large ones, so the language is not an excuse and picking something famous is the whole strategy.

Two things follow from renting a room rather than buying a drink. The bill is for the room and what came into it, so it is settled as a group and not as rounds. And the evening has no natural closing time built into it — which brings us to the law.

## Why the door has a window in it

Every mainstream KTV in China is an entertainment venue under a State Council regulation — **Order No. 458**, in force since **1 March 2006** and amended in 2016 and again in November 2020, replacing a 1999 predecessor.

Article 16 is the one you can see with your own eyes:

> 歌舞娱乐场所的包厢、包间内不得设置隔断，并应当安装展现室内整体环境的透明门窗。包厢、包间的门不得有内锁装置。

Private rooms in song-and-dance venues **may not contain partitions**, must have **transparent doors or windows showing the whole of the interior**, and **the door may not have an internal lock.**

That is why the room you are shown into has a glass panel in the door and no way to lock yourself in. It is not an oversight in the design. It is the design, and it exists so that what happens in the room can be seen from the corridor.

## The signs on the wall are load-bearing

Article 30 requires venues to display, **in the hall and inside every private room**, warning notices covering the prohibition of drugs, gambling and prostitution, along with signs barring or restricting minors — and those notices must carry **the reporting telephone numbers of the public security and culture authorities.**

Most visitors read these as decorative officialdom. Read them instead as an index of what the regulation expects to go wrong in this category of venue, and as a route that exists if it does.

## The price list you are owed

Article 29 is short and worth knowing before the bill arrives:

> 娱乐场所提供娱乐服务项目和出售商品，应当明码标价，并向消费者出示价目表；不得强迫、欺骗消费者接受服务、购买商品。

Prices must be clearly marked, **a price list must be shown to the customer**, and the venue **may not force or deceive a customer into accepting a service or buying goods.**

This is the third trade on this site where the same national duty turns up applied by name — [the marked price binds and nothing may be added beyond it](/guides/marked-prices-in-china/) is the general rule, and entertainment venues get their own article restating it. The reason is the upsell: fruit platters, spirits by the bottle, and "service" nobody asked for. **Ask for the price list when you take the room, not when you leave it.**

## The evening ends at two

Article 28: **entertainment venues may not trade between 02:00 and 08:00.** Not a licensing quirk that varies by district — a national rule.

Plan the end of the night around it rather than being surprised by it, and remember that [the metro will have stopped long before](/guides/riding-the-chinese-metro/), so the way home is a car.

## The distinction that matters

Be clear-eyed about this, because the word covers two different things.

The overwhelming majority of KTV is mainstream: chains and independents where families, colleagues and school friends rent a room and sing. That is what you will be invited to and there is nothing to be wary of in it.

A separate category of venue uses the same vocabulary and sells something else. The site's position on those is set out where it belongs — [premises trading under a respectable sign where the service is not the advertised one are raided, and a foreign passport present during a raid is its own problem](/culture/massage-and-bodywork-in-china/) regardless of why you were there. The tells are the same in both trades: you did not choose the venue, nobody will show you a price list, and someone is very keen that you come in.

## If you are invited

- **Say yes.** It is a better window into how people socialise than any expat bar.
- **Sing something.** Badly is fine and expected. Refusing entirely is the only wrong move.
- **Pick famous.** The English catalogue is deep; the room wants a chorus it can join.
- **Let the host host.** Whoever invited you will handle the room and usually the bill; offering to split is polite, insisting is not.
- **Ask for the price list early**, especially if anyone starts ordering bottles.
- **Watch the toasting.** Baijiu arrives in these rooms, and [the toasting conventions are their own subject](/culture/chinese-etiquette/) — a glass raised at you is an invitation, not an order.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is KTV in China?

Private-room karaoke. You rent a room by the hour for your own group, order food and drinks to it, and everyone takes turns singing. It is the default night out for most people in China, and it is nothing like singing in front of strangers in a bar.

### Do Chinese KTV rooms have English songs?

Yes, and usually a large catalogue. The song machine has a language filter. Choosing something widely known matters more than singing it well, because the room is going to join in.

### Why do KTV rooms have a window in the door?

Because a State Council regulation requires it. Private rooms in song-and-dance venues may not have partitions, must have transparent doors or windows showing the whole interior, and their doors may not have an internal lock, so the room can be seen from outside.

### What time do KTV venues close in China?

Entertainment venues may not trade between 02:00 and 08:00 under the national regulation, so the night ends by two. The metro will have stopped well before that, so plan a car home.

### Is KTV expensive in China?

It varies enormously by venue and by hour, and the bill is for the room plus whatever was ordered into it rather than per person. The protection is procedural rather than a price cap: the venue must mark its prices and show you a price list, and may not force or deceive you into taking services or buying goods.
