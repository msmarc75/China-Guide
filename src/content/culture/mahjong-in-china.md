Chinese cities have a soundtrack, and in Chengdu a large part of it is tiles. It comes from teahouses, from ground-floor rooms with the door propped open, from card tables set up on a pavement under a tree — a hard clattering rattle, then a pause, then it starts again.

This site has already told you it matters. The Chengdu nightlife page says that [mahjong is not a tourist activity but the activity](/destinations/chengdu/nightlife/), and the tea guide lists it among [the things a Sichuan teahouse afternoon consists of](/culture/tea-culture-in-china/). Neither says what it is. When this page was written no page here covered any Chinese game at all; [weiqi and Chinese chess have since been given one of their own](/culture/weiqi-and-chinese-chess/), and this page is about the tiles.

## What you are looking at

**麻将** (*májiàng*) is a four-player tile game. Players draw and discard from a wall of tiles, building a hand of sets and pairs, and the round ends when someone completes one. It is closer to rummy than to poker in structure, and it is played fast by people who have been playing since childhood.

Two things surprise visitors.

**The table deals itself.** Most parlours and teahouses now use an automatic machine — the General Administration of Sport, describing a competition run on them, calls them **智能麻将机** (*zhìnéng májiàng jī*, intelligent mahjong machines) and notes that they handle **automatic tile sorting, scoring, statistics and live streaming**. Tiles are pushed into a slot in the middle, and the next wall rises through the table already stacked. It is why the noise is so regular, and why a game moves so quickly.

**The rules are local.** There is no single Chinese mahjong. Sichuan plays a format called **血战到底** (*xuèzhàn dàodǐ*, fight to the bitter end), in which play continues after the first winner instead of stopping — a version formalised enough that competitions have been run under it. Other provinces play their own. Learning "mahjong" does not transfer cleanly across a provincial border, which is one reason a table will happily teach a foreigner rather than assume they know.

## Three games, one set of tiles

The single most useful thing to understand is that Chinese law does not see one activity here. It sees three, and they are distinguished by intention and stakes rather than by the tiles.

**As a sport.** Competitive mahjong is organised, scored and played without stakes. The General Administration of Sport has published on competitions run to promote mahjong as a **healthy, positive and standardised sport** supporting the national-fitness programme, played under rules endorsed by the World Mahjong Organization and the International Mind Sports Association. This is the same category logic the site met when [a sporting fixture and a commercial performance turned out to sit under different regimes](/guides/watching-sport-in-china/).

**As recreation.** Four friends in a teahouse, small sums moving around the table, an afternoon gone. This is what the overwhelming majority of mahjong in China is, and it is what you are most likely to be invited into.

**As gambling.** An operation run for profit, or play at a scale the law treats as serious.

## Where the line is, and the article that moved

Here is the part that is out of date everywhere else in English.

The **中华人民共和国治安管理处罚法** (*Zhōnghuá Rénmín Gònghéguó Zhì'ān Guǎnlǐ Chǔfáfǎ*, Public Security Administration Punishments Law) received its **first comprehensive revision** since it was enacted. The revised law was passed by the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress on **27 June 2025**, promulgated as **Presidential Order No. 49**, runs to **six chapters and 144 articles**, and has been **in force since 1 January 2026**.

The gambling provision is now **Article 82**. It used to be Article 70, and Article 70 is what almost every English-language page on this subject still cites.

> 以营利为目的，为赌博提供条件的，或者参与赌博赌资较大的

Two limbs: **providing the conditions for gambling for the purpose of profit**, or **taking part in gambling with relatively large stakes**. Either brings administrative detention or a fine, and the revision raised the maximums rather than lowering them. Above that sits the Criminal Law, which deals separately with assembling people to gamble for profit, making a living from it, and running a gambling house.

Read the first limb again, because it is the one that describes a venue rather than a player. **The person the provision is aimed at first is whoever is running the room for money.**

## The recreational play that is not punished

The counterpart is a Ministry of Public Security instrument, **公通字〔2005〕30号** (*gōng tōng zì*, public security general circular no. 30 of 2005), the notice on applying the law in gambling cases. Its Article 9 addresses mahjong by name:

Where play is **not for the purpose of profit**, an activity involving winning and losing property **among relatives** is not punished; and among people other than relatives, a recreational activity involving **small amounts** of property changing hands is not punished either.

So the afternoon in the teahouse is not an offence hiding in plain sight. It is the case the rules explicitly decline to punish.

:::warn There is no national money figure — and this is where English sources go wrong
"Relatively large stakes" is not defined nationally in yuan. Public security bureaux publish their own discretionary benchmarks: Fuzhou, answering a citizen on its municipal portal, has cited a per-person starting figure under its own local penalty-benchmark notice.

That is **one city's benchmark**, issued by that city's police, and it was published against the old article number. Any page that quotes a single number as the Chinese national threshold is quoting a municipality and presenting it as the country. Where the line falls in practice is a local judgement, which is exactly why the safe posture below is about conduct rather than arithmetic.
:::

## If you are invited to a table

The invitation is genuine and it is a good thing to accept. The Chengdu page already gives the correct social move — admit you cannot play and let someone teach you.

- **Say yes, and say you do not know the local rules.** They will differ from any mahjong you have played, and being taught is the normal way in.
- **Play the recreational version.** Friends, a teahouse, trivial sums or none. That is the case the 2005 notice describes.
- **Do not be the person organising it for money**, and be wary of any room where somebody is taking a cut of the play. Running the venue for profit is the first limb of Article 82, and a foreign passport in a room being cleared is a bad place to be.
- **Table charges are ordinary.** A teahouse charges for tea and for the table; that is a venue fee, not a stake.
- **Expect it to be loud and long.** An afternoon is the unit, in the same way that [an early morning in a Chinese park is a unit](/culture/parks-in-the-morning-in-china/).
- **Hong Kong and Macau are separate systems entirely**, with their own law on gaming, and nothing on this page describes them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is playing mahjong legal in China?

Yes. Mahjong itself is an ordinary pastime and is also played competitively as an organised, unstaked sport. What the law targets is gambling: providing the conditions for it with a profit motive, or taking part with relatively large stakes. A Ministry of Public Security notice states that recreational play not aimed at profit, with small sums among non-relatives or with relatives generally, is not punished.

### Is mahjong for money gambling in China?

It depends on intention and scale rather than on the presence of money. Small sums in a recreational game are the case the rules decline to punish; play at a scale treated as sizeable, or a room operated for profit, is the case they address. There is no national figure separating the two, so the judgement is made locally.

### Which law covers gambling in China?

The Public Security Administration Punishments Law, comprehensively revised on 27 June 2025 and in force since 1 January 2026, where the provision is Article 82. Serious conduct is dealt with by the Criminal Law instead. Note that the article was renumbered — older English sources cite Article 70.

### Why do Chinese mahjong tables deal themselves?

Because most venues use automatic machines. The General Administration of Sport, writing about competitions played on them, describes intelligent mahjong machines handling tile sorting, scoring, statistics and live streaming. The wall rises through the table already stacked, which is why games move so fast.

### What is Sichuan mahjong?

A regional format known as fight-to-the-bitter-end, in which play continues after the first player completes a hand rather than the round ending there. Rules vary substantially by province, so mahjong learned in one Chinese city does not transfer unchanged to another.

### Can foreigners play mahjong in China?

Yes, and an invitation is common in teahouse cities. The polite and practical answer is to admit you do not know the local variant and let the table teach you. Keep it recreational, and avoid any venue where someone appears to be running the game for profit.
