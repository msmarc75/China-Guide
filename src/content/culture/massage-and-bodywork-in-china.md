Western visitors tend to arrive with one mental category — massage as a spa treatment, booked, expensive, quiet, faintly indulgent. China has at least three, they use overlapping vocabulary, and the differences between them are what decide whether you have a good evening.

## Tuina is medicine, not relaxation

推拿 (*tuīná*) is one of the branches of Chinese medicine, alongside acupuncture, herbal medicine and dietary therapy. It is old: the *Huangdi Neijing*, the foundational Chinese medical text, discusses 按跷 (*àn qiāo*) — a hands-and-feet manipulation technique — and describes when it is and is not indicated. A dedicated massage classic, the *Huangdi Anmo Jing*, is recorded but lost.

What that history means in practice is that tuina is worked on you rather than administered to you. You keep your clothes on, or change into loose cotton the shop provides; the practitioner works dry, through fabric, with thumbs, elbows and forearms; and it is often uncomfortable. It is closer to physiotherapy than to a massage table with whale song.

Hospitals of traditional Chinese medicine run tuina departments, and that is where you go for an actual back problem rather than a tired afternoon. ## Foot treatment is a social activity

足疗 (*zúliáo*) is the one most visitors will actually experience, and it is not a spa treatment in the Western sense. You stay dressed, trousers rolled; your feet go into a hot herbal basin; then someone works hard on your feet, ankles and calves for an hour, frequently extending to shoulders and neck.

The important part is the setting. It happens in a shared room, with a television on, at conversational volume, often with friends or colleagues in the adjacent chairs. It is an ordinary way to spend an evening — the equivalent of going to a pub, not of booking a treatment. Prices are a fraction of the Western equivalent, which is why it is a routine weekly habit for a great many people rather than an occasional luxury.

If the pressure is too much, **轻一点** (*qīng yīdiǎn*) — lighter, please — is understood everywhere and causes no awkwardness at all.

## Why the blind-masseur sign matters

The single most useful thing on a Chinese street sign is 盲人按摩 (*mángrén ànmó*), blind masseur. It is worth understanding why.

The same two-categories-one-vocabulary problem turns up in nightlife, where [private-room karaoke is overwhelmingly mainstream and a separate kind of venue borrows the name](/culture/ktv-in-china/), and where the tells are identical.

Massage has been the principal organised employment route for blind people in China for decades, run as national policy through the China Disabled Persons' Federation. The federation's own statistical bulletins put the scale of it plainly: it counts **over 22,000 blind health-massage establishments** nationally, alongside roughly a thousand medical-massage ones, and it trains thousands of new practitioners in each category every year.

Two consequences follow, and both matter to a visitor.

**The practitioners are trained and examined.** Medical massage practitioners sit a national examination organised by the federation's examination committee, and the resulting certificate is what permits them to practise. It is not a decorative qualification.

**The premises had to clear a licensing bar to exist.** The certificate is also required to open a blind medical-massage establishment. A business fronting for something else has no reason to enter that regime — which is why the sign is a signal about the shop and not only about the masseur.

The practitioners are also, in the ordinary sense, very good. The work is done by touch and the touch is the profession.

## The category is genuinely undocumented

There is no reliable directory of massage shops in China. Places open, change hands and close without leaving a trace in any source a foreign visitor can read, and a share of premises trading under plain 按摩 (*ànmó*) signage are fronts for sex work, which is illegal and periodically raided.

This is why our city pages name no parlours. It is not caution for its own sake — a recommendation we cannot verify is worse than none. What we give instead is the skill: how to read a frontage, what the vocabulary on the sign indicates, and what to do if something other than massage is offered. That last one has a short answer, and it is the same in every city: settle what you owe and leave without arguing. A foreign passport in the room during a raid means detention and probably deportation, whatever you went in for.

## Regional variation worth knowing

**Chengdu adds ear cleaning.** 采耳 (*cǎi'ěr*) is performed in the open at teahouses, it is a genuine local tradition rather than something invented for visitors, and it carries a documented infection risk with a specific mechanism — shared tools, grazed ear canals, and fungi that resist the disinfection these operations typically use. The [Chengdu page sets out both the tradition and the risk](/destinations/chengdu/massage-and-spa/) rather than one or the other.

Beyond that, our four city pages differ mainly in their local licensing detail rather than in what is on offer — Shanghai's certificate is re-registered every two years, Chengdu administers the national scheme through prefecture-level federations. Foot treatment and tuina are available in every Chinese city of any size, and the shopfront-reading skill transfers unchanged.

## Practical points

- **Tipping is not customary** — not in massage shops, not at teahouse tables. Do not.
- **Alipay and WeChat Pay** are accepted essentially everywhere, including by ear cleaners working a park.
- **Decline prepaid membership cards.** They are the standard upsell and you are not coming back next month.
- **Your hotel can book it for you** at a higher price, which removes the judgement call entirely — a reasonable trade on your first night.
- **Look for the certificate.** A bathhouse or spa is a public place in law, so [the hygiene licence must be on show and the venue's test results published in a prominent position](/guides/hygiene-licences-in-china/).
