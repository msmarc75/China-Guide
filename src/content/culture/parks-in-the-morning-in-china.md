Almost every itinerary on this site contains the same instruction, and it is good advice: be in a park early. [Beijing's Temple of Heaven grounds](/destinations/beijing/) are the standard example, [People's Park in Chengdu](/destinations/chengdu/) is the other, and West Lake at dawn is the third.

What none of those pages does is explain the scene once you are standing in it. This page is that explanation, because the morning park is not a quaint survival or a performance laid on for anybody. It is a functioning social institution with a demography behind it and, in one respect, a statute.

## Why there are so many of them

Start with the thing that actually produces the crowd, because it is the part no travel writing mentions.

China's statutory retirement ages have been low by international standards: **60 for men, and 50 or 55 for women** depending on the category of work. A country of that size with those thresholds has an enormous population of people who are fully retired, in good health, financially settled, living in dense cities — and free every single morning.

That is now changing, slowly and by design. On **13 September 2024** the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress adopted a decision to raise the statutory ages **gradually over fifteen years**, taking effect from **1 January 2025**: men from 60 to 63, and women from 55 to 58 and from 50 to 55. The pace is deliberately glacial — for men and for women retiring at 55, the age moves by one month every four months; for women retiring at 50, by one month every two months.

The point for a visitor is that the reform confirms the picture rather than ending it. The parks will look like this for a long time yet.

It also explains something the site mentions elsewhere without connecting: [people aged 60 to 69 get half fare on the railways](/answers/do-students-get-discounts-at-chinese-attractions/). The same demographic that fills a train at a concessionary fare fills the park at seven.

## What you are actually looking at

Several completely separate activities run in parallel, usually in different corners, each with its own regulars. Learning to tell them apart is most of the pleasure.

- **Tai chi**, alone or in a loose group, often silent, often with a teacher at the front and no formality about who joins. Sword and fan forms are the same tradition with an object in the hand.
- **Ballroom dancing.** Couples, in the open, to music from a portable speaker, and taken seriously. This is social dancing rather than exercise, and the standard is frequently high.
- **Square dancing** — the large synchronised group, usually women, facing one leader, doing a set routine to loud pop or folk music. It is the most visible and most-discussed leisure activity in urban China.
- **Group singing.** Choirs with a conductor and printed sheets, and separately the [amateur opera circles this site sends you to hear](/culture/chinese-opera-and-performance/), which are unstaged and free.
- **Water calligraphy.** A long brush, a bucket of water and a paving slab. The characters evaporate in minutes, which is the point: the practice is the object, not the artefact.
- **Jianzi**, the weighted shuttlecock kept up with the feet, played in a standing circle. Easy to be invited into and hard to be good at.
- **Badminton and diabolo**, both without a net or a stage, both everywhere.

## Why the music goes quiet

Here is the detail visitors notice and cannot explain: a dance group with a speaker at full volume, and then — at some particular moment, or in some particular corner — restraint. There is a law behind it.

The **Noise Pollution Prevention and Control Law**, adopted on 24 December 2021 and **in force since 5 June 2022**, replaced the 1996 environmental noise law. Its Article 64 addresses this activity by name:

> 在街道、广场、公园等公共场所组织或者开展娱乐、健身等活动，应当遵守公共场所管理者有关活动区域、时段、音量等规定，采取有效措施，防止噪声污染；不得违反规定使用音响器材产生过大音量。

Anyone organising or taking part in entertainment or fitness activities **in a street, square, park or other public place must comply with the manager's rules on where, when and how loudly**, must take effective measures against noise pollution, and may not use sound equipment at excessive volume in breach of those rules.

The second half of the article is the one that explains what you see. **The manager of the public place must set reasonable rules on the area, the time slots and the volume** for entertainment and fitness activities — and may install **automatic noise monitoring and display equipment** to enforce them.

That is what the electronic board showing a number near a dance group is. It is a live decibel readout, and it is there because the statute contemplates it.

Enforcement is unusually gentle by design: the designated local authority is directed to **persuade and educate first**, then order correction. Only on refusal does it become a warning and a fine — ¥200 to ¥1,000 for an individual, ¥2,000 to ¥20,000 for an organisation.

So the tension you may sense between the dancers and the flats behind them is real, it is nationally recognised, and it has been given a procedure rather than a ban.

## The matchmaking corner

One further thing happens in some parks, usually at weekends rather than at dawn, and it is worth knowing before you walk into it: an open-air marriage market.

Parents — not the young people themselves — display **A4 sheets in plastic sleeves**, hung from railings, umbrellas and tree branches, each advertising an adult child. The sheets carry year of birth, height, education, occupation and income, and what is being sought in return. Photographs are generally not shown until an approach turns serious. Shanghai's People's Park is the best-known, and [Chengdu's People's Park has its own](/destinations/chengdu/).

Two points of manners. It is a public display in a public park and looking is not intrusive, but it is a real negotiation about real people rather than a spectacle, so photograph the scene rather than individual sheets and faces. And be aware that a foreigner reading the boards attentively is occasionally approached — the answer is a smile and a shake of the head.

## How to be there properly

- **Go early and go on a weekday.** The park fills before most hotels finish breakfast and thins out well before mid-morning.
- **Walk the whole park before settling.** The activities are distributed, not concentrated, and the interesting one is usually not the first one you meet.
- **You may join almost anything.** Tai chi at the back, jianzi in a circle, a dance where couples are swapping — none of it requires a word of Mandarin, and turning up is the whole application process.
- **Do not join a rehearsed routine mid-flow.** A choir with sheet music and a square-dance line running a set piece are performing something they have practised; stand and watch those.
- **Ask before photographing a person close up.** Wide shots of a public scene are unremarkable; a lens in someone's face is the same discourtesy it is anywhere.
- **Bring nothing.** There is no equipment to hire and no ticket to the activity, though the park itself may charge admission.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why are Chinese parks full of people early in the morning?

Because China has a very large retired population with early statutory retirement ages — 60 for men and 50 or 55 for women — so a great many healthy people in their fifties and sixties have every morning free. Those ages began rising gradually from January 2025, but over a fifteen-year phase-in, so the pattern is not about to change.

### What time should I go to a Chinese park?

Early. The activity builds from around dawn and is largely over by mid-morning, which is why itineraries on this site put the Temple of Heaven and People's Park before the rest of the day rather than after it.

### Can I join in tai chi or dancing in a Chinese park?

Yes, and it is entirely normal. Stand at the back of a tai chi group, join a jianzi circle, accept a dance if offered. Nobody will ask who you are and no language is needed. The exception is a rehearsed routine — a choir or a set square-dance piece — which is better watched than joined.

### What is square dancing in China?

Large synchronised group dancing, mostly by retired women, to amplified music in a public square or park, following one leader through a set routine. It is the most widespread organised leisure activity in urban China and the main reason parks have rules about volume.

### Is there a law about music in Chinese parks?

Yes. Under Article 64 of the Noise Pollution Prevention and Control Law, in force since June 2022, people running entertainment or fitness activities in public places must follow the manager's rules on area, timing and volume, and the manager must set those rules — and may install automatic noise monitoring and display equipment, which is what the decibel boards in some parks are.

### What is the marriage market in a Chinese park?

An open-air matchmaking display, usually at weekends, where parents post sheets advertising their unmarried adult children with age, height, education, occupation and income, and negotiate with other parents. Shanghai's People's Park is the famous one; Chengdu has another.
