Three pages here mention libraries. One lists them among the premises that need a hygiene filing. One counts them in a tally of Shenzhen venues that dropped their booking requirement. One quotes the law on concessions for older visitors, which names public libraries alongside museums and parks.

In none of them is a library somewhere you might actually go. That is a gap worth closing, because a Chinese city library is free, air-conditioned, open longer than almost anything else, and — in the cities that have built new ones this century — frequently the most striking modern building in town.

## The entitlement is a national law

The **中华人民共和国公共图书馆法** (*Zhōnghuá Rénmín Gònghéguó Gōnggòng Túshūguǎn Fǎ*, Public Library Law of the People's Republic of China) was adopted on **4 November 2017** at the 30th session of the 12th National People's Congress Standing Committee and has been **in force since 1 January 2018**. It was amended once, by the omnibus decision of **26 October 2018** that revised fifteen laws at a stroke; nothing has changed it since.

Its **Article 2** defines a public library as a cultural facility that is **open to the public free of charge**, collecting, organising and preserving documents and information and providing search, borrowing and related services.

Free is not a policy each library adopts. It is what the word means in the statute.

## What "free" actually covers

**Article 33** is the provision to know, because it goes well beyond the books. A public library established by government must provide, **free of charge**:

1. **document and information search, and borrowing**;
2. **the opening of reading rooms, self-study rooms and other public spaces, facilities and sites**;
3. **public-interest lectures, reading promotion, training and exhibitions**;
4. other free services prescribed by the state.

The second item is the one a traveller uses. **A desk in a reading room is a free service by law**, not a courtesy extended to people who look like they belong there. In a city where the alternative is buying a coffee every ninety minutes, that is a genuinely useful thing to know at three in the afternoon in July.

**Article 34** adds that a government-established public library must provide a **children's reading area**, and that areas with the means may set up separate children's libraries — which is why [travelling in China with children](/plan/china-with-children/) has an indoor option most guides never mention.

## Opening hours: the law sets the duty, cities set the numbers

**Article 38** of the national law is short and does two things: a public library **must open on public rest days**, and **must have opening hours on national statutory holidays**.

Note what it does not do. It puts no number on it.

:::warn There is no national hour count, and this is where it gets misreported
Search for Chinese library opening hours and you will find precise weekly and daily figures presented as national rules. They are not. The national law states the duty; **the hour counts come from municipal and provincial regulations**, and they differ.

**Beijing** is the clearest case. **Article 21** of the **北京市图书馆条例** (*Běijīng Shì Túshūguǎn Tiáolì*, Beijing Municipal Libraries Regulations) — adopted in 2002 and last amended on 25 November 2016 — sets municipal public libraries at **not less than 70 hours a week**, district public libraries at **not less than 63 hours**, and children's libraries at **not less than 43 hours**, with **not less than 8 hours a day** during national statutory holidays and school winter and summer vacations.

Those are Beijing figures under a Beijing regulation. **Hubei** carries a comparable vacation provision in its own provincial regulations. Quote either as the Chinese national rule and you are presenting one jurisdiction as the country.
:::

Seventy hours a week is ten hours a day, seven days a week. Set against a Chinese museum, which closes on Mondays and shuts in the late afternoon, that is a different kind of institution.

## Shanghai rewrote its own rules in 2025

The local layer is also the layer that moves, and one major destination has just changed.

The **上海市公共图书馆条例** (*Shànghǎi Shì Gōnggòng Túshūguǎn Tiáolì*, Shanghai Municipal Public Library Regulations) was adopted by the Standing Committee of the 16th Shanghai Municipal People's Congress on **28 November 2024** and has been **in force since 1 January 2025**. It is a municipal regulation and binds in Shanghai only.

It restates free opening to the public, requires libraries to keep **fixed opening hours** and to have hours on national holidays, and encourages **extended-hours and staggered-hours service** in response to public demand — the recognition that a library open only while everyone is at work is not open in any useful sense.

## Using one as a visitor

Here the page has to be careful about what it can and cannot tell you.

**Reading on site is the free entitlement, and it is the one that matters to you.** The national law creates no registration requirement and names no document, so it neither grants nor withholds anything on the basis of nationality.

**Borrowing is a different matter.** A library card, any deposit, and what identification is accepted are set by each library rather than by the statute, and a visitor passing through has little use for one anyway. This site does not invent those details, so ask at the desk.

**This site names no specific libraries.** The same reasoning applies as with [massage shops](/culture/massage-and-bodywork-in-china/): a venue we cannot verify is worse than none. What transfers is the structure — every Chinese city of size has a **municipal library**, most districts have their own, and the municipal one is usually a recent, deliberately impressive building on a metro line.

**And it is not a booking problem.** Libraries were among the venues cities released from reservation requirements, though [the general rule about checking a specific venue rather than trusting a headline](/guides/booking-chinese-attractions/) applies here as everywhere.

## Why the buildings are worth the detour anyway

There is a second reason to go, which has nothing to do with the reading rooms.

Municipal library projects have been a favoured category of Chinese public building for two decades, and they are where a city puts its architectural ambition when it is not building a museum. Anyone who has spent a week reading roofs and bracket sets will find [the contrast with the timber tradition](/culture/chinese-art-and-architecture/) instructive: same country, opposite century, and free to walk into.

## In practice

- **Go in the afternoon heat or the rain.** It is free, it is quiet, and nobody will move you on — and on [an orange or red heat day](/guides/heat-warnings-in-china/) that is the whole plan for the middle of the afternoon.
- **Look for the municipal library**, which is the largest and usually the newest.
- **Expect a bag scanner** at the door, as at any large Chinese public building.
- **Reading rooms and study desks are covered by the free-service duty**, not just the shelves.
- **Do not assume museum hours.** A library is likelier to be open on a Monday, on a Sunday and on a public holiday than a museum is.
- **Hong Kong and Macau run their own library systems** under their own law, and none of this describes them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are public libraries in China free?

Yes, by statute. The Public Library Law defines a public library as a facility open to the public free of charge, and requires government-established libraries to provide document search and borrowing, the opening of reading rooms and self-study rooms, and public-interest lectures and exhibitions without charge.

### Can foreigners use Chinese public libraries?

For reading on site, yes. The national law creates no registration requirement and names no document, so nothing in it turns on nationality. Borrowing normally involves a card issued on each library's own terms, which is a matter for the desk rather than the statute.

### What are the opening hours of Chinese public libraries?

The national law requires libraries to open on public rest days and to have opening hours on national statutory holidays, but sets no figure. The numbers are local: Beijing requires at least 70 hours a week at municipal libraries and 63 at district ones, under its own municipal regulations.

### Do Chinese libraries open on public holidays?

They are required to have opening hours on national statutory holidays, and to open on public rest days, which makes a library one of the more reliable places to find open when a museum is not. Local regulations add specific minimums in some cities.

### Is there a children's section in Chinese public libraries?

Yes. Government-established public libraries must provide a children's reading area, and places with the means may set up separate children's libraries. Beijing additionally requires children's libraries to open at least 8 hours a day during statutory holidays and school vacations.

### Did China change its library rules recently?

The national law has stood unamended since a housekeeping revision in October 2018. Locally, Shanghai adopted new municipal public library regulations on 28 November 2024, in force since 1 January 2025, which restate free opening and encourage extended and staggered hours.
