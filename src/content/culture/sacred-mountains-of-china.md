Chinese mountains are not sacred in a vague scenic sense. Nine of them are sacred in a specific, systematised, administratively recorded way, and they belong to two entirely separate schemes that visitors routinely blend into one.

Knowing which is which turns a confusing list of names into something legible — and explains why the mountain most foreigners can picture is not on either list.

## The Five Great Mountains: a map of the cosmos

The **五岳** (*wǔyuè*), the Five Great Mountains, are a Taoist and imperial system rather than a ranking of scenery. They are placed at the four compass points and the centre, which is the whole idea: the emperor's realm made legible as a landscape.

| Mountain | Province | Position | Known for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Taishan** (*Tài Shān*) | Shandong | East | The imperial mountain; the great stairway |
| **Huashan** (*Huà Shān*) | Shaanxi | West | Granite spires, and the plank walk |
| **Hengshan** (*Héng Shān*) | Hunan | South | Forested, temple-dense, less visited by foreigners |
| **Hengshan** (*Héng Shān*) | Shanxi | North | The Hanging Temple below it is the draw |
| **Songshan** (*Sōng Shān*) | Henan | Centre | Shaolin Temple sits on it |

The two Hengshans are a genuine trap: different characters, different provinces, the same romanisation without tone marks. If a booking or a schedule says "Hengshan", establish which province before you commit to anything.

### Taishan, and why it outranks the rest

Taishan in the east is first among the five, and the reason is political rather than geological. It was the site of the **封禅** (*fēngshàn*) sacrifices — rites in which an emperor announced to heaven and earth that his mandate was secure. Not every emperor performed them; performing them was a claim, and one that a ruler had to be confident enough to make.

The ascent is a stone stairway, and it is the reference case for [what "hiking" means on a Chinese mountain](/guides/hiking-in-china/). Published step counts differ, and the disagreement is not sloppiness: sources variously give around 6,293 for the official imperial route, about 6,660, and roughly 7,200 if you count the steps inside the temples and pavilions along the way. **More than six thousand** is the figure to plan your knees around.

## The Four Buddhist Mountains: four bodhisattvas

The Buddhist four are a different scheme entirely. Each mountain is understood as the earthly seat, or *bodhimaṇḍa*, of a particular bodhisattva, and pilgrimage is to that figure rather than to the mountain as such.

| Mountain | Province | Bodhisattva | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Wutai** (*Wǔtái Shān*) | Shanxi | Mañjuśrī, of wisdom | Five flat summits; also revered in Tibetan Buddhism |
| **Putuo** (*Pǔtuó Shān*) | Zhejiang | Avalokiteśvara (Guanyin) | An island, reached by ferry |
| **Emei** (*Éméi Shān*) | Sichuan | Samantabhadra | Above the Leshan Giant Buddha |
| **Jiuhua** (*Jiǔhuá Shān*) | Anhui | Kṣitigarbha | The least visited by foreigners |

Guanyin, whose seat is Putuo, is the most widely worshipped figure in Chinese Buddhism, which the [religion guide](/culture/religion-and-philosophy-in-china/) sets out along with how to tell a Buddhist temple from a Taoist one at a glance.

:::tip Pilgrimage mountains are the easiest places in China to eat vegetarian
On all four, monastery kitchens and the restaurants around them cook Buddhist vegetarian food as the default rather than as an accommodation — which is [the reverse of the usual problem](/food/vegetarian-vegan-in-china/) everywhere else in the country.
:::

## What UNESCO recognises, and what that does and does not mean

Four of the nine carry World Heritage inscriptions:

- **Mount Taishan** — inscribed 1987
- **Mount Emei Scenic Area, including Leshan Giant Buddha Scenic Area** — 1996
- **Mount Wutai** — 2009
- **Historic Monuments of Dengfeng in "The Centre of Heaven and Earth"** — 2010, on Songshan, and the property includes the Shaolin Temple complex

The other five are not inscribed, Huashan among them. That is worth stating plainly because inscription is often read as a ranking of importance, and here it is not one: it reflects what has been documented, nominated and successfully argued, not which mountain matters most in Chinese religious practice.

:::note Huangshan is not one of the nine
The Yellow Mountains are China's most photographed peaks and were inscribed by UNESCO in 1990, and they belong to neither the Taoist five nor the Buddhist four. Their fame is aesthetic and artistic rather than liturgical — they are the mountains of the ink paintings. Go for the scenery; [the practicalities are on its own page](/destinations/huangshan/), and none of the pilgrimage framing on this page applies to it.
:::

## Which one to actually go to

- **First trip, and you want the historical weight:** Taishan. It is the one the emperors climbed, it is reachable from Beijing or Shanghai by high-speed rail, and the stairway is the point rather than an obstacle.
- **You want the photograph and you have a head for heights:** Huashan, whose plank walk is the famous one. Take the [health and safety guide's altitude and insurance advice](/guides/health-and-safety-in-china/) seriously here.
- **You are already in Sichuan:** Emei, which pairs with the Leshan Giant Buddha in the same inscribed property.
- **You want the least commercial:** Jiuhua, or the Hunan Hengshan. Both see far fewer foreign visitors than their status suggests.
- **You want an island and a boat:** Putuo.

Whichever you pick, the mechanics are the same as every Chinese mountain — ticketed scenic area, marked routes, enforced closing times, cable cars priced separately — and [the rules that bite are covered separately](/guides/hiking-in-china/) rather than repeated here.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the five sacred mountains of China?

Taishan in Shandong (east), Huashan in Shaanxi (west), Hengshan in Hunan (south), Hengshan in Shanxi (north) and Songshan in Henan (centre). They are known collectively as the 五岳 and are arranged by compass direction as a Taoist and imperial map of the realm rather than ranked by height or beauty.

### What are the four sacred Buddhist mountains?

Wutai in Shanxi, Putuo in Zhejiang, Emei in Sichuan and Jiuhua in Anhui. Each is the earthly seat of a bodhisattva — Mañjuśrī, Avalokiteśvara, Samantabhadra and Kṣitigarbha respectively — and they form a scheme entirely separate from the Taoist five.

### Is Huangshan a sacred mountain?

No. Huangshan is neither one of the Five Great Mountains nor one of the four Buddhist mountains. It was inscribed by UNESCO in 1990 and is China's most famous mountain landscape, but its significance is artistic rather than religious.

### Which sacred mountains are UNESCO World Heritage sites?

Four of the nine: Mount Taishan (1987), Mount Emei with the Leshan Giant Buddha (1996), Mount Wutai (2009), and the Historic Monuments of Dengfeng on Songshan (2010), which include the Shaolin Temple. Huashan and the remaining four are not inscribed, which reflects the nomination record rather than their religious standing.

### How many steps are there up Mount Tai?

More than six thousand, and published figures genuinely differ — roughly 6,293 for the official imperial route, about 6,660 in some accounts, and around 7,200 if the steps inside temples and pavilions along the way are counted. Plan for a long stone staircase rather than a trail.
