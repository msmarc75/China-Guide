[The drinking-age answer](/answers/what-is-the-legal-drinking-age-in-china/) sets out a sentence pattern from the Law on the Protection of Minors: a seller must put up a sign saying it does not serve minors, and must ask for identity documents where it cannot tell. That page found it applied to tobacco, alcohol and lottery tickets.

The same sentence exists in another instrument entirely, about something the site has never covered. It is worth reading beside the first, because the two are drafted almost identically — and because everything else about the tattoo rules is unlike what a visitor would expect.

## One instrument, and it is not about tattooing

The document is the **未成年人文身治理工作办法** (*wèichéngniánrén wénshēn zhìlǐ gōngzuò bànfǎ*, Measures for the Governance of Tattooing of Minors), issued by the **Office of the State Council Leading Group for the Protection of Minors** as **国未保办发〔2022〕6号** on **6 June 2022**, in force from the date of issue.

Read the title again. It is not a tattooing regulation; it is a minors regulation that happens to be about tattoos. The distinction runs through everything below.

The core prohibition is short:

> 任何企业、组织和个人不得向未成年人提供文身服务，不得胁迫、引诱、教唆未成年人文身。

**No enterprise, organisation or individual may provide tattoo services to a minor**, or coerce, entice or incite a minor into being tattooed. The second limb is the wider one: not merely refusing the service, but not pushing anyone towards it.

## Four kinds of place, and no trade of its own

The Measures then have to say who is bound, and the answer is the most revealing sentence in the document:

> 文身服务提供者，主要是指专业文身机构、提供文身服务的医疗卫生机构（含医疗美容机构）和美容美发机构等各类主体，也包括提供文身服务的社会组织。

A "tattoo service provider" means **specialist tattoo establishments**, **medical and health institutions including medical-aesthetic ones** that provide tattooing, **beauty and hairdressing establishments**, and **social organisations** that provide it.

That is a definition by enumeration of things that already exist, and it tells you what the Chinese system does with tattooing: rather than licensing it as an activity and regulating the people who perform it, the rules reach it through whatever kind of establishment happens to be doing it. A tattoo done at a medical-aesthetic clinic sits under health regulation; the same tattoo done in a hairdresser's sits under [the hygiene licence that covers barbershops and beauty salons](/guides/hygiene-licences-in-china/); done in a specialist studio, it sits under ordinary market registration.

## The sentence you have already read on this site

:::warn Two instruments, one piece of drafting
**Article 5 of the tattoo Measures:**

> 文身服务提供者应当在显著位置标明不向未成年人提供文身服务。对难以判明是否是未成年人的，应当要求其出示身份证件。

A provider must **state in a conspicuous position that it does not provide tattoo services to minors**, and where it **cannot tell** whether someone is a minor, must **require them to produce identity documents**.

**Article 59 of the Law on the Protection of Minors**, as [quoted in full on the drinking-age page](/answers/what-is-the-legal-drinking-age-in-china/), contains the identical construction for tobacco, alcohol and lottery tickets: a sign in a conspicuous position, and identity documents where age is unclear.

Same two duties, same order, same trigger. In both instruments the obligation sits on the establishment rather than on the young person, and in both it is discharged by something displayed on the premises rather than filed with anyone.
:::

## The restriction is printed on the licence

This is the part with no obvious parallel elsewhere, and it is the reason the rule is visible to anybody who can read a wall.

The article assigning departmental duties tells the market regulator what to do at the moment a business registers:

> 市场监管部门在办理市场主体登记注册时，对于经营范围中包含文身服务活动的市场主体，应当在其营业执照相关经营范围后明确标注"除面向未成年人"，并指导其自觉依规经营。

Where a business registers a scope that **includes tattoo services**, the market regulation department must **write "除面向未成年人" — "except to minors" — immediately after that entry in the business scope on its business licence**.

[The business licence must be displayed, and its business scope is the line that says what the place is registered to do](/guides/business-licences-in-china/). So the restriction does not live in a circular or a database. It is printed on the certificate hanging behind the counter, in the same field as the activity it qualifies.

The health limb of the same article runs in parallel:

> 卫生健康部门不得审批同意医疗卫生机构（含医疗美容机构）开展未成年人文身服务项目。加大指导监管力度，指导医疗卫生机构（含医疗美容机构）不向未成年人开展文身服务，并对有意愿"去除文身"的未成年人提供规范医疗美容服务。

Health authorities **may not approve** a medical or medical-aesthetic institution to run a tattoo service for minors — and, in the same breath, are told to see that regulated medical-aesthetic **removal** services are available to minors who want a tattoo taken off. The instrument closes one door and deliberately opens another.

## What the Measures do not do

This is the finding a visitor should take away, and it is a finding about absence.

Nothing in the passages read here sets a standard for **needles, inks, sterilisation or single use**. Nothing sets a **qualification** for the person doing the work. Nothing requires a tattoo studio to hold a permission of its own beyond ordinary registration. The Measures regulate **who may be tattooed**, and leave the *how* to whatever regime the venue already sits under — which is precisely why the definition article had to list four kinds of venue rather than name one trade.

For an adult visitor that means the useful questions are the ordinary Chinese ones this site asks everywhere else: what does the displayed licence say the place is, and what else is on the wall.

## In practice

- **Read the business licence, not the studio's Instagram.** The business scope is where tattooing appears as a registered activity, and where the "except to minors" annotation sits.
- **Expect to be asked for ID if you look young.** The duty to ask is on the establishment and is triggered by doubt, not by suspicion.
- **A salon and a clinic are not the same counter.** The same tattoo in a medical-aesthetic institution sits under health regulation; in a hairdresser's it sits under the public-place hygiene regime; the Measures name all of them.
- **Removal is treated as a medical-aesthetic service.** The instrument routes it there explicitly, rather than leaving it to the studio that did the work.
- **Nothing here is a hygiene guarantee.** The Measures do not address instruments or technique, so the questions worth asking on the day are the ones you would ask anywhere.
- **Do not expect the age rule to be negotiable.** It binds enterprises, organisations and individuals alike, and the enticement limb is broader than the service limb.
- **Hong Kong and Macau legislate the protection of minors separately**, so neither the prohibition nor the licence annotation describes a studio there.

## Checked for newer, and what I could not read

The Measures were read on **the Hunan provincial civil affairs department's** republication of the issuing notice, and the key passages were cross-checked against **a Wuhan district government's** copy of the same text. The two agree, character for character, on the prohibition, on the conspicuous-position and identity-document duties, on the health department's duty and on the market regulator's licence annotation.

Two things rest on less. **The definition of a "tattoo service provider" — the four-category sentence this page leans on — was read on the Hunan copy only.** And **the article numbering is not settled**: the Wuhan copy numbers the signage duty as Article 5, which is why this page gives that number and no other. The departmental duties are cited here by what they say rather than by a number, because the two copies do not present them the same way.

The document number **国未保办发〔2022〕6号** appears in the title line of the issuing notice as published. **The notice's own file is a PDF, which this environment cannot read**, so the number and the date were not confirmed against the original document.

Two things this page does not attempt. It gives **no prices, no studio names and no assessment of any establishment**. And it says nothing about **whether tattoos affect employment, military service or visa applications**, because none of the material read here addresses that and the claims circulating about it were not verifiable.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can foreigners get a tattoo in China?

Nothing in the Measures restricts customers by nationality. The single restriction they impose is on age: no enterprise, organisation or individual may provide tattoo services to a minor, and a provider must ask for identity documents where it cannot tell whether someone is one.

### Is tattooing regulated in China?

Less than most visitors assume, and not as a trade. The one national instrument on the subject governs who may be tattooed rather than how tattooing is done, and it reaches providers through four existing categories — specialist studios, medical and medical-aesthetic institutions, beauty and hairdressing establishments, and social organisations.

### What is the legal age for a tattoo in China?

Eighteen, in the sense that providing the service to a minor is prohibited outright. The Measures also bar coercing, enticing or inciting a minor into being tattooed, which is wider than the prohibition on providing the service itself.

### Do Chinese tattoo shops have to display anything?

Yes. A provider must state in a conspicuous position that it does not provide tattoo services to minors. Separately, the market regulator writes "except to minors" after the tattoo entry in the business scope on the shop business licence, which itself has to be displayed.

### Are tattoo studios licensed in China?

Not with a permission of their own, on the material read here. Tattooing appears as an entry in the business scope of an ordinary market registration, and the hygiene or health regime that applies depends on what kind of establishment it is rather than on the tattooing.

### Can a minor have a tattoo removed in China?

The Measures point that way deliberately. Health authorities are directed to see that regulated medical-aesthetic services are provided to minors who wish to have a tattoo removed, in the same provision that bars approving tattoo services for minors in the first place.
