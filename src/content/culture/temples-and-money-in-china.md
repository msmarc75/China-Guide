The etiquette side of visiting a Chinese temple is straightforward and this site already sets it out. The money side is not covered anywhere, and it is the half where a visitor can actually lose something.

It is also, unexpectedly, the half with the most law behind it. China has spent the better part of a decade legislating against exactly the encounter a tourist is most likely to have.

## The encounter

It goes like this. Someone at or near a large temple — in robes, or in ordinary clothes and helpful — offers you incense, a blessing, a reading of your face or your palm, or a place at a ceremony. It is free, or nearly. Then a number appears: the incense you have been given is the expensive kind, the blessing needs a donation to be effective, the misfortune just diagnosed needs a remedy that costs.

If that shape feels familiar it is because it is the same one as [the tea ceremony that ends in a four-figure bill](/answers/what-is-the-tea-house-scam-in-china/): a free or trivial hook, a heavy close, and a setting designed so that walking out feels rude. The response is the same too. Walking out is available, and it is the correct move.

## Three rules that cover it

The relevant instruments are separate and worth keeping apart.

**The Regulations on Religious Affairs**, State Council Order No. 686, revised on 14 June 2017 and in force since **1 February 2018**, replacing the 2004 version. Two of its articles matter here:

- **Article 41** — a place that is *not* a registered religious venue may not organise religious activities and **may not accept religious donations.** A stall outside the gate is not the temple.
- **Article 53** — anyone who funds the building of a religious venue gets no ownership, no right of use and **no economic return from it**, and investing in or contract-operating a religious venue, or a large open-air religious statue, is prohibited, as is using religion for commercial promotion.

**The Measures for the Administration of Religious Venues**, issued as Order No. 19 of the religious affairs administration on 9 July 2023 and in force since **1 September 2023**, replacing a 2005 measure. Its Article 45 is the sentence to remember: a venue **may not charge unreasonable fees for providing religious services**. Article 4 separately forbids using a venue's name or influence for commercial promotion or illegal gain.

## The document that names the actual scam

The third instrument is the specific one, and it is unusually blunt.

**Opinions on Further Addressing the Commercialisation of Buddhism and Taoism**, document **国宗发〔2017〕88号** (*guó zōng fā*, the religious affairs administration's general series, No. 88 of 2017), issued on 3 November 2017 by **twelve departments** — religious affairs, publicity, united front, cyberspace, development and reform, **public security**, finance, housing, taxation, **tourism**, the securities regulator and cultural heritage.

Three of its provisions describe the tourist encounter almost exactly:

- **Clergy and tourism workers alike are strictly forbidden**, under any pretext, to induce or coerce visitors and worshippers into **burning high-priced incense** or **drawing fortune sticks**, or to hype and sell the "first incense" and "first bell" of the year.
- **Impersonating Buddhist or Taoist clergy — or employing people to do so — in order to swindle money is expressly prohibited.**
- **Commercial capital may not enter Buddhism or Taoism**, and no organisation or individual may invest in or contract-operate a Buddhist or Taoist venue, whether by shareholding, joint venture, lease or contract.

That last one explains the pattern. Where a temple has been effectively leased to an operator, everything inside it starts behaving like a shop, which is the thing the twelve departments were writing about.

## Clergy are certificated, and the register is online

This is the part worth knowing because it converts a judgement call into a check.

The same document instructs the authorities to see that Buddhist and Taoist bodies handle **the certification of clergy, the issue of certificates, and online lookup of their basic information.** A monk or priest holding an office in a registered venue has a certificate, and that certificate is verifiable.

You are unlikely to stand in a courtyard running a database query. The useful form of the fact is simpler: **being in robes is not a credential, and the person who approaches you outside the gate is the one the rules are about.**

## What this does not change

The temple itself is fine. Nothing here is a reason to skip one.

- **Entry tickets are normal** and fund upkeep, which [the etiquette guide already says](/culture/religion-and-philosophy-in-china/) — a ticket is not the thing being described above.
- **Donations go in the box, in whatever amount you like or none.** The rule that matters is that nobody may set the amount for you.
- **Buy incense inside, from the venue**, if you want to offer it. A stall outside is not the temple, and Article 41 is explicit about who may take a religious donation.
- **Carry small notes.** A merit box is one of the last genuinely cash-only things in China, which is why [the short list of places cash is still needed](/answers/how-much-cash-should-i-bring-to-china/) has temples on it.
- **Decline and keep walking.** The full repertoire of Chinese tourist approaches, and the one rule that defeats most of them, is in [the safety guide](/guides/health-and-safety-in-china/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do you have to pay for incense at a Chinese temple?

No. Ordinary incense is inexpensive and often included, and a donation is whatever you choose to put in the box. Inducing or coercing visitors into burning high-priced incense is expressly prohibited by a 2017 joint document of twelve departments, so a hard sell is the signal to walk away.

### Are the monks at Chinese temples real?

Those holding office at a registered venue are certificated, and the authorities were directed in 2017 to ensure clergy certification, certificates and an online lookup. Impersonating clergy, or employing people to impersonate them, in order to obtain money is separately prohibited. Robes on their own prove nothing.

### Is it a scam when someone offers a blessing at a Chinese temple?

Often, when it happens outside the venue or ends in a price. A place that is not a registered religious venue may not accept religious donations at all, and a venue may not charge unreasonable fees for religious services. Both rules point the same way: an unsolicited approach with a number attached is not how a temple works.

### How much should I donate at a Chinese temple?

Whatever you wish, including nothing. There is no expected amount and nobody is entitled to name one. Put it in the merit box rather than into a hand, and treat anyone who specifies a figure as outside the rules rather than inside the tradition.

### Can a Chinese temple be privately run for profit?

No. Investing in or contract-operating a religious venue is prohibited, commercial capital may not enter Buddhism or Taoism, and someone who funds a venue's construction gains neither ownership nor any economic return from it. Where a site nonetheless behaves like a business, that is the problem the rules were written for.
