Chengdu treats bodily comfort as a civic value, which is why the teahouses are full at three on a Tuesday and why someone will approach your table holding a tuning fork. Two things are worth understanding before you say yes to either: how to tell a legitimate massage business from one trading under the same signage, and what ear cleaning actually involves.

As on the other city pages, no individual premises are named. Massage shops here are undocumented and change hands constantly, and naming them would be guesswork.

## Reading the signage

| Sign | Pinyin | What it indicates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 推拿 | tuīná | Clinical manipulation, the medical tradition |
| 足疗 | zúliáo | Foot treatment — soak, then deep work on feet and calves |
| 足浴 | zúyù | Foot bath, effectively the same |
| 按摩 | ànmó | Massage, unqualified — the least useful sign |
| 盲人按摩 | mángrén ànmó | Blind masseur — the licensing signal, explained below |
| 采耳 | cǎi'ěr | Ear cleaning |

## The licence that gates the business, not just the practitioner

Blind medical massage sits inside a national framework run by the China Disabled Persons' Federation. Practitioners sit a national examination organised by the federation's examination committee, and the certificate it produces does more than permit them to work: it is also required **to apply to open a blind medical-massage establishment**, and without it a practitioner may not perform medical massage at all.

That is a stronger signal than it first appears. A premises advertising licensed blind masseurs has had to satisfy a licensing body to exist in that form — a bar that a business fronting for something else has no reason to clear.

I could not find implementing rules specific to Sichuan; the province appears to administer the national scheme through its prefecture-level disabled persons' federations rather than a separate provincial code. The framework is national, and it applies in Chengdu.

For treatment rather than relaxation, **成都中医药大学附属医院**, also called the Sichuan Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, has a tuina department. It is at **成都市金牛区十二桥路39号**, and its own transport page gives **Lines 2, 4 and 5, Zhongyida Shengyiyuan, exit C** — a station named after the hospital itself.

## Judging a shopfront

**Encouraging:** clear glass and a lit interior with treatment chairs visible; a printed list of treatments and durations where customers can read it; uniformed staff visibly working; a mix of customers by age and sex; ground-floor premises on a commercial street, or inside a mall or hotel; open in daylight as well as after dark.

**Keep walking:** curtained, tinted or mirrored glass with low red lighting; no treatment list on display; someone stationed outside drawing people in; an unmarked door with stairs and no identifiable business above; staff exclusively young women with no visible treatment role; opening hours that begin in the evening.

:::warn Leave if anything else is offered
Sex work is illegal in China and such premises are raided. A foreign passport in the room during a raid means detention and probably deportation, whatever you went in for. Settle what you owe and go without arguing.
:::

## What a session involves

**Foot treatment** keeps you dressed: trousers rolled, feet in a hot herbal basin, then firm work on feet, ankles and calves, often extending to shoulders. Shared room, television on, and painful in places by design.

**Tuina** means changing into loose cotton clothing the shop provides. It is dry and worked through the fabric with thumbs, elbows and forearms — physiotherapy rather than pampering.

If it is too much, **轻一点** (*qīng yīdiǎn*) gets you a lighter touch without any awkwardness.

## Ear cleaning, honestly

Ear cleaning began as a sideline of Sichuan barbers and moved into Chengdu's teahouses, where it became part of the streetscape — practitioners circulating between tables with a tray of tools and a tuning fork whose ring is part of the performance. In 2015 thirty Chengdu ear cleaners jointly applied for intangible cultural heritage recognition, a process requiring mastery of three core techniques.

A session runs several minutes per ear: a headlamp, thin blades and picks, feathered rods spun in the canal, and the tuning fork struck and held against a tool so the vibration carries inside. It is a strange and genuinely pleasant sensation, and it is a real part of the city rather than a novelty invented for visitors.

:::warn The risk is real and has a specific mechanism
Tools are shared between customers. Clumsy technique grazes the ear canal, and the blood and tissue fluid released make an ideal culture medium — while the fungi involved, aspergillus in particular, resist the alcohol-and-heat disinfection these operations typically use. Chinese media have documented visitors developing fungal ear infections after teahouse sessions, including a Beijing doctor. Chinese ENT specialists advise that anyone who genuinely needs wax removed should have it done at a hospital ear, nose and throat department, under an otoscope.
:::

If you want the experience, watch the practitioner work on someone else first, look at how the tools are handled between customers, and stop them if it hurts. If you want your ears cleaned, go to a clinic.

## Practical points

**No tipping**, in massage shops or at teahouse tables.

**Alipay and WeChat Pay** are accepted everywhere, including by ear cleaners working the park.

**Your hotel can book massage for you** and removes the judgement call, at a higher price.

**Decline prepaid membership cards** — you are not coming back next month.
