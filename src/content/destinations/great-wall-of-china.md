Most of what visitors see was built by the Ming dynasty between the 14th and 17th centuries — brick and stone battlements running along ridgelines north of Beijing. Earlier walls, going back to the 7th century BCE, were rammed earth and survive mostly as eroded ridges in the northwest. The often-quoted total length, from a 2012 government survey, is about **21,196 km** across all periods.

Roughly 30% of the Ming wall has disappeared entirely, and only a small fraction is restored and open.

### Mutianyu — the default choice

5.4 km of restored wall on a forested ridge, with 22 watchtowers. It is far less crowded than Badaling, the scenery is better, and it has enough length that walking fifteen minutes in either direction from the cable car leaves you nearly alone.

- **Entry** ¥45, plus ¥15 for the mandatory shuttle from the car park
- **Cable car** ¥120 return / ¥100 one way, or a chairlift
- **Toboggan** down a metal chute, ¥100 — genuinely fun and the standard way to descend
- Walk **left (west) towards tower 20** for the steepest, quietest, most dramatic stretch

**Getting there:** DiDi ¥350–450 each way; a hired car for the day ¥600–800 (the sensible option for two or more); the 916 express bus from Dongzhimen to Huairou then a local minibus; or a small-group tour. Leave central Beijing by 07:00.

### Badaling — skip it unless

The first section restored (1957), directly served by high-speed rail from Beijing North in about 30 minutes, with the most infrastructure and by far the most people. On a public holiday it is shoulder-to-shoulder. It is the right choice only if you have limited mobility (it has the best accessibility) or very limited time.

### Jinshanling — the hiker's section

Partly restored, partly wild, on a high ridge with more than 60 watchtowers and views along kilometres of wall snaking over hills. The **Jinshanling to Simatai** hike (roughly 10 km, 3–4 hours, moderately strenuous) is the classic Great Wall walk, though the eastern end towards Simatai has been closed to through-hikers at various times — check current access before planning it.

Entry ¥65, cable car ¥40. Buses run from Wangjing West in Beijing; most people go with a driver or a small tour.

### Simatai and Gubei Water Town

Simatai is steep, dramatic and requires a timed booking. It is the **only section open at night**, illuminated, which is a genuinely memorable experience. It adjoins **Gubei Water Town**, a large purpose-built resort village — entirely artificial, quite pretty, and a comfortable place to stay overnight in order to do the night wall.

### Jiankou — do not casually go

Completely unrestored: collapsing brick, sheer unprotected drops, sections requiring hands-and-feet scrambling on loose masonry. It is spectacular and it is where the famous photographs come from. It is also officially closed, and people have died there. Go only with an experienced guide, in dry weather, with proper footwear — or take Huanghuacheng instead for a ruined-wall feel with far less risk.

## Practical advice

**Season.** October is the best month — clear air, autumn colour, comfortable temperatures. Late April and May are next. Summer is hot, hazy and busy. Winter is cold and windy but the Wall under snow, nearly empty, is one of the great sights in China.

**Time.** Allow a full day from central Beijing: 1.5–2.5 hours each way plus 3–4 hours on the Wall.

**Physical demands.** The Wall follows ridgelines, so it climbs and drops constantly. Steps are uneven and often very steep — some sections at Mutianyu approach 45 degrees. Proper shoes, water and sun protection are not optional. There is little shade.

**Crowds.** Arrive at opening (07:30–08:30 depending on section) or after 15:00. Avoid weekends, and avoid Golden Week entirely.

**Guides and tours.** A private driver plus your own exploring is usually better value than a group tour. Be firm about avoiding tours that include a "jade factory" or "tea ceremony" stop — those are commission arrangements, not sightseeing.

**Camping and overnight stays on the Wall** are prohibited in most areas and actively policed at popular sections.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which part of the Great Wall is best to visit?

Mutianyu for most people — restored, forested, 1.5 hours from Beijing, with a cable car and a toboggan, and far fewer people than Badaling. Jinshanling is the best section for hiking and photography. Badaling is the most crowded and the least rewarding.

### How far is the Great Wall from Beijing?

Mutianyu and Badaling are both about 70 km from central Beijing, roughly 1.5 hours by road. Jinshanling and Simatai are 120–130 km, about 2.5 hours. All are full-day trips — [the distances section by section](/answers/how-far-is-the-great-wall-from-beijing/) sets out why distance is the wrong thing to choose on.

### How much does it cost to visit the Great Wall?

Mutianyu is ¥45 entry plus ¥15 shuttle and ¥120 for a return cable car. Jinshanling is ¥65 plus ¥40 for the cable car. Transport from Beijing adds ¥350–800 depending on whether you take a DiDi, a hired car or a bus.

### Can you hike the Great Wall?

Yes. The Jinshanling section offers the classic 10 km ridge hike over several hours, and Mutianyu has long walkable stretches. Unrestored sections such as Jiankou are dangerous, officially closed, and have caused fatalities — go there only with an experienced guide, if at all.

### When is the best time to visit the Great Wall?

October, for clear air, autumn colour and comfortable temperatures, followed by late April and May. Winter offers the Wall in snow with almost no visitors, at the cost of severe cold and wind on exposed ridges.

### Can you see the Great Wall from space?

No. The claim is a persistent myth — the Wall is long but only a few metres wide and made of materials the same colour as the surrounding terrain, and it is not visible to the naked eye from low Earth orbit.
