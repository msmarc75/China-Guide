Guizhou appears on thirteen pages of this site and on none of them as somewhere to go. [The ethnic minorities page](/culture/chinas-ethnic-minorities/) recommends it twice — large Miao and Dong populations, far fewer foreign visitors than Yunnan, *which is much of its appeal* — and then points nowhere. [The cuisine guide](/food/chinese-cuisine-regional-guide/) describes its cooking as sour rather than hot and moves on. The drinks guide names it as the home of Moutai. The best-time page puts it in a section heading, **Chengdu, Yunnan, Guizhou**, and then writes about Chengdu and Yunnan.

That is a strange way to treat a province that holds four natural World Heritage properties, or parts of them — a count that made it, when the last of the four was inscribed in 2018, the province with the most natural World Heritage in China.

## Four natural World Heritage sites

Guizhou is where China's natural heritage is concentrated, and the list is the fastest way to convey it.

- **Libo (荔波)**, inscribed as part of **South China Karst** — cone karst, forested, with the Xiaoqikong scenic area at its centre.
- **Shibing (施秉)**, inscribed in a later phase of the same **South China Karst** property — dolomite karst, a different rock and a different shape from Libo.
- **Chishui (赤水)**, inscribed as part of **China Danxia** — red sandstone, waterfalls and bamboo, in the far north near the Sichuan border.
- **Fanjingshan (梵净山)**, inscribed on **2 July 2018** at the 42nd session of the World Heritage Committee in Manama — China's 53rd World Heritage property and its 13th natural one, and Guizhou's first inscribed on its own rather than as part of a serial property.

Fanjingshan is the one to prioritise if you only take one. It is an isolated mountain rising out of subtropical forest, protected for its biodiversity, and the summit stack with a temple on each of its two heads is among the more improbable sights in China.

**Huangguoshu**, west of Anshun, is the waterfall everyone photographs and the one piece of Guizhou that does appear in general guidebooks. It is not World Heritage; it is a heavily developed scenic area, and it is genuinely large. No height or width is given here, because this page states a measurement only when it has read one on an official source.

## The southeast, which is the real reason to come

**Qiandongnan** — the Southeast Guizhou Miao and Dong Autonomous Prefecture — is the part of China that most rewards the effort of getting to it. **Kaili** is the usual base and the rail gateway.

**Xijiang (西江千户苗寨)** is the largest Miao village in China and now a ticketed, heavily visited one; go, but go knowing what it has become. **Zhaoxing (肇兴侗寨)** in Liping county is the Dong equivalent and quieter, built around **drum towers** — the tall, tiered timber structures that are the architectural signature of Dong villages, along with covered wind-and-rain bridges.

:::tip The thing to hear, not just look at
**侗族大歌** (*dòngzú dàgē*, Dong grand song) is a polyphonic choral tradition sung without a conductor and without instruments, with the singers holding several independent parts against each other.

UNESCO inscribed it on the **Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2009**, at the fourth session of the intergovernmental committee, which opened in Abu Dhabi on 28 September that year and approved 76 items, 22 of them Chinese. The nomination was made by Guizhou's provincial culture department together with **Liping county**. It had entered China's own national list in 2006.

It is sung in **Liping, Congjiang and Rongjiang** counties and in adjacent villages across the Guangxi border — which is a usefully precise answer to where you would have to be to hear it in the place it belongs to.
:::

The site's standing advice on visiting minority areas applies here more than anywhere: [the difference between a ticketed performance village and a place where people live](/culture/chinas-ethnic-minorities/) is the whole question, and the festivals are held for the communities themselves rather than scheduled for visitors.

## The telescope that has its own law

In the karst hills of **Pingtang** county sits **FAST**, the five-hundred-metre aperture spherical radio telescope, built into a natural doline. It is the largest filled-aperture radio telescope in the world, and it is the reason Guizhou contains a legally defined zone of radio silence.

The **贵州省500米口径球面射电望远镜电磁波宁静区保护办法** (*Guìzhōu shěng wǔbǎi mǐ kǒujìng qiúmiàn shèdiàn wàngyuǎnjìng diàncíbō níngjìngqū bǎohù bànfǎ*, Measures of Guizhou Province on the Protection of the Electromagnetic Quiet Zone of the 500-metre Aperture Spherical Radio Telescope) were promulgated as **provincial government Order No. 188** on **3 January 2019**, took effect on **1 April 2019**, and replaced an earlier order on the same subject.

**Article 2** draws the map. Measured from the telescope at **25°39′10″ north, 106°51′20″ east**, the quiet zone is three concentric rings: a **core zone within 5 kilometres**, a **middle zone from 5 to 10 kilometres**, and a **remote zone from 10 to 30 kilometres**.

:::warn What Article 7 means for you at the gate
The prohibition on entering the core zone is specific about the objects:

> 禁止擅自携带手机、数码相机、平板电脑、智能穿戴设备、对讲机、无人机等无线电发射设备或者产生电磁辐射的电子产品

**Mobile phones, digital cameras, tablets, smart wearables, walkie-talkies, drones** and other radio-transmitting devices or electronics producing electromagnetic radiation may not be taken in without authorisation.

So the storage lockers at the visitor centre are not a museum quirk and not a photography policy. They are a provincial regulation about radio astronomy, and the smartwatch counts. Plan for a visit during which you will have no phone, no camera and no way to check anything — and settle what you need to before you set off.
:::

The zone also governs radio stations, transmitters, high-voltage substations and power lines, airports, electrified railways and roads within its rings, which is why the landscape around the telescope is emptier than the rest of the province.

## Moutai comes from a town you can find on a map

**茅台镇** (*Máotái zhèn*, Maotai town) sits in Renhuai, under Zunyi, in the north of the province, and it is the whole of the protected origin for China's most famous spirit — [the sauce-aroma baijiu the drinks guide calls the emblem of the category](/food/chinese-tea-and-drinks/).

The name is protected on both sides of the world. On the Chinese side, the geographical indication was approved in 2001 and has carried a national standard ever since; **GB/T 18356-2026** was released on **28 January 2026** and takes effect on **1 August 2026**, fully replacing the 2007 version and adding requirements on the production environment and the brewing method. Note the letters: the 2001 standard was **GB**, compulsory; what stands now is **GB/T**, recommended — [the same change of force this site set out for the wine standards](/food/chinese-wine/), with the number carried across unchanged.

On the European side it is registered as a **PGI** under the China–EU agreement on geographical indications, in the spirits category, with **the Guizhou provincial government as the applicant** — the same instrument that protects [Chinese coffee origins](/food/coffee-in-china/) and [the Helan foothills wine region](/food/chinese-wine/).

Visiting the town is a distillery-landscape day rather than a tasting tour, and this page does not describe any producer's visitor arrangements.

## Eating in Guizhou

Sour, not hot — the distinction [the cuisine guide draws](/food/chinese-cuisine-regional-guide/) and the single most useful thing to know before you sit down. **酸汤鱼** (*suāntāng yú*, sour-soup fish) is the dish the province is built on: a broth soured with fermented tomato and rice water rather than vinegar, with fish cooked in it at the table. It is also [one of the regional hotpots worth crossing the country for](/food/hotpot-guide/).

Fermentation runs through the rest of it — pickled vegetables, sour chilli, glutinous rice. Heat is present but it is never the point.

## Practicalities

- **Days needed:** six to nine nights to do the southeast villages and one or two of the natural sites without rushing. Three nights covers Guiyang, Huangguoshu and a single village.
- **Getting there:** Guiyang is a high-speed rail hub with direct services from Guangzhou, Chengdu, Chongqing, Changsha and Kunming, and Kaili is on the line east. This is the part that has changed most in the last decade, and older guidance describing Guizhou as hard to reach is out of date.
- **Getting around:** the province is mountainous and the villages are not all on the railway, so expect buses, taxis and time. Distances that look short take longer than they should.
- **Season:** the plateau is temperate and humid, and Guizhou has a reputation for cloud. Spring and autumn are the usual recommendations, as they are for [the rest of the southwest](/plan/best-time-to-visit-china/).
- **Language:** Mandarin works in the cities. In the villages it is a second language for many people, and Miao and Dong are separate languages rather than dialects — [which is the pattern across China's minority regions](/answers/is-mandarin-spoken-everywhere-in-china/).
- **Pair it with Yunnan or Sichuan.** Guizhou sits between them and takes far fewer foreign visitors than either, which is the argument for going now.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Guizhou worth visiting?

For anyone who wants minority China without Yunnan's crowds, yes. It holds four natural World Heritage sites — more than any other Chinese province — the Miao and Dong heartland in the southeast, and a cuisine unlike anything served under the name Chinese food abroad. It also takes far fewer foreign visitors than Yunnan, which is the point of it for a lot of people.

### What is there to see in Guizhou?

Fanjingshan, inscribed by UNESCO in 2018; the karst of Libo and Shibing and the red sandstone of Chishui, all World Heritage; Huangguoshu waterfall near Anshun; the Miao and Dong villages of the southeast around Kaili; the FAST radio telescope at Pingtang; and Maotai town in the north.

### Can you visit the China Sky Eye telescope?

There is a visitor operation, but it sits inside a legally defined electromagnetic quiet zone. Under Guizhou provincial Order No. 188, in force since 1 April 2019, mobile phones, digital cameras, tablets, smart wearables, walkie-talkies and drones may not be taken into the core zone within five kilometres of the telescope without authorisation. Plan on surrendering all of it.

### Where is Moutai made?

In Maotai town, in Renhuai city under Zunyi, in northern Guizhou. That town is the protected geographical origin: the name is a Chinese geographical indication with its own national standard, and it is registered as a protected geographical indication in the European Union under the China–EU agreement, with the Guizhou provincial government as the applicant.

### Is Guizhou food spicy?

It is intense in a different direction. Guizhou cooking pushes into sourness and fermentation rather than raw chilli heat — sour-soup fish, built on fermented tomato and rice water, is the emblem. Chilli is present, but it is not what the cooking is organised around.

### How do I get to the Miao and Dong villages?

Kaili, in the southeast Guizhou Miao and Dong Autonomous Prefecture, is the usual base and is reachable by rail. From there the villages are road journeys of varying length — Xijiang is the largest Miao village and the most visited, Zhaoxing in Liping county is the Dong equivalent and quieter.
