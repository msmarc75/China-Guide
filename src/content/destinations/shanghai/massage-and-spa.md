An hour of foot massage after a day walking the Concession is one of the better decisions available in Shanghai. Choosing where is the part worth thinking about, because the density of options is enormous and the quality — and the nature — of them varies wildly. Naming individual shops would be irresponsible: they change hands constantly and cannot be verified from a distance. Reading the street can be taught.

## The vocabulary on the sign

## Shanghai licenses this properly

Blind massage is a regulated profession here, and Shanghai's framework is unusually specific. The municipal Disabled Persons' Federation issues the medical-massage practice certificate, **and it is reviewed and re-registered every two years** rather than granted once. Opening a blind health-massage establishment goes through its own approval process, and the city runs national-level standardised training bases for practitioners.

The practical consequence for a visitor: a shopfront advertising blind masseurs is operating inside a licensing regime that a front for something else has no reason to enter. It is the single most reliable signal on the street, and the practitioners tend to be very good.

For the clinical end, **上海中医药大学附属岳阳中西医结合医院** — the Yueyang Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine — runs a tuina department recognised nationally as a regional centre for the discipline. Its Dabaishu campus is at **上海市虹口区甘河路110号**, on **Line 3, Dabaishu**. That is a hospital appointment rather than an afternoon treat, but if you have an actual back problem it is the address that matters.

## Reading the frontage

**Encouraging:**

- You can see into the room from the pavement — lights on, chairs visible, nothing obscured
- A printed treatment list with durations displayed where customers can read it
- Staff in uniform, visibly working rather than waiting
- Customers of both sexes and a range of ages
- Ground floor on a commercial street, or inside a mall or hotel
- Trading during the day, not only after dark

**Keep walking:**

- Tinted, curtained or mirrored glass; low pink or red lighting
- No treatment list anywhere in view
- Someone outside inviting passers-by in
- An unlabelled door with stairs and no visible business above
- Staffed exclusively by young women with no evident treatment function
- Opening only in the evening

:::warn Walk out if anything else is offered
Sex work is illegal and these premises get raided. A foreign passport in the room during a raid means detention and very likely deportation, whatever you had actually gone in for. Settle anything already owed and leave without discussion.
:::

## What you are booking

**Foot treatment.** Clothes stay on. Trousers roll up, feet go into a hot herbal basin, and the therapist then works the feet, ankles and calves — often continuing to shoulders and neck. Reclining chairs, usually several people to a room, television on. It hurts in places, which is the intended effect.

**Body work.** You change into loose cotton clothing the shop provides. Tuina is dry and firm, applied through the fabric with thumbs, elbows and forearms rather than oil. It is closer to physiotherapy than to a Swedish massage.

Cupping (拔罐) and gua sha (刮痧) are usually offered alongside. Both leave visible marks for days — decline if that matters to you this week.

Ask for less pressure with **轻一点** (*qīng yīdiǎn*). It is a normal request, not a complaint.

## Practical points

**Tipping is not done.** Do not leave anything extra.

**Phone payment everywhere.** Alipay and WeChat Pay are universal, including at small independents.

**Hotels are the shortcut.** Shanghai's hotels are good and their spas or concierge recommendations remove the judgement call entirely, at a higher price.

**Say no to the card.** Chains push prepaid packages hard. Pay for the one session; you are leaving the country.
