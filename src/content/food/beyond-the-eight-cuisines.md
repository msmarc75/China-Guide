The [regional cuisine guide](/food/chinese-cuisine-regional-guide/) covers the eight great traditions and what to order in each. This page is the layer above it: where that scheme came from, why it misleads, and what to use instead when you are standing in a restaurant in a province you have never visited.

## The eight great cuisines are younger than the Spring Festival Gala

The **eight great cuisines (八大菜系)** are presented almost everywhere — including, until now, on this site — as an old classification, often attributed to the Qing dynasty. The evidence does not support that.

The word **菜系** itself has no pre-modern record. It appears to have entered use in the 1950s and 1960s, and is commonly credited to Yao Yilin, then Minister of Commerce, as a term coined to describe China's regional styles to foreign visitors. When the Ministry of Commerce compiled a national recipe collection in the 1970s, it proposed **four** cuisines: Shandong, Sichuan, Huaiyang and Cantonese.

The eight-cuisine list reached print later still. The earliest known published use is an article titled *Our Country's Eight Great Cuisines* by Wang Shaoquan, in *People's Daily* on **20 June 1980**. Through the 1980s the four-cuisine version remained the official one, used at China's first national cooking competition in 1983 and at the founding of the China Cuisine Association in 1987.

:::note Why the number kept changing
Once the idea of a canonical list existed, four, five, eight and ten all circulated at once. None of the versions matches [what Chinese food looks like abroad](/answers/is-chinese-food-in-china-different-from-chinese-food-abroad/), which descends from one corner of one province. Inclusion was commercially valuable: being named a great cuisine was excellent advertising for a province's restaurant industry at exactly the moment a market economy was opening up.

That is worth knowing not to debunk anything — the eight are real traditions and Chinese people do use the shorthand — but because it explains the list's shape. It is a product of provincial-level lobbying and journalism, not a survey of how China eats. The northwest, the northeast, Yunnan, Guizhou and every minority tradition are absent, and they are not marginal.
:::

We have corrected our own regional guide accordingly.

## The line that actually predicts your dinner

If you want one fact that tells you more about a Chinese meal than the eight cuisines do, it is the **Qinling–Huai line (秦岭—淮河一线)** — the mountain range and river that Chinese geography treats as the boundary between north and south.

It is a real boundary and an unusually crisp one. It approximates the January zero-degree isotherm, the 800-millimetre annual rainfall line, and the division between dry-field and paddy agriculture. North of it the staple crop is wheat; south of it, rice.

That single division shapes more of your eating than any provincial label:

- **North of the line**, the staple arriving with your meal is wheat — noodles, steamed bread, filled buns, pancakes. Dumplings are a meal, not a starter.
- **South of the line**, it is rice, and the rice usually arrives last, after the dishes, as a filler rather than a foundation.

Ordering a full table of dishes in the north and then being surprised by a basket of steamed bread is a common visitor mistake. So is waiting for rice in Guangzhou and discovering the meal was designed to be complete without it.

## Order by cooking method, not just by dish

Menus are grouped by category — cold dishes, hot dishes, staples — and the [ordering guide](/food/how-to-order-food-in-china/) covers reading those. The more useful skill is recognising the **cooking-method character** in a dish name, because it tells you what will arrive far more reliably than the ingredient does.

| Character | Method | What arrives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 炒 | Stir-fry over high heat | Quick, dry-ish, the default |
| 爆 | Very fast, very hot | Crisp-tender, minimal sauce |
| 烧 | Seared, then simmered with liquid | Sauced; 红烧 is the soy-and-sugar version |
| 焖 | Covered braise, low and long | Soft, deeply flavoured, little liquid left |
| 炖 | Simmered with plenty of liquid | Soupy — closer to a stew |
| 蒸 | Steamed | Clean, unsauced, ingredient-forward |
| 干煸 | Dry-fried until moisture goes | Chewy, concentrated, Sichuan speciality |

With those seven characters you can order a balanced table in a cuisine you know nothing about: pick something 炒 or 爆, something 烧 or 焖, something 蒸, and a vegetable. You will not get four versions of the same texture, which is the actual failure mode of ordering blind.

## What each tradition is built on

The eight-cuisine descriptions tend to list dishes. What distinguishes the traditions is more usefully described as a flavour logic.

**Sichuan** is built on **numbing-spicy (麻辣)** — the combination of chilli heat with the tingling anaesthesia of the Sichuan peppercorn. The numbing half is the part visitors do not expect, and it is not the same sensation as heat. Sichuan cooking is also far broader than its spicy reputation; a properly ordered Sichuan table includes several dishes with no chilli in them at all.

**Hunan** is hot without the numbing — a drier, sharper, more direct chilli heat, often with fermented and pickled elements. Hunanese people will tell you theirs is the hotter of the two, and on the pure-chilli axis they have a case. The [Changsha guide](/destinations/changsha/) goes into this.

**Cantonese** is built on restraint and on **鲜 (xiān)** — a quality somewhere between freshness and savouriness that has no clean English equivalent. The aim is to taste the ingredient, which is why steaming and blanching dominate and why seasoning is light. Visitors who arrive expecting bold flavour sometimes read this as bland; it is the opposite of bland, but it is quiet.

**Jiangnan cooking** — the Jiangsu and Zhejiang traditions around the lower Yangtze — uses noticeably more sugar than the rest of China, in savoury dishes, deliberately. Shanghai's red-braised dishes taste sweet because they are meant to.

**The 清真 (halal) tradition** runs across the northwest and into every large city, cooked by Hui and Uyghur communities. Lamb and beef, wheat, cumin, no pork. It is the single most widespread food culture the eight-cuisine list ignores completely, and in cities like Xi'an, Lanzhou and Yinchuan it is not a minority option but the main event. The communities behind it are covered in [China's ethnic minorities](/culture/chinas-ethnic-minorities/), and [eating this way as a Muslim visitor](/guides/halal-travel-in-china/) turns on judging the establishment rather than the label.

:::warn Check what a dish is before assuming
English food writing about China carries a lot of inherited error. Three examples this site has had to correct: the Sichuan cold dish 夫妻肺片 contains no lung despite its name; classic 麻婆豆腐 is made with beef, not pork; and a famous Chengdu bar street that English guides place on a road called 玉林路 is not where they say it is.

The practical version for a traveller: in a halal restaurant, no dish contains pork whatever an English menu says, and a dish named after an animal part is not necessarily made from it.
:::

## How a Chinese meal is actually structured

There are no courses in the Western sense. Cold dishes arrive first, then hot dishes as they are ready, and the staple towards the end. Everything is shared from the centre of the table.

This has consequences worth knowing:

- **Dishes arrive when they are cooked**, not in a designed sequence. A dish landing early is not a mistake.
- **Nobody orders "a main"**. You order for the table, roughly one dish per person plus one, and you eat across all of them.
- **Soup placement varies by region** — in much of the south it comes late, in the north it may arrive with everything else.
- **Eating alone is well catered for** in noodle and rice-bowl shops, which are a different category of restaurant entirely, not a lesser version of the same one.

For the mechanics — what to say, how to pay, what the restaurant types are — see the [ordering guide](/food/how-to-order-food-in-china/). For eating standing up, see [street food](/food/chinese-street-food/), and for the one format that breaks all these rules, the [hotpot guide](/food/hotpot-guide/).

## The dish nearly every visitor under-orders

Vegetables in China are a dish, not a side. A vegetable arrives stir-fried with garlic, or blanched with oyster sauce, or dry-fried, in its own bowl, in the same quantity as the meat — and it is frequently the best thing on the table.

Foreign diners consistently under-order them, partly because Chinese restaurant menus abroad treat vegetables as accompaniments and partly because the meat dishes read as the point. Adding one green vegetable to any table you order will improve it, and in a cuisine you do not know it is the safest single item on the menu.

Seasonality is taken seriously alongside this. Asking what is good right now (**时令菜**, seasonal dishes) is a normal question and usually gets a real answer rather than an upsell, particularly outside tourist areas. In spring that might mean bamboo shoots; in summer, in Yunnan, it means wild mushrooms, which the province treats as an annual event.

## The same cuisine is not the same in two cities

A useful thing to expect: regional cuisines localise. Sichuan food in Shanghai is sweeter and less numbing than in Chengdu. Cantonese food in Beijing is heavier. Lanzhou beef noodles outside Lanzhou are frequently a franchise product with little relation to the city's own shops.

The Lanzhou case has a specific explanation, and it is not franchising in the ordinary sense: [most of those shops are run by Hui families from Qinghai](/food/chinese-noodles/) rather than by anyone from Lanzhou, trading under the city name because it is the one customers know.

This is not adulteration so much as the normal behaviour of food moving. But it means the strongest single rule for eating well in China is geographic rather than gastronomic: eat what the place you are standing in is known for. The reason the [Chengdu](/destinations/chengdu/), [Xi'an](/destinations/xian/) and [Guangzhou](/destinations/guangzhou/) guides each have their own food sections is that those three cities are eating genuinely different food.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are the eight great Chinese cuisines an ancient classification?

No. The word for a culinary system (菜系) has no pre-modern record and appears to date from the 1950s or 1960s. A four-cuisine scheme came first, in the 1970s. The earliest known published use of the eight-cuisine list is a *People's Daily* article of 20 June 1980, and the four-cuisine version remained official through much of that decade.

### What is the wheat–rice line in China?

The Qinling–Huai line, running along the Qinling mountains and the Huai river, is the conventional boundary between northern and southern China. It approximates the January zero-degree isotherm and the 800-millimetre rainfall line, and separates wheat-growing dry fields from rice paddies — which is why the staple on your table changes when you cross it.

### How do I order Chinese food in a cuisine I don't know?

Order by cooking method rather than by ingredient. Choose one stir-fried or flash-fried dish (炒 or 爆), one braised (烧 or 焖), one steamed (蒸) and a vegetable. That gives you different textures and intensities rather than four variations on the same thing.

### What is the difference between Sichuan and Hunan spice?

Sichuan combines chilli heat with the numbing tingle of the Sichuan peppercorn — 麻辣, numbing-spicy. Hunan heat is drier and more direct, chilli without the numbing element, often with fermented and pickled notes. [Which of the two is actually hotter](/answers/what-is-the-difference-between-sichuan-and-hunan-spice/) settles it on capsaicin rather than reputation, and covers how to order around either.

### Is Cantonese food bland?

No, but it is deliberately restrained. The tradition prizes 鲜, a quality between freshness and savouriness, and uses steaming and blanching so the ingredient itself is what you taste. Visitors expecting the intensity of Sichuan or Hunan food sometimes misread that as a lack of flavour.

### Which Chinese cuisines are missing from the eight?

The northeast, the northwest, Yunnan, Guizhou, Shaanxi, Chaoshan and every ethnic minority tradition, including the halal cooking found across the northwest and in every major city. The list reflects provincial-level advocacy in the 1980s rather than a survey of what China eats.
