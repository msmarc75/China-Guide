Almost every Chinese neighbourhood has a covered produce market, and almost no visitor goes into one. It is not a sight and nobody will suggest it, which is exactly why it is worth twenty minutes: it is the most ordinary room in China, and it is full of things that turn out to be governed by rules you can use.

The site sends you to [morning stalls rather than night markets](/food/chinese-street-food/) for street food, which is right. This is the third thing — not stalls, not a night market, but the hall where the stalls buy from and where everyone else shops.

## The scale on the plinth

Look for it near the entrance or in the centre of the aisle: a scale, on its own stand, with nobody selling anything beside it. Visitors read it as a stray piece of equipment. It is the **公平秤** (*gōng píng chèng*, fair scale), and it is there because a national rule puts it there.

The current instrument is the **Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Measurement in Markets**, SAMR Order No. 94, promulgated on 4 December 2024 and **in force since 1 March 2025**, replacing a 2002 predecessor. Article 5, sixth item, sets out what the market operator must do:

> 合理设置用于公平复核的公平秤、公平尺等计量器具，摆放在显著、便捷位置，并予以标识；对用于公平复核的计量器具负责保管、维护和监督检查，保证量值准确。

The operator must **reasonably provide fair scales and fair measures for re-checking, place them somewhere prominent and convenient, and label them** — and must keep, maintain and supervise them so that the readings are accurate.

Article 16 defines what those instruments are for in one line: they are the measuring devices that **settle a dispute** between a trader and a customer about the quantity of goods.

So it is not decoration and it is not for staff. It is a public instrument, provided for you, and using it is the intended behaviour rather than an accusation.

## What happens if the weight is short

This is the part that changes how you shop, because the usual assumption — that a tourist has no recourse — is wrong here.

Article 12 says that where goods are **kept in their original state** and a re-check finds them short, the customer may claim compensation from the trader. If the trader's tenancy in the market has ended, the customer **may claim from the market operator instead**, and the operator then recovers from the trader.

Keep the phrase *in their original state* in mind, because it is the condition that makes the right usable: re-weigh before you unpack anything, not after you get home.

Where a rigged instrument was used to defraud, Article 12 sends you to Article 55 of the Consumer Rights Protection Law rather than settling the amount itself.

## What the trader has to show you

Two duties in Article 9 are worth knowing because they tell you what a correct transaction looks like.

A trader who settles by quantity **must actually measure it with an instrument** — the price cannot simply be asserted for a bag someone hefted. And in a face-to-face sale the trader must **make the unit of measurement, the weighing itself and the resulting figure visible to you.**

That is the market's own version of the duty that runs through this whole site: [the price must be marked and nothing may be added beyond it](/guides/marked-prices-in-china/). In a produce hall the same logic lands one step earlier, on the quantity rather than the price, because the quantity is what the price is multiplied by.

Practically, it means the display should be turned towards you. If it is turned away, that is not shyness.

## The price is for half a kilo

Here is the single most useful thing on this page, and it catches out nearly everybody.

Prices in a Chinese food market are quoted per **斤** (*jīn*), which is **500 grams**, not per kilogram. A sign reading 12 means twelve yuan for half a kilo. Visitors who mentally convert to kilograms halve the price and then wonder why the total is double what they expected.

The oddity is that this unit was supposed to be gone. The State Council's 1984 command unifying legal units of measurement allowed the old market units to continue **in everyday life until 1990**, with the transition to be completed by the end of that year. Four decades later the entire fresh-food trade still quotes in jin, and Article 9 requires the trader to display the unit precisely because of cases like this one.

Two numbers therefore matter at a stall: the figure on the sign, and whether it is followed by 斤 or 公斤 (*gōng jīn*, kilogram).

## The lamps you will not see

Older accounts of Chinese meat counters describe a deep red glow that made everything look fresher than it was. Those lamps are gone, and the reason is a dated rule rather than a change in taste.

The **Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Quality and Safety in the Market Sale of Edible Agricultural Products**, SAMR Order No. 81, took effect on **1 December 2023** and abrogated the 2016 order it replaced. The second paragraph of Article 7 is the operative sentence:

> 销售生鲜食用农产品，不得使用对食用农产品的真实色泽等感官性状造成明显改变的照明等设施误导消费者对商品的感官认知。

Selling fresh produce, a trader **may not use lighting or similar fixtures that markedly alter the true colour or other sensory characteristics of the goods** so as to mislead the customer about what they are looking at.

Article 38 backs it: an order to correct and a warning, and a fine of between ¥5,000 and ¥30,000 for refusing to correct.

The consequence for you is small and real. **The meat and fish in front of you are the colour they actually are**, so the ordinary test — colour, firmness, smell — works the way it does at home. That is a better guarantee than a guess about which stall looks cleanest, and it is the same instinct as [reading the posted hygiene grade in a restaurant](/food/restaurant-hygiene-ratings-in-china/) instead of judging by the frontage.

## How to actually use one

- **Go in the morning.** The hall is a working supply chain and the best of it moves early; by late afternoon you are choosing from what is left.
- **Take the loop first.** Walk the whole hall before buying anything. Prices for the same thing vary stall to stall and you cannot tell until you have seen three.
- **Point and hold up fingers.** No language is required. The trader bags it, weighs it, shows you a figure and you pay by QR code like everywhere else.
- **Watch the display, not the bag.** The screen faces you or it does not; that is the whole test.
- **Buy cooked food too.** Most halls have a corner of prepared dishes, pickles, fresh noodles and tofu made on site — often the cheapest good meal in the neighbourhood, and it needs no more ordering skill than [pointing at what you can see](/food/how-to-order-food-in-china/).
- **Re-weigh if something feels light**, before you open the bag. It costs nothing and nobody minds.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a Chinese wet market?

The everyday neighbourhood food market — a covered hall of independent stalls selling vegetables, fruit, meat, fish, tofu, eggs, noodles and pickles, usually open every morning. It is where households and small restaurants buy fresh food, and it is an ordinary and entirely unremarkable place to walk into.

### Are food market prices in China per kilo?

No — they are quoted per 斤 (jīn), which is 500 grams. A sign showing 12 means ¥12 for half a kilogram, so the price per kilogram is double the number displayed. Traders are required to make the unit of measurement visible, so check whether the sign says 斤 or 公斤.

### What is the scale on a stand in a Chinese market?

A fair scale, provided so that customers can re-check a purchase. Market operators are required to place instruments for fair re-checking in a prominent, convenient position, label them, and keep them accurate. Using one is normal, not confrontational.

### What if I am short-changed on weight in a Chinese market?

If the goods are still in their original state and a re-check shows short weight, you can claim compensation from the trader, and from the market operator if the trader's tenancy has ended — the operator then recovers it from the trader. Re-weigh before you unpack anything.

### Can I haggle in a Chinese food market?

Not really on the marked price, which is what it is, though traders commonly round a total down or add a handful of herbs or spring onions. The negotiation in a produce hall is about quantity and quality rather than about beating the price down.

### Why do Chinese markets no longer use red lights over the meat?

Because lighting that markedly changes the true colour of fresh produce has been prohibited since December 2023, with a fine for traders who refuse to stop. It means the meat and fish you are looking at are the colour they really are.
