There is no such thing as a Chinese noodle in the way there is an Italian pasta shape. What there is instead is a set of local traditions that share a technique and agree on almost nothing else — and the first thing to know is that the difference between them is usually the flour, not the sauce.

## Wheat above, rice below

The staple map does most of the sorting for you. North of the Qinling–Huai line the grain is wheat, so the noodle is wheat: pulled, cut, slapped or rolled, and usually called **面** (*miàn*). South of it the grain is rice, so the noodle is rice: **米粉** (*mǐfěn*) in most of the south, **河粉** (*héfěn*) for the wide flat sheets in Guangdong.

That line is the single most useful fact about eating in China and [it already has its own explanation here](/food/beyond-the-eight-cuisines/) — it predicts your staple, your dumpling and your bread as well as your noodle. The practical consequence for noodles alone: ordering "noodles" in Guilin and expecting the Xi'an thing will disappoint you, and the failure is agricultural rather than culinary.

## The question at the counter

This is the part nobody warns visitors about. In a Lanzhou-style beef noodle shop you order at a window, and **you are asked what width you want** — the dough is pulled to order and the puller waits for an answer. A visitor who does not know it is a question tends to get whatever the queue behind them prefers.

| What you say | What arrives |
| --- | --- |
| **毛细** (*máo xì*) | Hair-thin, cooks in seconds, soaks up the most broth |
| **细** (*xì*) | Thin |
| **二细** (*èr xì*) | The common middle, and the safe answer if you have no view |
| **三细** (*sān xì*) | A little thicker than the middle |
| **韭叶** (*jiǔ yè*) | "Chive leaf" — flat and narrow, like a slim tagliatelle |
| **薄宽** (*báo kuān*) | Wide and thin |
| **大宽** (*dà kuān*) | Belt-wide, chewy, the most work to eat |

Say **二细** and you will be understood everywhere. The finer cuts carry more broth and go soft faster; the wide cuts stay chewy and suit a slow bowl.

## How Lanzhou judges a bowl

Lanzhou has a five-point formula for what a correct bowl looks like, and once you know it you cannot unsee it: **一清二白三红四绿五黄** (*yī qīng èr bái sān hóng sì lǜ wǔ huáng*).

- **One, clear** — the beef broth is clean and transparent, not cloudy.
- **Two, white** — slices of white radish in it.
- **Three, red** — chilli oil, bright rather than muddy.
- **Four, green** — coriander and garlic sprout on top.
- **Five, yellow** — the noodles themselves, faintly yellow and glossy.

It is a description, not a recipe, and it is the fastest way to judge a shop you have never been to before.

:::warn In Lanzhou it is beef noodle, not pulled noodle
In the city the dish is called **兰州牛肉面** (*Lánzhōu niúròu miàn*), Lanzhou **beef** noodle. "Lanzhou lamian" — 兰州拉面, Lanzhou *pulled* noodle — is what the rest of the country calls it, and the distinction turns out to matter for the reason below.
:::

## The Lanzhou noodle shop that is not from Lanzhou

Our own regional guide notes in passing that [Lanzhou beef noodles outside Lanzhou are frequently a franchise product](/food/beyond-the-eight-cuisines/) with little relation to the city's own shops. That is true, and the reason is one of the more remarkable internal migration stories in modern China.

The green-signed "兰州拉面" shops on high streets in every Chinese city are, overwhelmingly, **not run by people from Lanzhou**. They are run by Hui people from **Hualong**, a county in Qinghai, several hundred kilometres away. Hualong was designated a national poverty-alleviation priority county in 2001, and noodle shops became the county's export industry: state media reported in December 2024 that some **113,000 people from Hualong were running about 18,000 noodle shops across 337 Chinese cities**, up from around 17,000 shops in 271 cities in 2019. By 2019 the county's "noodle economy" was reported at over ¥10 billion a year.

They adopted the Lanzhou name because it was the one customers recognised. The result is a national chain that is not a chain, trading under a city that is not its own — and it is why the bowl in Shanghai tastes different from the bowl in Lanzhou, and why the bowl in Lanzhou is worth the detour if the Silk Road route takes you through.

None of that makes the Qinghai version bad. It makes it a different dish with a borrowed name, which is worth knowing before you conclude that Lanzhou noodles are overrated.

## The ones worth crossing a city for

Most are already covered where they belong, which is with the city that makes them:

- **Biangbiang noodles** in [Xi'an](/destinations/xian/) — hand-slapped belts of dough, written with one of the most complicated characters in common use.
- **Crossing-the-bridge noodles** in [Yunnan](/destinations/yunnan/) — rice noodles cooked at the table in a bowl of broth kept scalding under a film of oil.
- **Hot dry noodles** in [Wuhan](/destinations/wuhan/) and **xiaomian** in [Chongqing](/destinations/chongqing/) — both breakfast, both eaten standing up, both the city's own.
- **Rice noodles** in [Guilin](/destinations/guilin-yangshuo/), which are the local breakfast rather than a tourist dish.

The rule from [the street food guide](/food/chinese-street-food/) applies here more than anywhere: a shop that makes one noodle will beat a restaurant with a long menu, every time.

## Frequently asked questions

### What width of noodle should I order in China?

Say 二细 (*èr xì*), the common middle width — it is understood in any Lanzhou-style beef noodle shop and suits most people. Finer cuts like 毛细 carry more broth and soften quickly; wider cuts like 大宽 stay chewy. The dough is pulled to order, so the question is real and someone is waiting for your answer.

### Why are Lanzhou noodle shops not from Lanzhou?

Because most of them are run by Hui families from Hualong county in Qinghai, several hundred kilometres from Lanzhou, who adopted the better-known city name. State media reported in December 2024 that around 113,000 people from Hualong ran roughly 18,000 shops across 337 Chinese cities.

### What is the difference between Chinese wheat and rice noodles?

Geography, mostly. North of the Qinling–Huai line the staple crop is wheat, so noodles are wheat-based and called 面; south of it the crop is rice, so they are rice-based — 米粉, or 河粉 for the wide flat Cantonese sheets. The texture difference follows from the flour rather than from the cooking.

### How do you tell a good bowl of Lanzhou beef noodles?

By Lanzhou's own five-point description: clear broth, white radish, red chilli oil, green coriander and garlic sprout, and faintly yellow glossy noodles — 一清二白三红四绿五黄. A cloudy broth or muddy chilli oil is the quickest sign of a bowl that is not trying.

### Is Lanzhou beef noodle the same as Lanzhou lamian?

They are the same dish under two names, and the names signal different things. In Lanzhou it is 兰州牛肉面, beef noodle. Elsewhere in China the sign says 兰州拉面, pulled noodle — and that sign usually belongs to a Qinghai family rather than a Lanzhou one.

### What noodles should I try in China?

Start with whatever the city you are in is known for rather than with a national list: biangbiang in Xi'an, crossing-the-bridge in Yunnan, hot dry noodles in Wuhan, xiaomian in Chongqing, rice noodles in Guilin. A shop that makes one noodle and nothing else is almost always the better choice.
