Two pages here touch Chinese wine and neither finishes the thought. [The drinks guide](/food/chinese-tea-and-drinks/) gives it two sentences — a real and rapidly improving industry, centred on Ningxia, and the good bottles are genuinely good — and moves on to huangjiu. [The Yinchuan guide](/destinations/yinchuan/) is better: it calls the eastern foothills of the Helan mountains **a designated wine region** and says the estates are a reason to come.

Designated by whom, though? That is the half nobody explains, and it turns out to be the interesting half.

## Designated by a national standard written for one region

China has a category of national standard that exists to describe a single named product from a single named place. Wine from the Helan foothills has one.

**GB/T 19504-2008**, *地理标志产品 贺兰山东麓葡萄酒* (Geographical indication product — Wine of the eastern foothills of the Helan mountains), was published on **31 July 2008** and took effect on **1 November 2008**. It sets out the protected area, the permitted grape varieties, the product classes — red, white, rosé and sparkling — and the physical and chemical requirements a wine must meet to be sold under that name. The national standards catalogue records it as **current**, last reviewed on **4 August 2025**.

It replaced an earlier standard, and the two changes between them tell you how Chinese law on this moved:

- **The name of the category changed.** The 2004 version was headed 原产地域产品 — "product of a place of origin". The 2008 version is headed 地理标志产品 — "geographical indication product", the international term.
- **It stopped being compulsory.** The 2004 standard was mandatory. The 2008 one is recommended, which is what the **/T** in the code means.

:::note What GB and GB/T actually mean, since they are everywhere in China
**GB** is a compulsory national standard; **GB/T** is a recommended one. The difference is not cosmetic and it moves: on **23 March 2017** the standards administration published two announcements at once, one abolishing 396 compulsory national standards and one converting 1,077 more into recommended standards, in these terms — 自公布之日起，上述标准不再强制执行，标准代号由GB改为GB/T，标准顺序号和年代号不变 — *from the date of publication these standards are no longer compulsory; the code changes from GB to GB/T, and the number and year stay the same.*

So a Chinese standard can keep its number and quietly change its force. The general national standard for wine, **GB/T 15037-2006**, is recommended today; the compulsory floor for fermented drinks sits in a separate food-safety standard rather than in the quality standard.

There are rungs below these two as well, and one of them settled an argument about what counts as salmon — [what a group standard is, and who it binds](/food/salmon-in-china/). A recommended standard can also be rewritten and renumbered without losing its grip: [the whisky standard lost its own number in 2026 and became part one of a series](/food/whisky-in-china/). And the traffic runs the other way too: [the compulsory standard for a dispensed pair of glasses was abolished in March 2026 and replaced by a recommended one](/answers/can-i-buy-glasses-in-china/).
:::

## What the region physically is

The **Chinese Mission to the European Union**, presenting the region as one of the products covered by the China–EU agreement below, describes it in figures a wine reader will recognise: **37 to 39 degrees north**, altitude **1,000 to 1,500 metres**, around **3,000 hours of sunshine a year**, annual rainfall **under 200 millimetres**, mineral-rich sandy soil off the Gobi, and irrigation from the Yellow River.

That is a desert appellation kept alive by a river, which is why the vines here are buried every winter and dug out every spring — a labour cost no European region carries.

On scale, the same source gives the planted area as **550,000 mu**, about **a third of China's total wine-grape area**, describing the region as the country's largest contiguous wine-grape and estate-wine region. That figure was published in 2022 and areas move, so treat it as an order of magnitude rather than a current number.

:::note Two figures I am leaving out
The same presentation carries an annual bottle count and a total industry value. My readings of both were inconsistent by an order of magnitude, and neither survived a check against the planted area, so this page gives the area and the geography and omits the output figures rather than repeating a number I cannot stand behind.
:::

## The mark on the bottle, and who is allowed to print it

Here is the checkable part, and it is the twentieth time this site has found a consumer question already answered in plain sight in China.

The framework was rewritten recently. The **地理标志产品保护办法** (*dìlǐ biāozhì chǎnpǐn bǎohù bànfǎ*, Measures for the Protection of Geographical Indication Products) were issued by the intellectual property administration as **Order No. 80** and have been **in force since 1 February 2024**. Its Article 2 defines the category:

> 地理标志产品，是指产自特定地域，所具有的质量、声誉或者其他特性本质上取决于该产地的自然因素、人文因素的产品。

A product **from a specific area whose quality, reputation or other characteristics are essentially determined by the natural and human factors of that place.** Article 20 sets the route: a producer inside the protected area applies through the local intellectual property authority with inspection reports, and after provincial and national verification may print the geographical indication mark. Article 23 closes the door behind it:

> 其他单位或者个人不得擅自使用受保护的地理标志产品名称或者专用标志。

**Nobody else may use the protected product name or the mark.**

:::tip Look for one mark, not several
There used to be a scatter of different origin marks in China. The intellectual property administration unified them: **announcement no. 354 of 3 April 2020** published interim measures making a single geographical indication mark the official one, and abolished the old marks with a transition that ended on **31 December 2020** — 原相关地理标志专用标志使用过渡期至2020年12月31日 — with stock produced before that date allowed to keep circulating.

So on a bottle bought now, the origin claim is either carried by that one official mark, or it is a claim the producer has made on its own account. The mark is not decoration; it is an approval that had to be applied for.
:::

## Protected in Europe as well

The name is not only defended at home. **Wine of the eastern foothills of the Helan mountains is on the first batch of the China–EU agreement on geographical indications** — the hundred Chinese names protected from **1 March 2021**, when the agreement took effect.

This site has met that agreement once before, over [three Chinese coffee origins on the same list](/food/coffee-in-china/). The consequence is the same and worth stating plainly: **the name is protected in the European Union too**, so a European bottle claiming Helan origin is making a claim enforceable in Europe rather than a marketing gesture.

## China wrote a law for one wine region

Ningxia went further than a standard. The **宁夏回族自治区贺兰山东麓葡萄酒产区保护条例** (Regulations of the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region on the Protection of the Helan Mountain East Foothill Wine Production Area) was adopted on **5 December 2012** and took effect on **1 February 2013** — described in Chinese legal coverage as the country's first local law protecting a wine production area.

It was comprehensively revised on **26 March 2024** by the ninth session of the thirteenth Ningxia People's Congress Standing Committee and the revised text has been **in force since 1 May 2024**. Yinchuan's own municipal government describes the revision as writing the **酒庄基地一体化** (*jiǔzhuāng jīdì yītǐhuà*) model — estate and vineyard as one unit — into law for the first time, and adding duties to determine planted area and scale and to delimit protected zones for wine-grape planting.

:::warn What I read, and what I did not
I read the **2012** text in full in an official database, where it is flagged as amended. Two provisions there are worth knowing because they show what this kind of law does: an estate had to grow enough of its own grapes to supply itself and to carry out **fermentation, ageing, bottling and bottle storage entirely on its own premises**, and a new or extended winery could not take more than **five per cent** of its vineyard base as built land.

The **revised 2024 text I could not read on an official host** — the Ningxia government and congress sites both refused requests from here. So this page gives the revision its dates and the one feature the Yinchuan government describes, and does **not** state the current article numbers or their terms. If you need the operative wording, go to the regulation itself rather than to any summary, including this one.
:::

## In practice

- **The region name is the useful filter.** 贺兰山东麓 (*Hèlánshān dōnglù*, eastern foothills of the Helan mountains) on a label is a protected term with a standard behind it, not a producer's flourish.
- **Look for the official mark**, and remember there is only one legitimate one since the end of 2020.
- **Ningxia is a place you can actually go.** The estates run along the foothills within reach of [the Western Xia tombs and the rock art](/destinations/yinchuan/), which makes a wine day an add-on to a trip rather than the whole reason for one.
- **Shandong and Xinjiang produce seriously too**, and the drinks guide has the wider picture of [what else is worth drinking in China](/food/chinese-tea-and-drinks/).
- **Domestic beats imported on price by a distance.** Imported wine carries heavy duty, which is why it sits in the more-expensive-than-expected column of [the cost guide](/plan/china-trip-cost-budget/).
- **Buried vines are not a defect.** Winter burial is standard practice in this climate and it is part of why the wines cost what they do.
- **Hong Kong and Macau run their own labelling and trademark law**, so neither the mark nor the protected name described here works the same way on a bottle bought there.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Chinese wine any good?

That is a taste question this page does not settle. What it can tell you is that one Chinese region has a national standard written for it alone, a name protected in China and in the European Union, and a mark that a producer has to be approved to print — which is a level of formal structure most wine countries take much longer to build.

### Where is China's main wine region?

The eastern foothills of the Helan mountains in Ningxia, on the edge of the Gobi at 37 to 39 degrees north and 1,000 to 1,500 metres, irrigated from the Yellow River. Shandong and Xinjiang also produce at scale. Ningxia is the one with its own national standard and its own protective law.

### What does the mark on a Chinese wine bottle mean?

If it is the geographical indication mark, it means the producer sits inside a protected area, applied through the local intellectual property authority with inspection reports, and was approved at provincial and national level. A single official mark replaced the earlier assortment, with the transition ending on 31 December 2020.

### Is Chinese wine protected in Europe?

Wine of the eastern foothills of the Helan mountains is in the first batch of the China–EU agreement on geographical indications, which took effect on 1 March 2021. Names in that batch are protected on both sides, so the origin claim is enforceable in the European Union as well as in China.

### What is GB/T on a Chinese label?

The code of a recommended national standard. GB alone marks a compulsory one. The distinction can change over time: in March 2017 the standards administration converted 1,077 compulsory national standards into recommended ones, keeping the same numbers and simply adding the /T.

### Can I visit wine estates in Ningxia?

Estates are spread along the Helan foothills between Yinchuan and the mountains, several of them close to the imperial tombs, and the Yinchuan guide treats a half-day among them as a reasonable fourth block on a two-to-three-night trip. This page does not name individual estates or state their opening arrangements.
