Coffee appears on this site fourteen times, and every one of them is scenery. Independent coffee in the former French Concession. A street in Shanghai nicknamed the coffee street. A price on a budget table. A drink you buy to justify ninety minutes at a table. Meanwhile [the drinks guide](/food/chinese-tea-and-drinks/) works through baijiu, beer, Ningxia wine, huangjiu, milk tea and cooling teas, and never mentions coffee once.

That is worth correcting, because the interesting thing about coffee in China is not the cafés. It is that China is a coffee-growing country, and says so officially.

## Three of the first hundred were coffees

Here is the fact that reframes the subject.

The **Agreement between the Government of the People's Republic of China and the European Union on Cooperation on, and Protection of, Geographical Indications** was signed on **14 September 2020** and came into force on **1 March 2021**. The first batch protected from that day covered **100 Chinese geographical indications** and 96 European ones, with a second batch of 175 each to follow.

Under the agreement's listing for other agricultural products, China's hundred include **28 teas** — Anxi Tie Guan Yin, Wuyi rock tea, Fuding white tea and the rest — which is exactly what anyone would predict.

They also include **three coffees**:

| Geographical indication | Where it is grown |
| --- | --- |
| **保山小粒咖啡** (*Bǎoshān xiǎolì kāfēi*, Baoshan small-bean coffee) | Baoshan, western Yunnan |
| **普洱咖啡** (*Pǔ'ěr kāfēi*, Pu'er coffee) | Pu'er, southern Yunnan |
| **兴隆咖啡** (*Xīnglóng kāfēi*, Xinglong coffee) | Wanning, on Hainan |

Not a novelty crop, then, and not a marketing exercise. When China chose the first hundred names it wanted the European Union to protect — the same list that carries Pu'er tea and Shanxi aged vinegar — three of them were coffees.

Note the third one especially. **Xinglong is not in Yunnan.** It sits on Hainan, the tropical island in the far south, and Hainan's agriculture department describes Xinglong coffee as both a national geographical indication product and one of that first hundred protected in the EU. Two growing regions, not one.

## The Pu'er standard is a year old

This is the part that dates most of what is written in English about Chinese coffee, and it is recent enough that almost nothing has caught up.

**GB/T 45690-2025**, *地理标志产品质量要求 普洱咖啡* (*dìlǐ biāozhì chǎnpǐn zhìliàng yāoqiú*, "Geographical indication product — quality requirements — Pu'er coffee"), was released on **30 May 2025** and took effect on **1 September 2025**. It is a national standard, the **GB/T** prefix marking it as a recommended rather than mandatory one, and it sets the protected production area, the grades, and the testing, labelling and packaging rules for coffee sold under the Pu'er name.

:::note What I could and could not read
The two national standards portals that host the full text — `std.samr.gov.cn` and `openstd.samr.gov.cn` — refused every request, and the Yunnan market regulator's announcement page returned a server error. Two independent retrievals carry the same number, title and dates, so the standard is named here rather than paraphrased, and no clause of it is quoted or summarised beyond its stated scope.
:::

The practical consequence is small but real. A bag marked 普洱咖啡 in a Chinese supermarket is making a claim with a national standard behind it and a defined growing area, in the same way a bag marked 普洱茶 (*Pǔ'ěr chá*, Pu'er tea) is. A bag marked "Yunnan coffee" is making no such claim.

## The credential on the bag

Regular readers of this site will recognise the shape of that. Chinese commerce puts a startling amount of verifiable information on display and visitors read almost none of it — [the hygiene grade on a restaurant wall](/food/restaurant-hygiene-ratings-in-china/), [the licence behind the counter](/guides/business-licences-in-china/), [the nutrition letter on a drinks menu](/food/chinese-milk-tea/).

The geographical indication mark is the same idea applied to a bag of beans, with one distinction the others do not have: **these three names are protected in the European Union as well**, under a bilateral agreement, so the claim survives the flight home. [Chinese wine from the Helan foothills is on the same list](/food/chinese-wine/), and works exactly the same way.

That makes coffee an unusually good thing to carry back. It is light, it is not a plant product with the import complications [that tea can bring](/culture/tea-culture-in-china/), and the origin on the bag is checkable rather than decorative.

## Why your iced coffee has a paper straw

Now the visible detail, which surprises people who assume the paper straw is a chain's own policy.

It is national, it applies to the catering trade, and it is five years old. The **国家发展改革委** (National Development and Reform Commission) and the **生态环境部** (Ministry of Ecology and Environment) issued 发改环资〔2020〕80号 (*fā gǎi huán zī*, development and reform commission environment and resources circular no. 80 of 2020), *Opinions on Further Strengthening the Governance of Plastic Pollution*, on **16 January 2020**. Among its staged prohibitions:

> 到2020年底，全国范围餐饮行业禁止使用不可降解一次性塑料吸管

**By the end of 2020, the catering trade nationwide is prohibited from using non-degradable single-use plastic straws.** The NDRC's own explainer, published on 12 January 2021, dates the effect from **1 January 2021**.

The same paragraph stages single-use plastic tableware for eating in rather than taking away: prohibited from the end of 2020 in the built-up areas of prefecture-level and higher cities and at scenic spots, extended from the end of 2022 to county-town built-up areas, with a separate target of cutting non-degradable single-use plastic tableware in city takeaway by 30% by 2025.

:::warn What the rule covers, and what it does not
Read the sentence again: it binds **餐饮行业** (*cānyǐn hángyè*), the catering trade. It is about the straw a café or restaurant hands you.

**It says nothing about the straw glued to the side of a carton of milk or a bottled drink**, and I could find no official statement extending it there — which is why those straws are still plastic in a Chinese convenience store while the one in your iced americano is not. That gap is a reading of the instrument, not a loophole anyone is exploiting.

Checked for newer: the 2020 opinions have not been repealed. The **14th Five-Year Plan action plan on plastic pollution**, 发改环资〔2021〕1298号 of **8 September 2021**, continues the programme in general terms and neither restates nor changes the straw rule.
:::

Hong Kong and Macau legislate their own disposable-plastic rules, and none of the above describes them.

## Drinking it, in practice

- **Ordering is the same mechanism as everything else.** The chains run mini-programmes, so you [pick the shop, the drink and the options in the app and collect by number](/food/chinese-milk-tea/) exactly as you would for tea. No install, no Mandarin.
- **The tea shops outnumber the coffee shops** on most commercial streets, and by a wide margin. Coffee is the minority habit; it is simply a very large minority in the big cities.
- **This site names no individual cafés**, for the same reason it names no massage shops — a venue we cannot verify is worse than none. Where a whole street is the answer, it is already on the page: [the Shanghai nightlife guide names the quarter](/destinations/shanghai/nightlife/) where the density is highest.
- **Look for the origin, not the roast.** Yunnan beans are widely sold in Chinese cafés and supermarkets, and the three protected names above are the ones with a defined area behind them.
- **Expect it hot and expect it sweet by default** in a chain, in the same way [milk tea is ordered by specifying the sugar level](/food/chinese-tea-and-drinks/) rather than assuming it.
- **Budget accordingly.** International-brand coffee is on the short list of things [that cost more here than visitors expect](/plan/china-trip-cost-budget/); a domestic chain is a different price altogether.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does China grow coffee?

Yes, in two regions. Yunnan in the southwest is the main one, and Hainan in the tropical south is the other. Three Chinese coffee geographical indications — Baoshan small-bean coffee, Pu'er coffee and Xinglong coffee — were in the first batch of 100 Chinese names protected in the European Union when the China-EU geographical indications agreement took effect on 1 March 2021.

### What is Pu'er coffee?

Coffee grown in the Pu'er area of southern Yunnan, protected as a geographical indication, and since 2025 covered by its own national standard. GB/T 45690-2025 was released on 30 May 2025 and took effect on 1 September 2025, setting the protected production area, the grades and the testing, labelling and packaging rules. It is unrelated to Pu'er tea beyond sharing the place name.

### Is Chinese coffee any good?

The question is really about origin rather than nationality. Yunnan arabica is a recognised origin sold widely in Chinese cafés, and the three protected names have a defined growing area and, for Pu'er, a published national standard behind them. A bag simply labelled "Yunnan coffee" carries no such definition.

### Why do Chinese cafés use paper straws?

Because a national rule requires it. Opinions issued by the NDRC and the Ministry of Ecology and Environment on 16 January 2020 prohibited the catering trade nationwide from using non-degradable single-use plastic straws by the end of 2020, with effect dated from 1 January 2021. It is not a company policy, and it applies to catering rather than to straws attached to packaged drinks.

### Are there more coffee shops or tea shops in China?

Tea shops, comfortably. Freshly made tea chains outnumber coffee shops on a typical Chinese commercial street, often with several branches of one brand within a few hundred metres. Coffee is a large and growing habit in the major cities rather than the national default.

### Can I bring Chinese coffee home?

It travels better than most souvenirs from China: light, sealed, and with an origin on the bag that is checkable. The three protected names are recognised in the European Union under the bilateral agreement, so the designation means something outside China too. Check your own country's rules on importing food products before you buy in quantity.
