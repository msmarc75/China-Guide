The advice everywhere on this site is to follow the queue, and it is sound: turnover is the best proxy a stranger has. But it is a proxy for something that is, in fact, measured, graded and posted on the wall — and almost no visitor knows to look at it.

Food shopping has the same pattern: the thing you would judge by eye is also governed by a published rule, which is why [the lamps over a Chinese meat counter no longer flatter what is under them](/food/chinese-food-markets/).

## The board with a face on it

Chinese food-service premises are required to display a **food-safety grade notice board** in a prominent position — the entrance or the dining room — and to keep it up until the next inspection replaces it.

The grade is shown as a **cartoon face**, and that is the part you can use without reading any Chinese. The direction is consistent everywhere: **a broader smile is a better grade.**

Guangdong's provincial rules are a documented example of how the scale is drawn:

| Face | Grade | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **大笑** (*dà xiào*, big laugh) | A | Food-safety condition good |
| **微笑** (*wēi xiào*, smile) | B | Medium |
| **平脸** (*píng liǎn*, flat face) | C | Average |

## Do not memorise the faces

This is the catch, and it is why the page tells you to read the expression rather than match an icon.

**The grading is administered province by province, and the sets differ.** Guangdong's worst face is a flat one. Other provinces publish scales in which the lowest grade is a **frowning** face, and some run a *dynamic* grade from the most recent inspection alongside an *annual* one, so a single establishment may be showing you two boards that are not saying the same thing.

Sources on the exact face-to-letter mapping genuinely disagree, and this page does not pretend otherwise. What survives everywhere is the ranking: **compare the face you are looking at to the other two on the scale printed beside it.** A flat face is the bottom of Guangdong's scale and the middle of someone else's.

The face is not the only paper on that wall. A separate regime licenses the **premises** — air, water, lighting, noise and customer utensils — and [that certificate is compulsory across seven categories of public place, from bathhouses to barbershops](/guides/hygiene-licences-in-china/). Different rule, different issuer, different question answered.

## The kitchen you can see into

The second instrument is national rather than provincial, and it is the more interesting one.

**明厨亮灶** (*míng chú liàng zào*, "bright kitchen, bright stove") is defined in guidance issued by the State Administration for Market Regulation on **26 April 2018** as food-service providers using **transparent or video means to show food-preparation processes to the public**.

The guidance names what should be visible: kitchen environment hygiene, cold-dish preparation, raw-food preparation, cooking, and the washing and disinfection of tableware.

That is why so many Chinese restaurants have either a glass wall onto the kitchen or a screen in the dining room showing it live. It is not a design fashion. It is a supervision programme, and the screen in the corner playing what looks like security footage of a wok station is the point of it.

## What the grade does not tell you

Worth being clear, because it changes how you use it.

The rating measures **process and premises** — storage, separation, cleaning, records, the things an inspector can check. It says nothing about whether the food is any good, whether the noodles are hand-pulled, or whether the place is worth crossing a city for.

So the two instruments do different jobs and neither replaces the other. [The queue tells you about turnover and about whether locals rate the cooking](/answers/how-do-i-find-good-restaurants-in-china/); the board tells you about the kitchen you cannot see. A long queue outside a C-grade shop is a real trade-off rather than a contradiction, and plenty of very good Chinese food is sold from premises that will never score an A. Note also that the face does not travel: [a delivery listing shows credentials, shopfront and address instead of a grade](/guides/food-delivery-in-china/).

## Where this leaves the standard advice

Nothing on the site changes — it gains an instrument.

- **Look at the board on the way in.** It takes a second and needs no Chinese.
- **Prefer the visible kitchen** where you have a choice between two similar places.
- **Keep following the queue**, which remains [the most reliable signal for stalls and small shops](/food/chinese-street-food/) where a board may be a licence card rather than a graded notice.
- **Judge the individual kitchen in front of you**, which is the rule that governs when the paperwork and the crowd disagree.
- **The same habit pays elsewhere.** A drinks menu in Shanghai now carries [a nutrition grade from A to D on each drink](/food/chinese-milk-tea/), read the same way and just as quickly.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do Chinese restaurants display a hygiene rating?

Yes. Food-service premises are required to post a food-safety grade notice board in a prominent position, showing a graded cartoon face, and to keep it displayed until the next inspection replaces it.

### What do the smiley faces on Chinese restaurants mean?

They are the food-safety grade. A broader smile means a better grade, everywhere. The precise set varies by province — Guangdong uses a big laugh, a smile and a flat face for A, B and C, while other provinces use a frowning face for the lowest grade — so read the expression against the scale printed beside it rather than memorising one icon.

### Why do Chinese restaurants show the kitchen on a screen?

Because of 明厨亮灶, a national programme defined in 2018 guidance as showing food-preparation processes to the public by transparent or video means. Kitchen hygiene, cold-dish and raw-food preparation, cooking and dishwashing are the processes it names.

### Is a C-grade Chinese restaurant unsafe?

Not necessarily, and the grade is not a verdict on the food. It measures premises and process rather than cooking, and the letter scales differ by province. Treat it as one input alongside turnover, and [the rules that cover most of the actual risk](/answers/is-street-food-safe-in-china/) still apply whatever the board says.
