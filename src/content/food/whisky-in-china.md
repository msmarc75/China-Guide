[The drinks guide](/food/chinese-tea-and-drinks/) runs through baijiu, beer, Chinese wine, rice wine and minority spirits. Whisky is not in it, and until this page the word did not appear anywhere on this site.

China has had a national standard for whisky since 2008 and has just replaced it. What happened to the replacement is more interesting than the replacement itself.

## Three standards became one

Until recently China had three separate national standards for three imported spirit categories, each with its own number, all dated 2008. They have been consolidated into a single numbered series called **烈性酒质量要求** (*lièxìng jiǔ zhìliàng yāoqiú*, Quality requirements for spirits), and the national standards catalogue records the result like this:

| Part | Standard | In force | Replaced |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1 — Whisky** | GB/T 11856.**1**-2025 | **1 Feb 2026** | GB/T 118**57**-2008 |
| 2 — Brandy | GB/T 11856.**2**-2023 | 1 Jul 2024 | GB/T 118**56**-2008 |
| 3 — Vodka | GB/T 11856.**3**-2025 | 1 Jul 2026 | GB/T 118**58**-2008 |

Read the right-hand column against the left. **Brandy kept its number and the other two lost theirs.** Whisky had been 11857 since 2008; it is now part one of the number brandy used to hold alone. Vodka's 11858 is gone the same way.

That is not a cosmetic renumbering. A standard with its own number is a document about one product. A standard that is part one of three is a framework, and parts two and three are the same framework applied to something else — which means the definitions, the test methods and the labelling rules now line up across the three categories instead of each going its own way.

All three are administered by the **全国酿酒标准化技术委员会** (National Technical Committee on Brewing Standardization), under the China Light Industry Federation.

## What kind of standard this is

:::note Recommended, not compulsory — and the site has been here twice
The catalogue records GB/T 11856.1-2025 as **推荐性 现行** — *recommended, current*. The **/T** is the whole story, and [the wine page sets out what GB and GB/T mean and how a standard can change its force without changing its number](/food/chinese-wine/).

So this standard does not make it illegal to sell something called whisky that fails it. What it does is fix what the words mean when a producer chooses to use them, and give an inspector, a buyer or a court something to point at. [The rung below this one settled an argument about what may be called salmon](/food/salmon-in-china/), and the same logic applies here.

Before deciding "it is only recommended, so it does not matter", note who wrote it: **33 drafting organisations and 47 named drafters**, including the China Alcoholic Drinks Association, the China National Research Institute of Food and Fermentation Industries, and the Beijing representative office of a Hong Kong foreign-spirits producers company. An industry that agrees on definitions tends to use them.
:::

The standard was **published on 24 January 2025** and came into force **1 February 2026**. That gap — the window in which producers relabel — is **just over twelve months**, and it is notably longer than its two siblings got: brandy had six months and vodka has seven. Whisky was given twice the runway.

## What the definitions do

The base definition, as the standard is reproduced in Chinese trade coverage of it, is the conventional one:

> 以谷物为原料，经糖化、发酵、蒸馏、陈酿、经或不经调配而成的蒸馏酒

**A distilled spirit made from grain, through saccharification, fermentation, distillation and maturation, with or without blending.**

Three things the revision added are worth a traveller's attention.

**Single malt is now defined, and it is defined by the factory.** The new text adds 单一麦芽威士忌 and 单一谷物威士忌 — single malt and single grain — and the test is that saccharification, fermentation and distillation are all completed **at the same plant**. Not the same company, not the same region: the same works. Trade coverage of the standard reports that single malt additionally carries a maturation floor of **three years**.

**There is a general maturation floor too.** The same coverage quotes the standard as requiring that 威士忌原酒的陈酿时间不应少于2年 — the maturation time of the base spirit **should not be less than two years**.

**And a flavoured category exists.** 调味威士忌, flavoured whisky, is named as its own thing rather than left to argument, which is the same move Chinese standards made over [what may be called salmon](/food/salmon-in-china/): if a category is going to be sold, name it and let the label carry the difference.

## The one definition worth carrying into a shop

:::warn Age means barrel time, and nothing else
The revision adds a definition of **酒龄** (*jiǔ líng*), age. As reproduced in trade coverage of the standard, it is the time the base spirit spends maturing in wood:

> 威士忌原酒在木桶中陈酿的时间，与产品灌装后贮藏的时间无关

The maturation time of the base spirit **in the wooden barrel**, and it has **nothing to do with the time the product is stored after bottling**.

That second clause is the useful half. A bottle that has sat on a shelf for a decade has not aged for a decade. Whisky drinkers in producing countries take this for granted. Writing it into the standard puts it beyond argument at the counter, which is the same reason [a mark or a number on a bottle beats a shopkeeper's account of it](/guides/shopping-in-china/).

The standard also defines **原酒** (*yuán jiǔ*), the base spirit itself — what comes off the still and out of the barrel **before bottling**. Age attaches to that, not to the bottle.
:::

## In practice

- **Look for the part number, not the old one.** A label or a spec sheet citing GB/T 11857-2008 is citing a standard that has been replaced. GB/T 11856.1 is the current reference for whisky.
- **"Single malt" now has a factory test behind it in China.** One plant for saccharification, fermentation and distillation — a claim that can be checked rather than asserted.
- **An age statement is a barrel statement.** Time in the bottle does not count, and the definition says so in as many words on the reading available here.
- **This is a quality standard, not a safety one.** Recommended standards set what words mean; the compulsory floor for a drink sits in food-safety standards elsewhere, which is the same split [the wine page describes](/food/chinese-wine/).
- **Vodka joined the series on 1 July 2026**, and brandy in July 2024. All three parts are now the current reference for their category; the three 2008 numbers are all superseded.
- **Imported spirits carry heavy duty in China**, the same as imported wine, so the price gap against home is usually the wrong way round.
- **Hong Kong and Macau do not apply mainland national standards**, so none of the definitions above governs a bottle bought there.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does China have a national standard for whisky?

Yes. GB/T 11856.1-2025, *Quality requirements for spirits — Part 1: Whisky*, published on 24 January 2025 and in force since 1 February 2026. It replaced GB/T 11857-2008, which had been the whisky standard since 2008.

### Is the Chinese whisky standard compulsory?

No. The catalogue records it as recommended and current — the /T in GB/T marks a recommended standard rather than a compulsory one. It fixes what terms such as single malt mean when a producer uses them, rather than setting a legal floor for sale. Food-safety requirements sit in separate compulsory standards.

### What does an age statement mean on a Chinese whisky?

Time the base spirit spent maturing in wood. The standard's definition of age is explicit that it has nothing to do with how long the product has been stored after bottling, so a bottle that has sat on a shelf for years has not aged for years.

### What counts as single malt whisky in China?

The revision defines single malt and single grain whisky by where the work happens: saccharification, fermentation and distillation must all be completed at the same plant. Trade coverage of the standard reports a three-year maturation minimum for single malt on top of that.

### Why did the whisky standard change its number?

Because three separate 2008 standards for whisky, brandy and vodka were consolidated into one numbered series, *Quality requirements for spirits*. Brandy kept the number 11856 and became part 2; whisky moved from 11857 to 11856.1 and vodka from 11858 to 11856.3. The point of the series is that definitions and methods now line up across the three categories.

### When did the new Chinese vodka standard take effect?

GB/T 11856.3-2025, part 3 of the same series, was published on 2 December 2025 and took effect on 1 July 2026, replacing GB/T 11858-2008. Brandy went first, in July 2024, then whisky in February 2026 and vodka last.
