Two pages here point at Cantonese morning tea and then walk away. The Guangzhou guide names [the grand houses and what to order](/destinations/guangzhou/) and says, in full, that morning tea is the institution and to go between seven and eleven. The tea guide notes that in Guangzhou and Hong Kong, [tea is the frame for dim sum](/culture/tea-culture-in-china/), and moves on to the next city.

Both are correct. Neither prepares you for the table.

## It is a morning, not a dish

**早茶** (*zǎochá*, morning tea) is not a category of food. It is a way of spending the first part of a day, and the food is the accompaniment to it.

The order of the words matters: *yum cha* is Cantonese for **drinking tea**. Tea comes first and is chosen first; the small dishes arrive around it. Groups sit for two or three hours. Newspapers get read. Three generations turn up together, and the noise level is considerable. Nobody is in a hurry, and the table is not being turned.

That is the single most useful thing to know, because the Western instinct is to treat dim sum as a lunch that happens to be small. Arriving at half past nine expecting a quick plate, and finding a full room of people who have been there since eight and intend to stay until eleven, is the standard first experience.

The [street breakfast tradition](/food/chinese-street-food/) that dominates the rest of China is the opposite of this: eaten standing, in five minutes, on the way to work. Guangzhou has that too. Morning tea is the other thing.

## The charge nobody ordered

Here is what actually catches people out, and it is the only part of the meal with rules attached.

A Cantonese restaurant will normally add a **茶位费** (*chá wèi fèi*, tea-seat charge) — a per-person charge covering the tea, and often the pickles, the wet towel and the crockery. You did not order it and it is not a service charge. It is the cost of the seat and the pot.

It is also lawful, provided it is displayed. **Not** displaying it is where the problem lies, and the position is set by the general price rules rather than by anything specific to catering.

:::warn What Guangzhou told its own restaurants
On **11 January 2025** the **Guangzhou Municipal Market Regulation Bureau** issued a price-behaviour reminder to caterers ahead of the Spring Festival. Its instruction on this point is direct: where a tea-seat charge is collected, it **must be clearly marked in a conspicuous position**, and the menu, the price labels and the ordering app must all carry **the same** prices.

The same reminder names a second practice as a pricing offence: **反向抹零** (*fǎnxiàng mǒ líng*), "reverse rounding" — rounding the bill **up** at settlement rather than down.

That sits on top of the national rule this site sets out separately, under which [a business may not collect any fee it did not mark](/guides/marked-prices-in-china/). A tea charge on a board is a price you can decide about. A tea charge that appears only on the bill is the thing the rule prohibits.
:::

## Minimum charges are prohibited, and the rule moved in 2025

The other charge worth knowing about is the one that is not allowed at all — and the instrument behind it was replaced last year, which is why most published advice cites the wrong article.

The **餐饮业促进和经营管理办法** (*cānyǐn yè cùjìn hé jīngyíng guǎnlǐ bànfǎ*, Measures for the Promotion and Administration of the Catering Industry) were issued jointly by the **Ministry of Commerce and the National Development and Reform Commission** as **Order No. 3 of 2025**, promulgated on **12 May 2025** and **in force since 15 June 2025**. They expressly **repeal** the 2014 trial measures that preceded them.

**Article 16** is one sentence:

> 禁止餐饮服务经营者设置最低消费。

**Caterers are prohibited from setting a minimum charge.** The rule itself is not new — it carried over from Article 12 of the repealed 2014 measures — but the article number changed, and anything citing Article 12 is now citing a repealed text.

This matters most in the private rooms that Cantonese restaurants are full of. A minimum spend attached to a room is the practice the article addresses.

**What the new measures do not do**, having looked: they say nothing about tea-seat charges. There is no article requiring a caterer to display one, and none prohibiting it. That gap is why the answer sits with the marked-price rules and with municipal reminders like Guangzhou's rather than in the catering measures themselves.

## One provision that helps two people order

**Article 13** of the same measures is aimed at food waste and turns out to be useful at a dim sum table.

Caterers are to make **small portions and multiple sizes** reasonably available across staples, dishes and drinks, and may enrich the menu by marking the **main ingredients, the portion size, the flavour and the suggested number of diners**.

Two people facing a menu of forty steamer baskets are exactly the case that provision imagines. Where a menu carries a suggested headcount, use it; ordering four dishes and adding more is the local method anyway.

The idea is not the ministry's alone. [The Anti-Food Waste Law carries the same instruction at the level of a statute](/food/food-waste-law-in-china/), which is why small portions and portion markings turn up on menus well beyond Guangdong.

## In practice

- **Go between seven and eleven**, which the [Guangzhou guide](/destinations/guangzhou/) already tells you and is worth repeating because the kitchen genuinely stops.
- **Expect a per-person tea charge**, and look for it on the board or the menu before you sit.
- **Order in rounds.** Two or three dishes at a time, then more. The steamer baskets stack up and there is no service in bringing everything at once.
- **The tea is a choice.** You will be asked which one; it is a real question, and [the six categories](/culture/tea-culture-in-china/) are the answer to it.
- **Tap two fingers when someone pours for you.** The wordless thank-you, and the one piece of table etiquette that transfers everywhere in the south.
- **A minimum spend on a private room is prohibited.** So is a charge that was never displayed.
- **Hong Kong runs its own consumer and pricing law**, so the mainland rules above describe Guangzhou and the rest of Guangdong, not the yum cha you will eat across the border.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is yum cha?

Cantonese for "drinking tea" — a morning of tea with small dishes served alongside, lasting two or three hours rather than the length of a meal. The tea is chosen first and the dim sum accompanies it, which is the reverse of how visitors usually understand the phrase.

### What time is yum cha in Guangzhou?

Roughly 07:00 to 11:00. It is genuinely a morning institution rather than an all-day option, and the kitchens stop, so arriving at midday for dim sum means a different meal in a different restaurant.

### What is the tea charge in a Chinese restaurant?

A per-person charge covering the tea and often the pickles, wet towel and crockery. It is lawful where it is displayed. Guangzhou's market regulator told caterers in January 2025 that a tea-seat charge must be clearly marked in a conspicuous position and that the menu, price labels and ordering app must show the same prices.

### Can a Chinese restaurant set a minimum charge?

No. Article 16 of the Measures for the Promotion and Administration of the Catering Industry, in force since 15 June 2025, prohibits caterers from setting a minimum charge. The rule carried over from the repealed 2014 measures, where it was Article 12.

### Do I have to pay a tea charge I was not told about?

The catering measures do not address tea charges at all. The general position is that a business may not collect a fee it did not mark, so a charge appearing only at settlement is the situation those rules govern — and rounding a bill up rather than down is separately named as a pricing offence.

### Is yum cha only in Guangzhou and Hong Kong?

It is Cantonese in origin and strongest across Guangdong and Hong Kong, though large Cantonese restaurants in other Chinese cities run a version of it. Elsewhere in China the morning meal is the street breakfast tradition instead, which is a completely different format.
