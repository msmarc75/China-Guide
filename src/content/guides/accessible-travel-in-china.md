Accessibility in China is not one condition, it is two, sitting next to each other. A high-speed station built in 2019 is level, lifted and signed. The lane of hutong outside it has a kerb, a step and a parked scooter across the dropped section. Planning a trip here is largely the work of staying on the new side of that line.

Everything below is stated only where two independent sources agreed. Where they did not, the claim is left out rather than softened — a guide that is nearly right about a ramp is worse than no guide, because somebody builds an afternoon on it.

## The law changed in 2023, and it shows

China passed its **Barrier-Free Environment Construction Law** (无障碍环境建设法, *wúzhàng'ài huánjìng jiànshè fǎ*) on 28 June 2023, and it came into force on **1 September 2023**. It is the country's first comprehensive statute on the subject, running to 8 chapters and 72 articles, and it covers physical facilities, accessible information and public services together.

What that means for a visitor is not abstract. Infrastructure built or renovated since then is built to a standard with legal force behind it, which is why the newest airports, stations and metro lines are markedly better than anything from the previous decade. It also means the gap between new and old is widening rather than closing, and the old is what most historic sights are made of.

## High-speed rail is the strongest part of the trip

The trains themselves are the best-provisioned transport in the country. China Railway's own passenger information confirms that high-speed services carry **accessible toilets with grab rails and SOS alarms**, and larger stations run a **priority passenger service desk** (重点旅客, *zhòngdiǎn lǚkè*) that can be booked in advance and will provide assistance through the station.

Book that assistance ahead rather than arriving and hoping. Chinese stations are airport-scale — the walk from entrance to platform can be several hundred metres, and the [gate closes three to five minutes before departure](/answers/how-early-should-i-arrive-at-a-chinese-train-station/) whether or not you have reached it.

:::warn The disabled fare allocation is closed to foreign visitors
China Railway sets aside a specific ticket allocation for disabled passengers, and 12306 now sells it online rather than only at a window. **It requires a People's Republic of China disability certificate** (残疾人证, *cánjírén zhèng*) at grade 1 or 2, or a PRC disabled-veteran certificate.

A foreign passport holder cannot obtain either, so the reserved allocation and the fare reduction are not available to you. Book an ordinary ticket and arrange assistance separately through the station's priority passenger service. This is the single most common thing an English-language guide gets wrong about Chinese rail.
:::

The system is not flawless, and it is worth knowing that from the reporting rather than from experience. Chinese media covered a case in August 2025 in which 12306 allocated a designated accessible seat to an ordinary passenger, leaving a wheelchair user beside the toilet for a four-hour journey. Provision exists; enforcement of it is uneven.

## The metro: better inside than outside

Beijing completed a **network-wide barrier-free upgrade in 2022**, and the detail of how matters more than the headline.

Older lines — 1 and 2 among them — were not retrofitted with full lifts, because the stations physically cannot take them. They use **wheelchair lift platforms** (轮椅升降平台, *lúnyǐ shēngjiàng píngtái*) and **stair-climbing machines** (爬楼车, *pá lóu chē*) operated by staff. From 2020 the operator renewed 109 lift platforms and 52 stair-climbers, and added 33 more platforms and 7 stair-climbers.

The practical consequence: on an older line, getting between street and platform is a **staffed procedure rather than a lift you use yourself**. Budget time for it, and expect to wait for someone to come.

Every station has a **one-touch call button** (一键呼叫, *yī jiàn hūjiào*) to summon that help, and staff run a relay — the origin station hands you to the destination station, so assistance is arranged end to end rather than at each hop.

The most useful finding is one the *Beijing Daily* published after accompanying wheelchair users through the upgraded network: **inside the station it works; outside it, the obstacles remain.** Dropped kerbs interrupted by parked scooters, tactile paving that stops, a station exit that puts you on the wrong side of a road with no crossing. Plan the metro journey and then plan the two hundred metres at each end separately, because they are the harder part.

## The Forbidden City has a step-free route, and its scale is the problem

The Palace Museum built a barrier-free route along its **central axis** — roughly 600 metres, running north from 昭德门 (*Zhāodé Mén*) to the 神武门 (*Shénwǔ Mén*) exit at the far end. That route exists and is the reason a wheelchair user can see the main halls at all.

It is worth understanding what it does not solve. The Palace Museum's own account of the project describes the underlying difficulty in a sentence that has not stopped being true: 逢台必有阶，遇门定有槛 (*féng tái bì yǒu jiē, yù mén dìng yǒu kǎn*) — every terrace has steps, every gateway has a raised threshold. The side courtyards, the galleries off the axis and most of the smaller halls are reached by exactly those steps and thresholds. The central axis is a route through the site, not access to it.

The same caution applies to the other imperial sights, and I have deliberately not listed which parts of the Summer Palace, the Temple of Heaven or any Great Wall section are passable. I could not confirm those to two sources, and guessing at them is precisely the failure this page is trying not to commit. Contact the site before you go.

## What you cannot get as a visitor

A recurring pattern is worth stating once, plainly. Most Chinese accessibility concessions — free or reduced entry at museums and sights, the reserved rail allocation, priority queuing schemes — are **gated on the PRC disability certificate** (残疾人证, *cánjírén zhèng*). It is issued to Chinese citizens after assessment, and there is no visitor equivalent and no reciprocal recognition of a foreign disability card.

So budget for full-price entry everywhere, and treat any English-language page promising free admission for disabled visitors as describing a benefit you will be asked for a Chinese certificate to claim.

## Practical points that hold generally

**Newer is better, without exception.** A 2020s metro line, a high-speed station, a five-star hotel and a new shopping centre will be level and lifted. A 1990s building, a hutong, a temple and a street market will not.

**Ride-hailing is not a solution on its own.** Ordinary DiDi cars are ordinary cars. If a folding chair and a transfer work for you, this is the most flexible way to move around a city; if you travel in a powered chair that cannot be folded, road transport needs arranging specifically rather than hailing.

**Ask the hotel to write it down.** A request in Chinese characters, shown at a ticket desk or handed to a taxi driver, gets further than any attempt to explain in English. The [phrase and translation tools](/culture/mandarin-phrases-for-travellers/) that work for everything else work here too.

**Boiling water, toilets and rest points are everywhere**, which sounds minor and is not. Every station and most public buildings have free hot water and seating, and [accessible toilets are part of what the 2023 law mandates for new build](/answers/is-toilet-paper-provided-in-china/) — though carrying tissues remains the rule in older facilities.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is China wheelchair accessible?

Its newest infrastructure genuinely is — high-speed rail, recent metro lines, new airports and modern hotels are level, lifted and signed. Its older infrastructure, which includes most historic sights and older residential streets, largely is not, and the two sit side by side in every city.

### Do Chinese high-speed trains have wheelchair spaces?

The trains carry accessible toilets with grab rails and SOS alarms, and larger stations run a bookable priority-passenger assistance service. There is also a reserved ticket allocation for disabled passengers, but it requires a Chinese disability certificate that a foreign visitor cannot obtain.

### Can foreign disabled travellers get free entry to Chinese attractions?

Generally no. Chinese concessions are tied to the PRC disability certificate, issued to citizens after assessment, and foreign disability cards are not recognised in its place. Budget for full-price admission.

### Does the Beijing metro have lifts at every station?

Not everywhere. Older lines use staff-operated wheelchair lift platforms and stair-climbing machines rather than passenger lifts, because those stations cannot physically take one. Every station has a one-touch call button to summon assistance, and staff arrange it as a relay from your origin station to your destination.

### What is the hardest part of travelling in China with limited mobility?

By the reporting of people who have done it, the street rather than the building — dropped kerbs blocked by parked scooters, tactile paving that stops, and station exits that leave you on the wrong side of a road. Stations and new buildings tend to work; the couple of hundred metres between them is what needs planning.
