For a few years the rule for China was simple and unforgiving: everything needed booking, days ahead, or you did not get in. A lot of English-language guidance still says exactly that.

It stopped being the whole truth in July 2024, and the shape of what replaced it is more useful than either version.

## What changed in 2024

Through July 2024 a run of major cities lifted reservation requirements at most of their attractions. Xinhua reported Shanghai, Beijing, Suzhou, Chengdu and Hangzhou dropping the requirement at most major sights, and [Shenzhen](/destinations/shenzhen/) removing it across 133 venues — 17 tourist attractions, 61 museums, 33 art galleries, 12 libraries and 10 cultural centres.

The Ministry of Culture and Tourism's guidance behind this was explicit that a "one-size-fits-all" approach had to be avoided, and the National Cultural Heritage Administration encouraged museums to extend opening hours and drop reservation policies. The State Council's own account notes the effect was immediate: search volume for Beijing attraction tickets nearly tripled.

So the default has flipped. For most sights in most cities, you can now turn up.

The same instinct is written into the rules elsewhere. For a large performance [the organiser must name the ticketing agents it has authorised](/guides/concerts-in-china/), so the question of which seller is official has a published answer rather than a guessed one.

## The exceptions are the famous ones

This is the part that matters, because the cities kept reservations exactly where demand is highest.

**Beijing kept two**: the Palace Museum — the Forbidden City — and the National Museum of China. **Suzhou kept three**: the Humble Administrator's Garden, the Lion Grove Garden and the main site of the Suzhou Museum.

Read that list again and notice what it is. It is not a random remnant; it is the headline sights. A visitor who reads "China scrapped its booking rules" and plans accordingly will walk to the Forbidden City and be turned away, which our own [Forbidden City page](/answers/do-i-need-to-book-forbidden-city-tickets-in-advance/) describes as the single most common way a Beijing itinerary breaks — there is no ticket window at all to fall back on.

The same holds beyond those cities. The [Mogao Caves at Dunhuang](/answers/do-i-need-to-book-mogao-caves-tickets-in-advance/) release much further ahead than anything else in the country, and [Xiamen University](/answers/do-i-need-to-book-to-visit-xiamen-university/) rations campus entry in timed slots. Each of those pages carries its own current window, which is where those numbers belong, because they move.

:::warn Treat the exception list as a moving target
Which venues require booking is set city by city and changes. The durable rule is the shape, not the list: **assume the single most famous thing in any city still needs booking, and check that specific venue's own channel a week before you go.** Everything else, you can probably walk into.
:::

## Free does not mean walk-in

The most counterintuitive part of the Chinese system, and the one that catches visitors from countries where free means unticketed: **most state museums charge nothing and still issue a capped number of timed slots.**

The cap and the payment are simply two different things. Admission is free; the number of people admitted is [a maximum the site is legally obliged to publish and forbidden to exceed](/guides/chinese-scenic-areas/), so the booking exists to allocate that ceiling rather than to take your money.

The National Museum of China, the Shanghai Museum, provincial museums — free to enter, and among the best value in the country, which is why [the budget page](/plan/china-trip-cost-budget/) singles them out. Free also means heavily demanded. Popular morning slots at the busiest museums are reported to clear within minutes of release, particularly at weekends and in holiday periods.

A free ticket you did not book is worth exactly as much as a paid one you did not book.

## How the booking actually works

The pattern is consistent enough to learn once.

**The channel is the venue's own.** Bookings run through a museum or site's official WeChat mini-programme or its own website, not through a general marketplace. Where a venue publishes a list of authorised channels, use one of them — the Dunhuang Academy, for instance, states that it has authorised no third-party sellers at all, which makes anything else you find a reseller at best. If the phrase is unfamiliar, [a mini-programme is an app that runs inside WeChat or Alipay](/guides/mini-programmes-in-china/) rather than on your phone — and it is also where the real-name requirement below comes from, because the obligation sits on the platform rather than on the museum.

**It is real-name throughout.** You enter the passport number of every visitor at the time of booking, and each person carries that same passport to the gate, where the physical document is checked against the booking. Not a photograph, not a photocopy, and not a friend's booking made in their name. This is the same regime that governs [theme parks and large events](/guides/theme-parks-in-china/), and it is worth internalising once rather than rediscovering at each gate.

**Slots are released on a schedule** — a fixed number of days ahead, at a fixed hour, for the busiest venues. If something you want is sold out, the two things that actually work are being ready at the release hour for a later date, and checking back for cancellations, which do return to the pool.

One category in that Shenzhen count deserves more than a line, because it is free without being ticketed and open when almost nothing else is: [the public library, and the statute that keeps its reading rooms free](/culture/public-libraries-in-china/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I still need to book attractions in China?

Usually not. Through July 2024, Shanghai, Beijing, Suzhou, Chengdu, Hangzhou and Shenzhen lifted reservation requirements at most attractions. But each city kept a short list of exceptions, and those exceptions are the most famous sights — Beijing retained the Palace Museum and the National Museum of China.

### Why do free Chinese museums need a reservation?

Because entry is free but capacity is capped, so the booking is how the daily limit is allocated rather than how payment is taken. Popular slots at the busiest free museums are reported to clear within minutes of release.

### How do foreigners book Chinese attraction tickets?

Through the venue's own WeChat mini-programme or official website, entering the passport number of every visitor. Bookings are real-name, and the physical passport used to book is checked at the gate — a photo or photocopy is not accepted.

### What if the tickets are sold out?

Wait for the release hour and book a later date, or check back for cancellations, which return to the pool. There is generally no same-day walk-up alternative at the venues that still require booking, and no ticket office at some of them.

### Should I buy attraction tickets from a third-party site?

Prefer the venue's own channel. Some sites publish the list of channels they have authorised — the Dunhuang Academy states it has authorised none at all — which makes any other seller a reseller, with the real-name requirement still applying to whatever they issue you.
