If you hold a passport from one of the countries covered by China's 30-day visa-free scheme, a short business trip almost certainly requires no visa. That is not an interpretation or a loophole — the scheme names business as one of its permitted purposes, alongside tourism, family visits, exchanges and transit.

A great deal of advice still tells business travellers to apply for an M visa by default. For a week of meetings or a trade fair, most of them are queueing for a document they do not need.

## What visa-free actually covers, and what it does not

The [China Visa Application Service Centre's own guidance](/guides/china-visa-guide/) is unusually direct on both halves of this, and the second half is the one to read carefully.

**Covered:** entry "for the purpose of business, tourism, family or friends visits, exchange and transit". Meetings, negotiations, factory visits, procurement, trade fairs and corporate events all sit inside that.

**Not covered**, in the official wording: *"Visa waiver does not apply to those who come to China for work, study, news coverage or purposes alike."*

:::warn Business is not work, and the distinction is enforced
Attending meetings, negotiating, inspecting a supplier and exhibiting are business. Taking up a paid role with a Chinese employer is work, and work requires a **Z visa** with employer sponsorship and a work permit arranged before you travel. Entering visa-free or on a business visa and then working is an immigration violation, not a grey area.

Journalism is called out separately in the same sentence. If you are travelling to report, the visa waiver does not apply to you regardless of how short the trip is.
:::

Which nationalities qualify changes as the scheme is extended, so check rather than assume — the [visa guide](/guides/china-visa-guide/) carries the current routes and the [visa checker](/tools/china-visa-checker/) will tell you which one applies to your passport and trip.

## Thirty days, and what happens if you need longer

The stay is capped at **30 days**, and the official position is that anyone planning a longer trip should apply for the appropriate visa before travelling rather than sorting it out on arrival.

Sources differ on what is possible once you are already in the country. Much secondary guidance states flatly that a visa-free entry cannot be extended or converted. The official FAQ is softer: it says that those who "have to stay longer than 30 days for appropriate and sufficient reasons after entering China without a visa" should apply for a stay permit from the exit and entry administration. They disagree about whether an extension exists; they agree it is not something to rely on. **Plan the trip to fit inside 30 days**, and if it genuinely might not, get the visa before you fly.

## When the M visa is still the right answer

The M visa is the commercial and trade category, and it remains necessary in three situations:

- **Your passport is not covered** by the visa-free scheme or one of the regional schemes.
- **The trip runs past 30 days**, or you expect a run of trips that a single-entry permission would not cover.
- **The work is sustained** — an extended project, delivering training over weeks, or an assignment where you are effectively embedded with a Chinese partner.

The application turns on an **invitation letter** from a Chinese trade partner or host organisation. Getting it is the part with the lead time, not the visa appointment: it comes from the counterparty, and a counterparty who has not done it before will take longer than you expect. Ask for it the moment the trip is real.

## Expenses: ask for a fapiao, not a receipt

This is the most common way a business traveller loses money in China, and almost nothing written for tourists mentions it.

A **fapiao** (发票, *fāpiào*) is an official tax invoice issued under a government-administered system. It is not the same thing as the till receipt or the payment confirmation in Alipay, and the difference is not cosmetic — the fapiao is the document that carries legal weight for tax and reimbursement purposes. A receipt is a record that you paid; a fapiao is the instrument your finance department, and Chinese tax administration, actually recognise.

The practical consequences:

- **Ask at the point of payment.** The phrase is 我要发票 (*wǒ yào fāpiào*). Restaurants, hotels and taxis all issue them, and hotels will usually produce one at checkout without being asked if you say the stay is for business.
- **You will be asked who it is for.** A fapiao can be issued to an individual or to a company, and a company fapiao needs the company's registered Chinese name and tax number. Carry both, written down, before you land — reconstructing them from a hotel lobby is miserable.
- **It cannot reliably be issued later.** Like [the VAT refund form at the till](/answers/can-i-get-a-tax-refund-in-china/), this is a request to make at the time, not afterwards.

If your employer reimburses against receipts alone, none of this matters. If it reimburses against invoices, all of it does.

## Trade fairs

The Canton Fair in Guangzhou is the one most foreign buyers travel for, and its structure catches first-timers out: it does not run as a single continuous event but as **three phases, each around five days, separated by changeover days**, with different product categories in each. Which phase you attend is determined by your sector, not your calendar — turning up in the wrong one means the halls are full of an industry you do not buy from.

The spring session runs across April and May and the autumn session across October and November. [The Guangzhou guide](/destinations/guangzhou/) covers what the fair does to the city, which is the other half of the planning problem: hotel prices across Guangzhou rise sharply and rooms become scarce for its duration. Book before you finalise anything else, and confirm the current phase dates on the fair's own site rather than from any guide, including this one.

## The practical layer

**WeChat is the business channel, not email.** Expect a counterparty to move the conversation there immediately, and expect follow-up, documents and scheduling to happen in it. Have it installed and working before you arrive, along with whatever else you depend on — the [connectivity guide](/guides/internet-vpn-apps-china/) covers what does and does not reach you inside China, and the answer for most Western work tools is "not without preparation".

**Payments are the same as for any visitor.** Bind a card inside Alipay or WeChat Pay before you leave home; assume a company card will not be accepted at a counter.

**Meeting conventions are worth ten minutes of reading.** Business cards exchanged with two hands and read rather than pocketed, seniority reflected in seating and in how glasses meet at a toast, and problems raised privately rather than across a table — [the etiquette guide](/culture/chinese-etiquette/) sets out the ones that are actually noticed, and [what a banquet involves](/answers/what-is-baijiu-and-do-i-have-to-drink-it/) if you are hosted for dinner. Dinner with a Chinese counterparty frequently continues into a rented karaoke room, and [knowing how that evening is structured](/culture/ktv-in-china/) saves you improvising it.

**Do not hand an expensive suit to a dry cleaner without asking a specific question first.** Chinese laundry trade rules cap compensation for an ordinary garment at ¥2,000 unless you agree a declared value in writing at the counter, and courts have applied that cap to garments worth many times more — [how to ask, and what the ticket has to say](/guides/laundry-in-china/) takes two minutes and is the difference between a covered loss and an uncovered one.

**Build in more travel time than the map suggests.** Chinese cities are vast, and the airport or station is rarely near the district you are meeting in. A cross-town meeting is an hour, not twenty minutes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a business visa for China?

For a short trip, probably not. China's 30-day visa-free scheme explicitly covers business alongside tourism and family visits, so if your passport qualifies you can attend meetings, negotiations, factory visits and trade fairs without applying for anything. The M visa is for trips over 30 days, frequent travel, or nationalities the scheme does not cover.

### Can I work in China on a business visa?

No. Business travel covers meetings, negotiations, inspections and exhibiting; taking up paid employment requires a Z visa with employer sponsorship and a work permit obtained before you travel. The official guidance excludes work, study and news coverage from the visa waiver explicitly, and working on the wrong permission is treated as an immigration violation.

### What is a fapiao and do I need one?

A fapiao is China's official tax invoice, issued under a government-administered system, and it is not the same as a till receipt or an Alipay payment record. If your employer reimburses expenses against invoices rather than receipts, you need one — ask at the time of payment, because it usually cannot be issued afterwards.

### How long can I stay in China for business without a visa?

Thirty days per entry under the visa-free scheme. Official guidance is that anyone planning a longer stay should obtain the appropriate visa before travelling; secondary sources say a visa-free entry cannot be extended at all, while the official FAQ describes applying for a stay permit with sufficient reason. Plan the trip to fit within 30 days rather than relying on either reading.

### When is the Canton Fair and how is it organised?

It runs twice a year, in spring across April and May and in autumn across October and November, in three phases of roughly five days each, separated by changeover days. Each phase covers different product categories, so the phase you attend depends on your sector. Confirm current dates on the fair's own site and book Guangzhou accommodation early, because prices rise steeply for its duration.
