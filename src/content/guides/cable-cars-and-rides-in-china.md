Count the cable cars on this site and you get twenty-three pages: [three of them at Huangshan](/destinations/huangshan/), one 7.5 km long above Zhangjiajie City, one climbing to 4,506 m on Jade Dragon Snow Mountain, one at Mutianyu with a toboggan run beside it. Every mention is a fare and a queueing tip.

Not one of them says what a cable car legally *is* in China. It turns out to be the same thing as the lift in your hotel, the escalator in the metro station and the ride your children want to go on — and the category comes with a plate you can read while you wait.

## One box, eight kinds of machine

The governing instrument is the **中华人民共和国特种设备安全法** (*Zhōnghuá Rénmín Gònghéguó Tèzhǒng Shèbèi Ānquánfǎ*, Special Equipment Safety Law of the People's Republic of China), promulgated under **Presidential Order No. 4**, adopted on **29 June 2013** and **in force since 1 January 2014**. It has not been amended since.

Its Article 2 defines **特种设备** (*tèzhǒng shèbèi*, special equipment) as machinery posing a greater danger to personal and property safety, and lists eight kinds: boilers, pressure vessels including gas cylinders, pressure pipelines, **lifts**, lifting machinery, **passenger ropeways**, **large amusement facilities**, and site-specific motor vehicles.

Three of those eight are things a tourist rides for fun.

- **电梯** (*diàntī*, lifts) covers passenger and goods lifts **and escalators and moving walkways**. So [the 326 m glass elevator bolted to the cliff at Zhangjiajie](/destinations/zhangjiajie/) and the escalator inside the mountain at Heaven's Gate are the same regulated object as the lift in a Beijing hotel.
- **客运索道** (*kèyùn suǒdào*, passenger ropeways) covers aerial ropeways, aerial tramways and tow lifts — every cable car on this site.
- **大型游乐设施** (*dàxíng yóulè shèshī*, large amusement facilities) covers rides operated commercially to carry people for amusement.

That last one has a threshold, which is the useful part.

## The line that decides whether a ride is regulated

The Special Equipment Catalogue sets the scope of 大型游乐设施 by two measurements, joined by *or* rather than *and*: a **maximum design running speed of 2 m/s or more**, **or** a **running height of 2 m or more above the ground**.

*Or* is doing real work there. A slow ride that lifts you three metres is in. A fast ride that never leaves the ground is in. Only something both slow and low falls outside — which is roughly the definition of a toddler's roundabout.

There is also an exclusion worth knowing, because it is the same seam this site has met twice before. Facilities used for **sport, for artistic performance and for non-commercial activity** are outside the category. The regulator's interest is triggered by commercial operation, exactly as [a performance for an audience and a sporting fixture sit under different regimes](/guides/watching-sport-in-china/) despite filling the same stadium.

## The eleventh plate

Regular readers of this site will recognise the pattern by now: the thing you are told to trust turns out to be evidenced by a document displayed in plain sight. This is the eleventh one, and it is the easiest of all to find because it is at eye level in a queue.

**Article 33** requires the operator to register the equipment with the market regulator before it goes into service, or within thirty days of doing so, and to obtain a use-registration certificate. Then the sentence that matters:

> 登记标志应当置于该特种设备的显著位置。

The **registration mark must be placed in a prominent position on the equipment itself.** For a lift that means inside the car or at the landing door; for a cable car or a ride it is at the boarding point, which is where you are standing anyway.

And the plate is not decorative, because of what sits behind it. **Article 39** obliges the operator to carry out routine maintenance and periodic self-inspection of the equipment and its safety devices. **Article 40** requires it to apply to an inspection body for periodic inspection **one month before the current certificate expires** — and equipment that has not had its periodic inspection, or has failed it, **may not continue in use.**

So the date printed on that plate is the date the machine's permission to operate runs out. It is the cheapest safety check available to a visitor, and it takes about as long as reading a bus timetable.

## What has to be told to you, and what happens before opening

**Article 43** is the passenger-facing provision, and it has two halves.

The first: for a **passenger ropeway or a large amusement facility**, the operator must carry out a **test run and a routine safety check before it goes into service each day**, confirming the safety components and protective devices.

The first cabin of the morning is therefore not the first movement of the morning. The machine has already been run empty and signed off — which is a quiet argument for the early start [the scenic-area guide recommends for entirely different reasons](/guides/chinese-scenic-areas/).

The second half is a disclosure duty:

> 电梯、客运索道、大型游乐设施的运营使用单位应当将电梯、客运索道、大型游乐设施的安全使用说明、安全注意事项和警示标志置于易于为乘客注意的显著位置。

The operator of a lift, ropeway or large amusement facility must place the **safety instructions, safety precautions and warning signs where passengers will readily notice them.**

That is the source of the height bars, the restraint diagrams and the pictogram boards at Chinese ride entrances. They are not the park being cautious; they are a statutory notice, and failing to display them is separately punishable.

This is the same move the site found in a hotel stairwell, where [the evacuation diagram on your landing is required by name rather than by goodwill](/guides/hotel-fire-safety-in-china/). The pattern is consistent: the notice you walk past is usually the one somebody was ordered to put there.

## Checked for something newer, and there is

Two months before this page was written, the operating rulebook underneath the law was replaced.

**TSG 08-2026**, 特种设备使用管理规则 (*tèzhǒng shèbèi shǐyòng guǎnlǐ guīzé*, Rules for the Administration of Special Equipment Use), was announced by the market regulator on **2 February 2026** as **Announcement No. 6 of 2026**, and has been **in force since 1 May 2026**. It replaces TSG 08-2017 outright.

Its stated purpose is to implement **State Administration for Market Regulation Order No. 74**, the *Provisions on the Supervision and Administration of Special Equipment Using Units Implementing Their Principal Responsibility for Use Safety*, adopted on 27 March 2023 and in force since **5 May 2023**.

The practical point for a visitor is small but real: the plate you are reading is issued under a rulebook three months old, not the one every English-language description of Chinese lift safety was written against. The registration marking under these rules carries a **next inspection date**, which is the field to look for.

## In practice

- **Read the plate while you queue.** The registration mark is required to be in a prominent position, and the date on it is the operative one.
- **Look for the safety notice at the boarding point.** It is a legal disclosure, so its absence is itself a signal.
- **The first cabin is a checked cabin**, because a ropeway and a large ride get a test run and a safety check before each day's service.
- **Wind is the thing that stops a mountain, and it has its own colour scale.** [China grades gale separately from typhoon](/guides/gale-and-thunderstorm-warnings-in-china/), starting at Beaufort force 6 — but a ropeway suspends under its own operating rules rather than under the weather signal, so ring the operator rather than reading the colour.
- **The lift, the escalator and the cable car are one category.** If you have taken in the plate on one, you know where to look on all three.
- **Sport and performance are outside it.** A climbing wall or a stage rig is regulated as something else entirely.
- **None of this covers Hong Kong or Macau**, which run their own systems — as they do for [emergency numbers and much else besides](/answers/what-are-the-emergency-numbers-in-china/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Are cable cars in China safe?

They are regulated as special equipment under the Special Equipment Safety Law, in the same category as boilers and pressure vessels. A passenger ropeway must be registered, must display its registration mark prominently, must pass periodic inspection to stay in service, and must be given a test run and routine safety check before it opens each day.

### What is the plate on a Chinese lift or cable car?

The use-registration mark. Article 33 of the Special Equipment Safety Law requires the operator to register the equipment and place the registration mark in a prominent position on it. Under the current rules it carries a next inspection date, which tells you when its permission to operate expires.

### What counts as a large amusement ride in China?

The Special Equipment Catalogue draws the line at a maximum design running speed of 2 m/s or more, or a running height of 2 m or more above the ground — either one, not both. Facilities used for sport, artistic performance or non-commercial activity are excluded.

### Do Chinese theme park rides have to display safety warnings?

Yes. Operators of lifts, passenger ropeways and large amusement facilities must place the safety instructions, safety precautions and warning signs where passengers will readily notice them. The height bars and restraint diagrams at a ride entrance are that requirement being met.

### Are escalators special equipment in China?

Yes. The lift category covers passenger and goods lifts along with escalators and moving walkways, so an escalator carries the same registration and inspection obligations as a lift.

### Which rules govern special equipment in China?

The Special Equipment Safety Law, adopted in June 2013 and in force since January 2014, sits above the market regulator's operating rules. Those were replaced on 1 May 2026 by TSG 08-2026, which implements Order No. 74 of 2023 on how using units carry their safety responsibility.
