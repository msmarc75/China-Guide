Almost every attraction in China worth the name is a **景区** (*jǐngqū*, scenic area): a defined, gated, ticketed and actively managed site rather than a place you wander into. [Hiking in China](/guides/hiking-in-china/) covers what that means once you are inside one — the marked routes, the closing times, the legal edge of the open area. This page is about the two numbers attached to the site before you arrive.

Both are public. Neither is mentioned in most English guidance.

## The A-grade on the sign

Chinese scenic areas are graded on a five-level national scale, written as **A** through **AAAAA** and spoken as 1A through **5A**. The top grade is awarded by the **Ministry of Culture and Tourism** itself, which designates 国家5A级旅游景区 (*guójiā wǔ A jí lǚyóu jǐngqū*, national 5A-level tourist scenic areas) in formal public announcements, in batches, against a national standard and a set of management measures. Lower grades are awarded by provincial and municipal authorities on the same scale.

You will see the grade on the entrance plaque, on the ticket, on road signage and in every Chinese-language listing. It is a real administrative designation, not a marketing badge a site awards itself.

What it tells a visitor is less about beauty than about **scale and infrastructure**. The grading assesses things like transport, visitor facilities, services, safety and management alongside the appeal of the site itself. So:

- **5A** means the full apparatus: large car parks, shuttle buses, boardwalks, signage in English, staff, and the crowds that justify all of it. It is a reliable predictor that the place is well organised and heavily visited.
- **4A** is often the sweet spot — properly maintained and genuinely worth the trip, with meaningfully fewer people.
- **Below 3A** tends to mean a local site with minimal facilities, which may be exactly what you want or may be a disappointment, and is unlikely to have English anything.

The grade is therefore the fastest available answer to "will this be mobbed?" — and it is the one piece of information printed on every sign that visitors routinely ignore.

## The number that makes tickets run out

The second number is the more consequential, and it explains behaviour this site has described repeatedly without ever giving the reason.

Chinese scenic areas operate under a **legally binding daily ceiling**. Article 45 of the **中华人民共和国旅游法** (*Zhōnghuá Rénmín Gònghéguó Lǚyóufǎ*, Tourism Law of the People's Republic of China), adopted 25 April 2013 and amended in 2016 and 2018, puts it directly:

> 景区接待旅游者不得超过景区主管部门核定的最大承载量。景区应当公布景区主管部门核定的最大承载量，制定和实施旅游者流量控制方案，并可以采取门票预约等方式，对景区接待旅游者的数量进行控制。

A scenic area **may not receive more visitors than the maximum carrying capacity approved by its supervising authority**. It must publish that approved figure, must draw up and operate a visitor flow-control plan, and may use measures including ticket booking to control how many people it admits.

The article goes on: when visitor numbers **may** reach the maximum, the site must give advance public notice and report to the local government at the same time, and both must promptly take dispersal and diversion measures.

:::warn Why the gate staff cannot let you in
This is the part worth carrying around. Under Article 105 of the same law, a scenic area that fails to give notice, fails to report, fails to take diversion measures, or **admits visitors beyond its maximum carrying capacity** can be ordered to correct the failure — and where the circumstances are serious, ordered to suspend operations for rectification for **one to six months**.

A site that waves in fifty extra people is not being generous. It is risking a closure order measured in months. That is why there is no negotiating at the barrier, no unofficial extra tickets, and no queue to join instead.
:::

## What follows from it

Four things a visitor actually experiences, all downstream of that one rule:

**"Sold out" is not a sales tactic.** It is a legal ceiling being enforced by an operator with a great deal to lose. [When a Chinese attraction is booked out](/guides/booking-chinese-attractions/), the working responses are the release hour for a later date and returned cancellations — not turning up hopefully.

**Free museums still issue tickets.** If admission costs nothing, the ticket is not collecting money; it is allocating the legal cap. That is the whole explanation for the most confusing feature of the Chinese system.

**The capacity figure is published**, because the law requires it. For a site you are anxious about, the number and the current-day status are findable on its own channel rather than a matter of guesswork.

**An advance crowding announcement is a genuine signal.** When a site announces it is approaching capacity, that is a statutory notice, issued alongside a report to the local government — not an app nudge to buy sooner.

## Planning around it

The rules combine into a short and unusually reliable set of tactics.

1. **Read the grade before you commit a day.** A 5A site on a public holiday is the maximum-crowd scenario in China; the same region's 4A alternative is frequently the better visit.
2. **Book the famous ones and only the famous ones.** The exception list is short and it is the headline sights, which [the booking guide](/guides/booking-chinese-attractions/) sets out along with how the real-name system works at the gate.
3. **Weekday over weekend, always.** The cap is per day, so demand concentrates rather than spreading.
4. **Treat the national holidays as a different country.** [When to visit China](/plan/best-time-to-visit-china/) covers which weeks to avoid and why the peaks are so sharp.
5. **Arrive at opening.** It does not change the daily total, but it puts you ahead of the coach groups within it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does 5A mean for a Chinese tourist attraction?

It is the top of a five-level national grading scale running from A to AAAAA, with national 5A status designated by the Ministry of Culture and Tourism in formal public announcements. It assesses transport, facilities, services, safety and management as well as the site itself, so it predicts scale and organisation — and, reliably, crowds.

### Why do Chinese attractions sell out so quickly?

Because the daily limit is a legal ceiling rather than a commercial one. Article 45 of China's Tourism Law forbids a scenic area from receiving more visitors than the maximum carrying capacity approved by its supervising authority, and requires it to publish that figure and run a flow-control plan. Once the day's allocation is gone, there is nothing left to sell.

### Can I get into a Chinese attraction if tickets are sold out?

Generally no, and not by asking at the gate. A site admitting visitors beyond its approved capacity can be ordered to suspend operations for rectification for one to six months, so staff have no discretion to exercise. Wait for the release hour for a later date, or watch for returned cancellations.

### Why do free Chinese museums still require a booking?

Because the ticket allocates a legally capped number of daily places rather than collecting payment. Free admission and limited capacity are separate things, and the booking exists to manage the second. [How the booking system works](/guides/booking-chinese-attractions/) covers the channels and the real-name requirement.

### Is a 4A scenic area worth visiting?

Often more so than a 5A one. The grade measures facilities and management rather than interest, so a 4A site is usually well maintained and properly signposted while attracting materially fewer people. For travellers who dislike crowds, scanning for 4A alternatives near a famous 5A site is one of the more effective habits available.

### How do I find out how busy a Chinese attraction will be?

Check its grade and its published capacity. The law obliges a scenic area to publish the maximum approved by its authority, and to announce in advance when numbers may reach it, so both the ceiling and the day's status are normally available on the site's own channel — alongside whichever booking platform it uses.
