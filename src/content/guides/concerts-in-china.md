Live music in China is enormous — stadium tours, arena residencies, festivals in cities most visitors have never heard of — and a traveller with a free evening will find far more on than they expect.

The site already explains how a big-event ticket works here, in the context of football: [it is bought against a specific identity document and the same document is checked at the gate](/guides/watching-sport-in-china/), one document generally buying one ticket, with most of the house required to go on open public sale. All of that applies to concerts too and is not repeated here.

What that page does not cover, and what no English-language source seems to mention at all, is the other half of the same instrument: **a large show has to give you a way out of the ticket.**

## The instrument, and the line it draws

The rules come from a joint notice of the **Ministry of Culture and Tourism and the Ministry of Public Security**, document **文旅市场发〔2023〕96号** (*wén lǚ shì chǎng fā*, culture and tourism market circular no. 96 of 2023), issued on **12 September 2023**. Its scope is stated as a number: **commercial performances with an audience of 5,000 or more.**

Worth knowing what it is built on, because it explains the tone. The notice cites the **Regulations on the Safety Management of Large-Scale Mass Activities**, the Public Security Administration Punishments Law and the Criminal Law, alongside the culture and tourism market credit rules. It is a public-order instrument that happens to contain consumer protections, rather than the other way round.

Below five thousand, none of the strict regime applies. A club show, a small theatre and [an evening of opera at a working playhouse](/culture/chinese-opera-and-performance/) behave like live performance anywhere.

## The refund right

Here is the assumption worth correcting, because visitors treat a Chinese ticket as final the moment the payment clears:

> 演出举办单位应当建立大型演出活动退票机制，设定合理的梯次退票收费标准。

The organiser of a large performance **must establish a refund mechanism** and **set a reasonable tiered refund fee scale.** *Tiered* is the operative word: the charge is meant to rise as the date approaches, rather than the ticket simply becoming worthless the moment it is issued.

This is not a discretionary customer-service gesture. It is a required feature of selling tickets at that scale, and it sits alongside the real-name rules in the same document as part of the same clean-up of the ticketing market.

No percentages appear on this page. The notice requires a reasonable tiered scale and leaves the numbers to each organiser, so any figure printed here would be somebody's commercial policy rather than the rule.

## And you have to be told the rules

The refund right would be useless if it were buried, so the notice pairs it with a disclosure duty.

Organisers must **identify the ticketing agents they have authorised**, steer buyers towards lawful channels, and **inform consumers of the purchase, entry and refund rules.**

Two things follow. First, "which sellers are official?" has an answer the organiser is obliged to publish — the same instinct that governs [booking a Chinese attraction through the venue's own channel rather than a reseller](/guides/booking-chinese-attractions/). Second, the refund terms are meant to be in front of you at the point of sale.

**So the instruction is not to hope. It is: find the refund scale before you pay, because the seller is required to have one and to show it to you.**

## Buying one

- **Use the channel the promoter names as authorised.** Chinese ticketing runs through [mini-programmes inside the wallets](/guides/mini-programmes-in-china/) as much as through standalone apps.
- **Have every attendee's passport number ready** before the on-sale opens — the identity is entered at purchase, not afterwards.
- **Read the refund scale at the point of purchase.** It is the one piece of information this page exists to make you look for.
- **Carry the passport to the venue.** The gate checks the document itself.
- **Expect thorough security screening**, which is the norm at any large Chinese venue rather than anything specific to concerts.
- **For a smaller night out, ignore all of it.** [Beijing has the strongest independent music culture in China](/destinations/beijing/nightlife/), the venues change constantly, and the door works like a door.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I get a refund on a Chinese concert ticket?

There must be a mechanism. Organisers of performances with an audience of 5,000 or more are required to establish a refund scheme with a reasonable tiered fee scale, and to tell buyers the purchase, entry and refund rules. Find the scale before you buy rather than assuming the ticket is final.

### What is a tiered refund scale?

A fee that rises as the performance date approaches, rather than an all-or-nothing rule. The notice requires the scale to be reasonable and leaves the actual percentages to each organiser, so they vary from show to show.

### Which rules apply to concerts in China?

A joint notice of the Ministry of Culture and Tourism and the Ministry of Public Security, issued in September 2023, covering commercial performances with an audience of 5,000 or more. It sets the real-name ticketing regime, the public-sale proportion, the refund mechanism and the duty to disclose authorised sellers.

### Do the strict ticket rules apply to small gigs in China?

No. The threshold is an audience of 5,000, so club shows, small theatres and smaller festivals operate normally.

### How do I know which Chinese ticket seller is official?

The organiser is required to identify the ticketing agents it has authorised and to steer buyers to lawful channels, so the answer should be published rather than guessed at — the same principle as booking an attraction through the venue's own channel.

### Can foreigners buy concert tickets in China?

Yes, using a passport as the identity document at purchase and carrying the same passport to the gate. The mechanics are the same as for large sporting events, which this site sets out separately.
