China has somewhere in the region of 20–25 million Muslims, principally the Chinese-speaking **Hui**, who are dispersed nationwide, and the Turkic **Uyghurs** of Xinjiang. The practical consequence for a visitor is that halal food is not a specialist search in China the way it is in much of Europe — it is a food culture with its own restaurants on ordinary streets in ordinary cities.

What does need care is the assumption that the sign on the door corresponds to a certificate someone audits. In China, increasingly, it does not.

## 清真, and what it actually tells you

The word to look for is **清真** (*qīngzhēn*), which does duty for "halal" and, more broadly, for "Muslim" and "Islamic". It appears on restaurant signs, on packaged food, and on the green boards outside a whole quarter of shops in the northwest. Learning to recognise those two characters is the single most useful thing on this page.

What sits behind it is less tidy than most guidance suggests:

- **There is no national halal law or standard.** A national standard was drafted and then **halted in April 2016** after opposition, and nothing has replaced it.
- **Certification was provincial**, and several western provinces built a shared code between 2012 and 2015 to harmonise their rules.
- **Then three of the most important ones withdrew theirs.** Ningxia abolished its Halal Food Identification Standard in **2017**, Gansu on **13 December 2018** and Shaanxi on **18 December 2018**, as part of a campaign against what officials termed the "pan-halal tendency" (泛清真化, *fàn qīngzhēn huà*) — the extension of halal labelling to non-food goods. Gansu's abolition was reported by *China Daily*; Ningxia's label change by *Global Times*. Export-facing certification continued; domestic identification did not.

:::note What that means when you are standing outside a restaurant
Not that the sign is meaningless — a 清真 restaurant in a Hui neighbourhood is run by and for a community that would notice immediately if it were not observant, and that social accountability is real. But it is community accountability rather than an audited national certificate, and guidance telling you to "look for the certification" is describing a system that was dismantled in the three provinces where it mattered most.

Judge the establishment: who runs it, who eats there, whether it is in a Muslim quarter, whether alcohol is served. Those signals do more work here than a label.
:::

## The thing that makes China easy: Lanzhou beef noodles

**Lanzhou beef noodle shops** (兰州牛肉面, *Lánzhōu niúròumiàn*, often signed 兰州拉面, *Lánzhōu lāmiàn*) are the answer to almost every "what do I eat tonight" problem in China. They are Hui-run, halal by tradition and construction, small, cheap, open long hours, and present in effectively every Chinese city — in the big ones by the thousand, run by families from Gansu and Qinghai.

If you are in a city with no Muslim quarter and no obvious options, this is the fallback that exists anyway. The noodles are hand-pulled to order, the broth is beef, and there is no pork on the premises.

The wider tradition it belongs to — lamb and beef, wheat rather than rice, cumin, hand-pulled noodles and flatbread — is one the standard eight-cuisine framework leaves out entirely, which [the food pillar covers in its own right](/food/beyond-the-eight-cuisines/).

## Where it is easy, and where it takes planning

**Easy:** Xinjiang, Ningxia, Gansu, Qinghai and Shaanxi, where halal cooking is not a minority option but the region's main food culture. Xi'an's Muslim Quarter and Yinchuan are the obvious cases, and in much of the northwest the question inverts — you are looking for the non-halal restaurant.

**Straightforward:** any large city. Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Chengdu, Kunming and the rest all have Muslim quarters, Xinjiang restaurants and the Lanzhou shops.

**Takes planning:** small towns and rural areas in the south and east, and the parts of the country where pork is not merely present but structural — Hunan, Jiangnan, Guangdong, and the pork-centred end of Sichuan cooking.

## Where pork hides

Outside halal establishments, pork is the default meat in Chinese cooking, and it is not always the visible ingredient. The traps are the same ones [vegetarians run into](/food/vegetarian-vegan-in-china/):

- **Lard** as a cooking fat, including in vegetable dishes
- **Pork stock** (高汤, *gāotāng*) as the base of soups and sauces that name no meat at all
- **Dried shrimp, oyster sauce and pork floss** as seasoning agents scattered over things
- **Dumpling fillings** where pork-and-cabbage is the unstated default

The reliable phrase is 我不吃猪肉 (*wǒ bù chī zhūròu*) — "I don't eat pork" — but the same logic that governs [any serious dietary constraint in a Chinese kitchen](/food/food-allergies-in-china/) applies: a written card in Chinese characters is understood where a spoken request in a busy restaurant may not be, and eating in a 清真 establishment removes the problem rather than negotiating it.

## Mosques, prayer and visiting

China has mosques in every city with a Muslim community, and the historic ones are among the most interesting buildings in the country — the **Great Mosque of Xi'an**, built entirely in Chinese architectural idiom rather than Arab, **Niujie Mosque** in Beijing, **Id Kah** in Kashgar, and the **Qingjing Mosque** in Quanzhou, which dates to 1009 and was built in stone by Persian and Arab merchants. The [religion guide](/culture/religion-and-philosophy-in-china/) covers how Islam arrived and where the communities sit.

For visiting, the pattern is consistent across the well-known ones:

- **Courtyards and grounds are open to visitors** on a ticket, and are what tourists see.
- **Prayer halls are generally restricted to Muslims.** If you are visiting to pray rather than to sightsee, you are in a different category from the tourists in the courtyard — say so.
- **Dress modestly**, keep quiet, and ask before photographing anyone praying. Shorts and sandals are refused at some mosques.
- **Friday prayers** change the atmosphere and the access at all of them.

:::warn Older guidance about specific mosques may be out of date
Since 2018 a national campaign to "sinicise" religious architecture has removed domes and minarets from a large number of mosques, particularly across the northwest, and some mosques have been closed, merged or altered. This was reported by NPR in 2021 and documented by Human Rights Watch in a 2023 report.

The practical consequence for a traveller is narrow and worth stating plainly: a mosque you have seen photographed may not look like its photograph, and one listed as open in older guidance may not be. Check locally, close to the time, rather than relying on any published list — including this page.
:::

## Ramadan, alcohol and the social side

**Ramadan** is observed, and it changes opening hours and food availability in Muslim areas. Its effect is most pronounced in Xinjiang, where the [practicalities of travelling in the region](/answers/can-foreigners-travel-to-xinjiang/) already need more planning than elsewhere in China — including a local time convention that runs two hours off Beijing time, which matters a great deal when you are timing a fast.

**Alcohol** is the one social friction worth preparing for. Chinese business and hospitality culture builds around the shared table, and a hosted dinner may involve toasting with baijiu. Declining is entirely accepted when done early and clearly, and toasting with tea is a recognised substitute rather than an awkward improvisation — [how to decline without causing offence](/answers/what-is-baijiu-and-do-i-have-to-drink-it/) is a question the site answers for everyone, and the answer works here.

Halal restaurants do not serve alcohol, which is also a useful signal when you are assessing an unfamiliar place.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it easy to find halal food in China?

Easier than most visitors expect. Hui-run Lanzhou beef noodle shops are halal and present in effectively every Chinese city, the northwest is a halal food culture rather than a niche within one, and every large city has a Muslim quarter and Xinjiang restaurants. Small towns in the south and east are where it takes planning.

### What does the 清真 sign mean in China?

清真 (qīngzhēn) is the Chinese word for halal, and also for Muslim or Islamic more broadly. It marks restaurants, shops and packaged food. It is a claim made by the establishment and backed by the community around it rather than by an audited national certificate, because China has no national halal standard.

### Is halal food certified in China?

Not nationally. A national standard was halted in April 2016, certification was left to provinces, and three of the most significant — Ningxia in 2017, Gansu and Shaanxi in December 2018 — then abolished their halal food identification standards under a campaign against the "pan-halal tendency". Export-facing certification continued; domestic identification did not.

### Can non-Muslims visit mosques in China?

Yes, at the well-known historic ones — courtyards and grounds are open on a ticket. Prayer halls are generally restricted to Muslims. Dress modestly, stay quiet, ask before photographing worshippers, and expect access to differ around Friday prayers.

### How do I say I don't eat pork in Chinese?

我不吃猪肉 (wǒ bù chī zhūròu). Carry it written in Chinese characters as well as saying it, because pork appears as lard and as stock in dishes that name no meat, and a written card is understood in a busy kitchen where a spoken request may not be. Eating at a 清真 restaurant avoids the question entirely.
