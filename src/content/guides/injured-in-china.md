Two neighbouring cases are already covered here. If something is life-threatening, [the four emergency numbers and how to get the call answered](/answers/what-are-the-emergency-numbers-in-china/) is the page you want. If you are unwell and need to see a doctor, [the health guide describes how a Chinese hospital is organised](/guides/health-and-safety-in-china/) — register at a desk, pay before each step, queue by department.

This is the case in between, and it is the most common one: you have hurt yourself. Not dying, but not walking it off either.

## An injury is not an illness, administratively

The registration-and-queue description above is the **门诊** (*ménzhěn*, outpatient) route, and it is the wrong door for an accident. An injury goes to **急诊** (*jízhěn*, the emergency department), which Chinese health regulation treats as a separate pathway with its own documentation and its own order of business.

Large hospitals sign the two separately, and they are often in different parts of the building. Walking into the outpatient hall with a bleeding hand and joining the registration queue is a common and expensive mistake in time.

## You are triaged, not queued

This is the thing to know before you are sitting in one, because it is the opposite of what the outpatient description would lead you to expect.

Chinese emergency departments operate **预检分诊** (*yùjiǎn fēnzhěn*, triage screening). The national definition, in a National Health Commission notice of 4 September 2024, sets four levels and states plainly what they are for — determining the priority order in which emergency patients are seen and dealt with:

> Ⅰ级为急危患者，需要立即得到救治；Ⅱ级为急重患者，需要评估与救治同时进行；Ⅲ级为急症患者，需要在短时间内得到救治；Ⅳ级为亚急症或非急症患者。

Level I is critical and treated immediately. Level II is severe, with assessment and treatment running together. Level III is urgent and must be treated within a short time. Level IV is sub-urgent or non-urgent.

**Arrival order does not decide anything.** Severity does. A twisted ankle, a cut that needs closing, a suspected small fracture — these are level IV, and level IV waits behind every level I, II and III that walks in after you.

Beijing's health commission put concrete targets on this in a municipal scheme of 22 August 2019: level I resuscitated immediately, level II treated within ten minutes, level III given priority over level IV and re-assessed if it waits more than thirty minutes, and level IV seen in sequence and re-assessed if it waits more than four hours. Those figures are Beijing's own rather than national, and the level names differ slightly from the 2024 national wording — but the four-tier shape and the severity-first principle are the same everywhere.

:::tip What this changes about your afternoon
Budget the whole afternoon, and eat something first if you can. A minor injury in a busy urban emergency department is a long wait by design, not a sign that you have been forgotten or overlooked as a foreigner.
:::

## Will anyone actually help you?

Worth answering, because a lot of foreign visitors arrive having read that Chinese bystanders do not intervene — a reputation built on a handful of widely reported cases in which people who helped an injured stranger were later blamed for the injury.

The law settled this. Article 184 of the **中华人民共和国民法典** (*Zhōnghuá Rénmín Gònghéguó Mínfǎdiǎn*, Civil Code of the People's Republic of China) says:

> 因自愿实施紧急救助行为造成受助人损害的，救助人不承担民事责任。

Where damage is caused to the person being assisted as a result of **voluntary emergency assistance**, the rescuer **bears no civil liability**. It is stated without condition — no proviso, no negligence carve-out — which is why it is known in Chinese as the 好人条款 (*hǎorén tiáokuǎn*), the good-person clause.

That is the reason behind something this site already tells you: that handing your phone to a bystander is the fastest route to a dispatched ambulance. People do help, and the person who stops to help you is not taking a legal risk by doing so.

## The practical sequence

1. **Decide which problem you have.** Life-threatening goes to the numbers page. Anything else goes to an emergency department.
2. **Get there by car if you can.** An ambulance is billed and, in city traffic, is [frequently slower than simply going](/answers/what-are-the-emergency-numbers-in-china/) — that page covers the trade-off and when the ambulance is still right.
3. **Go to a large hospital.** The bigger the hospital, the better the emergency department and the likelier that someone on the floor speaks some English.
4. **Expect to pay before treatment**, as at every step of Chinese hospital care. The health guide covers the payment sequence and the international clinics that bill insurers directly.
5. **Show, do not describe.** Point at it. Have your hotel address in Chinese in your phone, and a translation app open for the history.
6. **Keep every receipt and printout.** Chinese hospitals hand you the paperwork and expect you to carry it; your insurer will want all of it.

Outdoors, one more rule applies that does not apply in a city: in some scenic areas [you can be billed for your own rescue](/guides/hiking-in-china/) if you were somewhere not open to the public when it happened. That is decided area by area rather than nationally, and it is a real reason to stay inside the marked route.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where do I go if I am injured in China?

The emergency department — 急诊 — not the outpatient hall. Chinese hospitals treat the two as separate pathways with separate signage and often separate entrances, and the registration-and-queue routine described for outpatients does not apply in emergency.

### Why am I waiting so long in a Chinese emergency department?

Because you are being seen by severity rather than arrival order. Chinese emergency departments triage into four levels, and a minor injury is level four — the lowest priority, behind every more urgent case that arrives after you. A long wait is the system working as designed rather than a sign you have been overlooked.

### Will Chinese bystanders help an injured stranger?

Generally yes, and the law explicitly protects them. Article 184 of the Civil Code states that someone who causes harm while voluntarily giving emergency assistance bears no civil liability — an unconditional provision known as the good-person clause, enacted to settle exactly the hesitation that older reporting described.

### Should I call an ambulance in China or take a taxi?

It depends on how serious it is. Ambulances are billed and in dense city traffic are often slower than a car, so for an injury that is not life-threatening many people simply go. [The emergency numbers page](/answers/what-are-the-emergency-numbers-in-china/) sets out the four numbers, the language problem and when the ambulance is still the right call.

### Do I have to pay before treatment in a Chinese hospital?

Yes — payment comes before each step rather than afterwards, in emergency as in outpatient care. [The health and safety guide](/guides/health-and-safety-in-china/) covers how the payment sequence works and which international clinics bill insurers directly instead.

### Can I be charged for a mountain rescue in China?

In some places, yes, where you were in an area not open to the public. It is applied scenic area by scenic area rather than under one national rule — [the hiking guide sets out how that works](/guides/hiking-in-china/) and why the boundary of the open area is a legal line rather than a suggestion.
