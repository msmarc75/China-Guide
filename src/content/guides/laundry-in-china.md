Any China trip beyond about ten days runs into the same problem, and almost no guidance addresses it: at some point you have to wash your clothes. The advice to pack light depends entirely on being able to.

You can. But the mental model most visitors bring — find a laundromat, or pay the hotel a lot — gets both halves wrong.

## The price ladder runs backwards

In most countries the more you pay for a room, the more laundry is looked after for you. China inverts that.

Chinese domestic hotel chains in the budget and midrange tiers commonly provide a **自助洗衣房** (*zì zhù xǐ yī fáng*, self-service laundry room): a couple of commercial machines, sometimes a dryer, **洗衣液** (*xǐ yī yè*, liquid detergent) provided, and a drying rack. International luxury properties are the ones most likely to have no guest machines at all and to bill instead by the piece, from a printed list, on a next-day turnaround.

That is not a rumour spread by backpackers. It is a competitive front the Chinese chains have been fighting on openly. Sichuan Online reported in November 2024 that guest laundry had become a battleground between the big domestic brands, with some properties running the machines free around the clock and adding drying racks, laundry baskets and sewing kits to the room — and that the whole push was a response to students and young renters booking **钟点房** (*zhōng diǎn fáng*, hourly rooms) purely to use the hotel's washing machine, because it turned around in a few hours rather than a few days.

The practical instruction that falls out of this is counterintuitive but reliable: **when you are choosing where to break a long itinerary, check the mid-tier Chinese chain for a laundry room before you check the international brand.** Where a specific property has one, its own listing normally says so; where the hotel does not, ask reception rather than assume, because it varies property by property rather than by brand.

## Laundromats are not the answer

Do not plan around finding one. Standalone coin or card laundromats — the self-contained shopfront full of machines that is ordinary in Europe, North America and Japan — never became a normal part of the Chinese street. Domestic washing-machine ownership is close to universal — national statistics for the end of 2021 are reported at around 100 machines per 100 urban households and 96 per 100 rural ones — so the demand that sustains laundromats elsewhere was absorbed at home long ago.

What exists instead are shared machines placed where people do not have their own — university dormitory blocks, staff housing, some apartment compounds and some hostels — operated by scanning a QR code with Alipay or WeChat Pay, the [same payment layer as everything else](/guides/money-and-payments-in-china/). Those are not aimed at visitors and you are unlikely to stumble across one.

:::tip Plan for air, not heat
Assume no dryer. Domestic Chinese laundry practice is built around hanging clothes out, and tumble dryers are a far smaller part of the household picture than in the United States or northern Europe. Even where a hotel laundry room has one, it may be shared between many rooms. Quick-drying fabrics are worth more in China than extra changes of clothes — which is why [the packing list](/plan/china-packing-list/) puts them in the summer column.
:::

## The dry cleaner, and the ¥2,000 rule

Dry cleaning — **干洗** (*gān xǐ*) — is easy to find, cheap by Western standards and generally good. It is also governed by a specific set of rules that decide what happens when something goes wrong, and the default outcome is worse than most visitors would guess.

The trade is regulated by the **Measures for the Administration of the Laundering and Dyeing Industry** (洗染业管理办法, *xǐ rǎn yè guǎn lǐ bàn fǎ*), issued jointly by the Ministry of Commerce, the State Administration for Industry and Commerce and the State Environmental Protection Administration as Order No. 5 of 11 May 2007 and in force since 1 July 2007. Three of its articles matter to you.

**Article 13 puts the inspection duty on the shop.** Before accepting a garment the operator is required to check it with you — prompting you to empty the pockets, confirming that buttons and trim are complete, pointing out delicate or fragile fittings, and telling you where a stain will not come out or where the fabric will change. If nobody does any of that, the shop is skipping the step that protects you.

**Article 15 makes the ticket the contract.** The **服务单据** (*fú wù dān jù*, service docket) the shop hands you is not a cloakroom stub. The regulation requires it to record the garment's name, quantity and colour, *any existing damage or missing parts*, the service being performed, the price, the drop-off and collection dates, the storage period, anything else agreed between you, and how disputes are to be resolved. Read the damage line before you leave. It is the only written statement of what condition the garment was in when you handed it over.

**Article 14 is the one nobody offers you.** It provides for **保值清洗** (*bǎo zhí qīng xǐ*), value-declared cleaning: you and the shop agree in writing on a declared value for the item, along with a cleaning fee to match, and if the shop then damages or loses it, compensation is measured against that declared value.

Here is why that matters. Where there is *no* written value agreement, the laundry trade's national dispute-resolution rules treat the item as an ordinary garment, and compensation for an ordinary garment is **capped at ¥2,000** however much it cost. This is not theoretical: in a case decided in May 2022, the Kaifu District People's Court in Changsha heard a customer whose ¥18,800 coat had been ruined and who asked for half its price. The court awarded ¥2,000, on the grounds that no value-declared agreement had been made.

:::warn If the garment is worth more than ¥2,000, say so at the counter
A suit, a coat or a dress worth more than the cap needs a written value agreement made *when you hand it in* — afterwards is too late. Ask for 保值清洗 by name. If the shop will not do it, that is useful information, and the sensible response is to carry the item home unwashed rather than to hope. This is the single most expensive thing that can go wrong with laundry in China, and it falls hardest on [business travellers](/guides/business-travel-in-china/), who are the people most likely to hand over something costly.
:::

Article 19 covers the more ordinary failure. Where the shop is at fault and the result misses the agreed standard, or the garment is damaged or lost, it must re-do the work, refund the fee or compensate you. Where the fault lies in the garment itself — a misleading care label, or manufacturing below national standards — the shop is not liable. That second half is worth knowing before you argue.

## What to actually do

- **Wash where you sleep, mid-trip.** Build it into a rest day rather than a travel day; a wash plus air-drying is an overnight job, not a two-hour one.
- **Check the laundry room before you book**, and treat a cheap Chinese chain as more likely to have one than a five-star international.
- **Carry a little detergent** as insurance. It is sold in every convenience store and supermarket, so this is a convenience rather than a necessity.
- **Use the dry cleaner for the things a machine would wreck**, read the docket, and declare a value on anything expensive.
- **Do not send out anything you cannot afford to lose**, which is the honest reading of a ¥2,000 default cap.
- **Leave hand-washing room in your plans.** Every hotel room has a kettle, a sink and somewhere to hang things — for socks, underwear and a shirt, that is the whole solution.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where can I do laundry in China as a tourist?

At your hotel, in most cases. Budget and midrange Chinese chains commonly have a self-service laundry room with machines and detergent provided, sometimes free. Standalone laundromats are rare in China, so a hotel laundry room or the hotel's own send-out service is the realistic option in most cities.

### Are there laundromats in China?

Not as a normal feature of the street. Domestic washing-machine ownership in China is close to universal, so the coin-laundromat shopfront familiar in Europe, North America and Japan never established itself. Shared machines exist in university dormitories, some apartment compounds and some hostels, unlocked by scanning a QR code, but they are not aimed at visitors.

### How much does hotel laundry cost in China?

It depends entirely on the format. A self-service machine in a Chinese chain hotel is cheap or included; an international luxury hotel charges per item from a printed list and is far more expensive. Hostel and hotel send-out laundry is charged by the load — [the 21-day itinerary](/itineraries/china-21-day-itinerary/) gives the typical range for a long trip.

### Is dry cleaning in China safe for expensive clothes?

Only if you declare the value in writing first. Without a written value-declared cleaning agreement — 保值清洗 — the laundry trade's dispute rules treat the garment as ordinary and cap compensation at ¥2,000, whatever it cost. A Changsha court applied exactly that cap in 2022 to a ¥18,800 coat.

### Do Chinese hotels have tumble dryers?

Some do, but do not assume it. Air-drying is the norm in Chinese households and dryers are much less common than in the United States or northern Europe. Where a hotel laundry room has a dryer it may be shared across the whole floor, so plan for clothes to dry overnight on a rack.

### What should I check on a Chinese dry-cleaning ticket?

The line recording existing damage or missing parts. Chinese regulations require the docket to record the garment's name, quantity, colour and pre-existing condition, the service, the price, the drop-off and collection dates and the storage period. That damage line is the only written record of what state the garment was in when you handed it over.
