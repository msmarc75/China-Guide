Same-sex activity between adults is legal in mainland China, it is not policed, and no visa question, entry form or hotel check-in asks about it. That is the short answer, and it has been stable for nearly thirty years.

The longer answer is that the direction of travel since 2016 has been the other way, and it has moved through censorship and civil society rather than through criminal law. Films, streaming series, university societies, Pride events and — most recently — dating apps have been closed off one at a time, while the legal position underneath has not changed at all. This page sets out what is verified and skips what is not.

## What the law says

**Legal since 1997.** The 1997 revision of the Criminal Law removed the offence of hooliganism (流氓罪, *liúmáng zuì*), which had been the provision under which same-sex conduct was prosecuted. It is worth being precise about what that was: the older law targeted specific acts rather than an identity, so 1997 removed a vague catch-all rather than repealing a ban on being gay. Either way, since 1997 there has been no criminal provision to apply.

**Removed from the psychiatric manual in 2001, incompletely.** The standard line is that China depathologised homosexuality in 2001, when the third edition of the Chinese Classification of Mental Disorders was published. That is half right. Homosexuality as such came out of the disorder list, but CCMD-3 kept a diagnosis for people distressed by their own orientation, filed under sexual orientation disorders — and China has never banned conversion therapy, which is still advertised and still sold. Treat "depathologised in 2001" as a headline that does not survive contact with the document.

**No marriage, no civil union, no partnership.** The Civil Code, in force since 1 January 2021, frames marriage in terms of a man and a woman, and there is no alternative status of any kind. This is not an oversight anyone is waiting to correct: asked directly in August 2019 whether the then-draft Civil Code would allow same-sex marriage, the spokesman for the legislature's legal affairs commission said Chinese law permits marriage only between one man and one woman, and that the rule suits the country's national conditions and cultural traditions.

**No anti-discrimination protection.** Chinese labour law prohibits discrimination on grounds including ethnicity and sex, and the Employment Promotion Law prohibits discriminatory hiring, but neither instrument mentions sexual orientation or gender identity. There is no statute a person could sue under on those grounds.

:::note One thing that does exist, and matters
Since 1 October 2017 any adult with full legal capacity has been able to appoint a guardian of their own choosing by notarised agreement (意定监护, *yìdìng jiānhù*), regardless of their relationship to that person. Same-sex couples use it — it can cover medical decisions and the handling of remains. It is genuinely useful and it is genuinely not marriage: it does not create next-of-kin status, and a family of origin can contest it in court. For a visitor it is context rather than a tool, since it is a Chinese notarial instrument.
:::

## What changed after 2016

The legal position has held still. Everything around it has not, and this sequence is the actual answer to "what is it like now".

- **2016** — the broadcast regulator's content rules for television drama listed same-sex relationships among "abnormal sexual relationships and behaviours" not to be depicted.
- **2017** — industry guidelines for online audiovisual content extended the same prohibition to web series, short films, animation and documentaries.
- **2020** — ShanghaiPRIDE, the mainland's longest-running and largest Pride event, announced an indefinite halt after eleven years, citing safety.
- **2021** — in July, more than twenty WeChat accounts run by university LGBT student societies were deleted on the same evening, without stated reason.
- **2023** — in May, the Beijing LGBT Center closed after fifteen years, citing circumstances beyond its control.
- **2025** — in November, the Cyberspace Administration of China directed that Blued and Finka, the two largest domestic gay dating apps, be removed from app stores. Apple confirmed it complied for the China storefront.

None of that is enforcement against individuals. All of it is the removal of shared space, and the cumulative effect is a country where the subject is close to absent from public media while private life carries on largely unremarked.

## The apps, which is the practical part

If you use dating apps, assume they will not work the way they do at home, and sort it out before you land rather than after.

**Grindr has not been available in Chinese app stores since January 2022.** Accounts of why differ — some reporting tied it to a content-purge campaign before the Beijing Winter Olympics, while the company was reported as saying it withdrew over the difficulty of complying with China's Personal Information Protection Law. The two accounts disagree on the reason and agree on the effect.

**Blued and Finka went in November 2025**, on the CAC directive above. Reporting at the time noted that both kept working for people who already had them installed, and that a lite version of Blued remained on Chinese marketplaces. That is a situation that can change without notice, which is the point: do not plan on a specific app being downloadable once you are in the country.

The general rule that governs all of this is the one that governs every app in China — [what works depends on what you arranged before arriving](/guides/internet-vpn-apps-china/), because the app store you reach is the Chinese one, tied to the region of your Apple or Google account.

## Day to day, as a visitor

**Nobody asks and nothing on paper records it.** Visa forms, arrival cards and hotel registration ask for your nationality, passport number and address. The hotel desk that takes your passport at check-in is [registering you with the local police](/guides/accommodation-in-china/), which is a document procedure applied to every foreign guest; it is not an assessment of who you are travelling with.

**Public affection is read through a different norm than you may expect.** Chinese cities are not demonstrative places for anyone — heterosexual couples are restrained in public too, and the visible warmth in public between friends of the same sex is ordinary and carries no implication. The practical consequence is that discretion here is not the concession it would be in a country where the alternative is a confrontation; it is the local default.

**Big cities and small cities are different countries on this, as on most things.** Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu and Guangzhou have visible scenes that a resident could point you to. This guide will not name bars, clubs or districts, and the reason is the same discipline applied everywhere else on this site: venues of this kind open and close fast, and a stale listing sends someone across a city at night to a shut door. Ask at a hostel or use a current local listing rather than any travel guide, including this one.

**Trans travellers face a document question rather than a legal one.** The problem to anticipate is the ordinary one of a passport, a booking and a face being checked against each other at a rail gate, a hotel desk or airport security, all of which happen constantly in China. Carrying documents that agree with one another matters more here than in a country with fewer identity checks.

## Health, HIV and medication

**China lifted its entry ban on foreign nationals with HIV in April 2010**, by a State Council decision amending the entry-and-exit implementation measures. There is no HIV-related entry restriction on visitors.

Bring any medication in its original labelled packaging with a copy of the prescription. That is the standing rule for [everything you cannot easily replace locally](/plan/china-packing-list/) and it applies with more force to anything you cannot interrupt for the length of a trip. Do not count on filling a foreign prescription in a Chinese pharmacy.

## Hong Kong is a different jurisdiction

Hong Kong has its own legal system and a different answer, and the answer is currently unsettled.

In 2023 the Court of Final Appeal ruled that the government had to establish, within two years, an alternative framework for legally recognising same-sex partnerships — with a deadline of 27 October 2025. A bill to register partnerships formed abroad, offering rights limited largely to medical decisions, hospital visits and the handling of remains, was gazetted in July 2025 and voted down by the Legislative Council on 10 September 2025 by 71 votes to 14. The Chief Executive said afterwards that the government would look at administrative measures instead. Marriage is not available in Hong Kong either, and a visitor's practical experience there is more relaxed than on the mainland — but the legal framework the court ordered does not yet exist.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it illegal to be gay in China?

No. Same-sex activity between consenting adults has not been a criminal offence since the 1997 revision of the Criminal Law removed the hooliganism provision, and there is no law against it today. There is also no legal recognition of same-sex relationships and no protection against discrimination.

### Is China safe for gay travellers?

There is no policing of same-sex activity and no entry, visa or hotel process that asks about it, and violent crime against foreigners is rare across the board. What has narrowed is public visibility — LGBT media, university societies, Pride events and dating apps have been closed off one by one since 2016 — rather than the treatment of individuals.

### Can a same-sex couple share a hotel room in China?

Nothing in the check-in procedure asks about a relationship. The hotel is scanning passports to register foreign guests with the police, a requirement that applies to every foreign guest regardless of who they are travelling with, and the more relevant question for any traveller is whether the hotel is licensed to accept foreign passports at all.

### Does Grindr work in China?

Grindr has not been in Chinese app stores since January 2022, and in November 2025 the Cyberspace Administration directed that the two largest domestic apps, Blued and Finka, also be removed. Anything you plan to use should be installed and working before you arrive, because the app store you reach in China is tied to your account's region.

### Is same-sex marriage legal anywhere in China?

Not in mainland China, where the Civil Code frames marriage in terms of a man and a woman and no civil union or partnership status exists. Hong Kong has no marriage either: a court ordered an alternative recognition framework by October 2025, but the bill to create one was voted down by the Legislative Council in September 2025.
