Rail covers most of China and flying covers the rest, which is why [the transport guide](/guides/transport-in-china/) gives the coach a single paragraph. But in the places where the bus is the answer — the Yunnan and Guizhou highlands, the last leg to a national park, anywhere the track has not reached — it is the *only* answer, and it runs on rules that resemble neither the train nor the plane.

## Buying the ticket

Chinese scheduled coach routes are sorted into four classes, and the class decides whether the ticket is real-name. The classification is administrative rather than by distance — Article 8 of the Road Passenger Transport and Coach Station Provisions (道路旅客运输及客运站管理规定, *Dàolù Lǚkè Yùnshū jí Kèyùnzhàn Guǎnlǐ Guīdìng*) sorts routes by which boundaries they cross.

| Your route | Real-name ticketing |
| --- | --- |
| Crosses a provincial border | **Required** |
| Within a province, crosses a prefecture-level city boundary | **Required** |
| Within a prefecture, crosses a county boundary | The province decides |
| Within one county, or between adjacent counties | The province decides |

For a visitor this collapses to something simple: **the long-distance coach you are taking almost certainly requires a real-name ticket**, because any journey worth calling long-distance crosses at least a prefecture boundary. Article 38 requires the buyer to present the **original** identity document — for a foreign passenger, the passport itself, not a photograph or a photocopy — and the seller records the passenger's identity on the ticket. The same identity is checked again at boarding.

Buy at the station ticket counter, which is the reliable route for a foreign passport. Local coach mini-programmes inside Alipay sell some routes, but the counter is where a passport is unambiguously handled.

## If you lose the ticket, it is not gone

This is the part worth knowing in advance, because the instinct is to assume the money is lost.

Article 38 closes with an obligation on the seller: where a passenger loses their ticket, then after the seller has verified their identity information, **the ticket must be reissued free of charge**. The real-name system that makes buying slightly more laborious is exactly what makes losing the ticket survivable — your identity is on file against that seat, so the paper is a receipt rather than the entitlement itself.

Go back to the ticket window that issued it, with the passport you bought it with.

## Why your night coach stops at three in the morning

Passengers on overnight sleeper coaches in China regularly find the bus parked at a motorway service area in the small hours with the engine off, and assume something has gone wrong. Nothing has. It is the law.

The Road Passenger Transport Enterprise Safety Management Standards, issued jointly by the Ministry of Transport, the Ministry of Public Security and the Ministry of Emergency Management on 11 November 2023, require that **long-distance coaches stop running between 02:00 and 05:00, or else operate 接驳运输** (*jiē bó yùnshū*, relay transport) — a scheme in which a fresh driver takes over at a designated handover point so that nobody is at the wheel through the dangerous hours.

The exemptions are narrow and none of them is a long-distance coach: fixed-route airport and high-speed-rail express services, commuter charters, customised passenger services, and short-haul shuttles running under 200 km one way.

So build it into the plan. An overnight coach schedule already accounts for the three-hour pause, but it means an advertised journey time is not a driving time, and it means the small hours are spent in a lay-by rather than moving.

## Luggage, and what the regulation does not say

There is no national free baggage allowance for coaches written into the Provisions — unlike rail, where [the allowance and the size limits are set nationally](/guides/luggage-and-left-luggage-in-china/), the coach operator sets its own. Do not carry a figure in your head; ask at the counter if you have something large.

What the regulation does address is what may not travel. Article 41 requires operators to refuse consignments that the law prohibits from being carried, that could endanger transport safety, or that the sender refuses to have security-screened. Coach stations screen bags the way stations and airports do, and the same categories cause the same trouble — lighters, large power banks, anything flammable.

## What is actually worth knowing at the station

- **Arrive with time.** Real-name verification at boarding is a queue, not a formality.
- **Buy inside the station.** People selling seats outside are not selling you the regulated product, and you lose the reissue protection above along with everything else.
- **Judge the vehicle, not the route.** Modern inter-city sleeper coaches are comfortable and well run; rural minibuses on mountain roads are a different experience, and in [Yunnan](/destinations/yunnan/) you will meet both on the same trip.
- **Allow for the roads.** Mountain routes are slow in a way that flat-country intuition does not predict, and a 200 km leg can take most of a day.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can foreigners buy long-distance bus tickets in China?

Yes, using your passport. Routes that cross a provincial or prefecture boundary must be sold under the real-name system, so you present the original passport at the counter and the seller records your identity on the ticket. Buy at the station ticket window rather than from anyone selling seats outside it.

### Do I need my passport for a bus in China?

For any long-distance route, yes, and the original document rather than a copy or a photograph. The regulation requires the buyer to present the original identity document at purchase, and the same identity is verified again when you board.

### What happens if I lose my bus ticket in China?

The seller has to reissue it free of charge once it has verified your identity, because your details are recorded against the seat under the real-name system. Return to the ticket window that issued it, with the passport you bought it with.

### Why do Chinese sleeper buses stop in the middle of the night?

Because national safety rules require long-distance coaches either to stop running between 02:00 and 05:00 or to hand over to a relief driver at a designated point. The pause is built into the timetable, so an advertised journey time includes roughly three hours of not moving.

### How much luggage can I take on a Chinese long-distance bus?

There is no national allowance for coaches — the operator sets its own, unlike on the railways where the limits are set nationally. Ask at the ticket counter if you are carrying something large, and expect bags to be security-screened at the station.

### Are long-distance buses in China safe?

Inter-city coaches are regulated tightly, screened at stations, and subject to the overnight driving restriction above. The variable is the vehicle rather than the system: modern sleeper coaches on main routes are a reasonable way to travel, while rural minibuses on mountain roads are the part of Chinese transport that most rewards patience.
