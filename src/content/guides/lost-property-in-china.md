Two neighbouring situations are already covered here. If your passport has gone, [that is a specific procedure with a specific order](/guides/theft-and-lost-passport-in-china/) and you should follow it. If you want to put a bag down for the day, [left luggage is its own system](/guides/luggage-and-left-luggage-in-china/).

This page is the ordinary case that sits between them: you still have your documents, but the phone is in the taxi, or the jacket is on the rack of a train now approaching Nanjing.

## Lost is not stolen, and the difference matters

Treat these as different problems with different first moves. A theft goes to the police, and if a passport is involved the order of the steps decides how long the rest of your trip takes. A genuine loss goes to **whoever operates the place you lost it** — the railway, the station, the hotel, the venue — because that is where it will physically be.

Getting this the wrong way round costs time. Reporting a forgotten umbrella to 110 achieves nothing; asking a train guard about a stolen wallet at the moment you should be at a police station costs you the report you actually need.

## Why the odds are better than you think

There is a legal reason the Chinese system tends to produce results, and it is worth knowing because it explains the behaviour you will meet.

Article 314 of the **中华人民共和国民法典** (*Zhōnghuá Rénmín Gònghéguó Mínfǎdiǎn*, Civil Code of the People's Republic of China) is direct about what a finder must do:

> 拾得遗失物，应当返还权利人。拾得人应当及时通知权利人领取，或者送交公安等有关部门。

Found lost property **shall be returned to the person entitled to it**. The finder must promptly notify that person to collect it, or hand it in to the public security authorities or another relevant department.

Article 315 then puts a duty on the receiving body: if it knows who the owner is, it must notify them promptly; if it does not, it must **publish a lost-property notice**. And Article 318 sets the clock — property unclaimed for **one year** from the date of that notice becomes state property.

:::warn Not six months
A great deal of writing on this, including some Chinese-language material, still gives six months. That was the figure in the old Property Law. The Civil Code, in force since 2021, sets the period at **one year**, and that is the rule now.
:::

The practical upshot: found property in China is not casually kept or thrown away. It is logged and held, and the default institutional behaviour is to hold it for a long time and announce it.

## Trains: there is a national service for this

The railway is the one operator with a country-wide system, which is fortunate, since a train is where things most often go missing.

**Tell staff immediately.** China Railway's own guidance is to report it to railway staff at once, and it notes that busier stations have a **失物招领处** (*shīwù zhāolǐng chù*, lost-property office). If you are still on the train, the guard for your carriage is the right person and the right moment.

**Then use 12306.** The same account and app you booked with runs a lost-item registration and search service, reached through the 12306 app, its WeChat mini-programme or the website. You do not strictly need a registered account to start — the 12306 customer service line handles it by phone as an alternative — but if you booked your ticket you already have one.

**Describe it properly.** The form asks where you lost it and what it is, and the quality of that description is doing most of the work. Note the train number and the carriage and seat if you have them; your booking record has all three.

Items recovered by the railway are registered, kept and publicised under the railway's own passenger transport regulations, which is why a found bag does not simply vanish into a back room.

## Everywhere else

Outside the railway there is no single national desk, and the route is the operator's own.

- **Hotels** are the easiest case and the one most often forgotten. Ring the property directly rather than the booking site.
- **Ride-hailing and taxis** are handled in the app you booked with, which has the driver and the trip. This is the strongest argument for [booking the car in an app rather than flagging one down](/guides/transport-in-china/). A flagged-down street taxi is not hopeless either, provided you kept the fare receipt the driver is required to give you — [that, the taxi company and the 12328 hotline are the route](/guides/taxis-in-china/), and the driver is separately obliged to try to return what you left.
- **Metros, museums and attractions** run their own lost-property desks. These are locally administered and the arrangements differ city by city, so ask at the station or the site rather than assuming a national procedure.

Whatever the operator, the Civil Code duty above applies to whoever picked the thing up, which is the reason asking is worth the awkwardness.

## What to do in the first hour

1. **Work out where, not what.** The operator of the place decides which route you take.
2. **Report while the trail is warm** — the same carriage, the same shift, the same driver.
3. **Have the identifiers ready.** Train number, carriage and seat; the trip in the app; the room number and dates.
4. **Photograph what you carry**, before you travel. A picture of the bag is worth more at a lost-property counter than any description you can produce through a translation app.
5. **Ask even when it feels hopeless.** The one-year holding rule means the thing may well still be sitting somewhere weeks later.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I do if I leave something on a Chinese train?

Tell railway staff immediately — the carriage guard if you are still aboard, station staff if you are not — and then register the loss through the 12306 app, WeChat mini-programme, website or customer service line, which runs a national lost-item search. Larger stations also have a lost-property office. Have your train number, carriage and seat ready; your booking record shows all three.

### Do Chinese lost-property offices actually return things?

Usually, and there is a legal duty behind it. The Civil Code requires a finder to return lost property to its owner or hand it to the public security authorities, and requires the receiving body to notify the owner or publish a lost-property notice. Items are logged and held rather than discarded.

### How long is lost property kept in China?

One year from the date the lost-property notice is published, after which unclaimed property becomes state property under Article 318 of the Civil Code. Older guidance saying six months is quoting the superseded Property Law.

### What if my phone is left in a Chinese taxi?

Go through the app you booked the ride in, which holds the driver and trip details. For a flagged-down street taxi the equivalent is the fare receipt, which the driver is required to issue without being asked; with it you can reach the operator or the 12328 transport hotline. Without it an anonymous cab really is hard to trace.

### Is losing something the same as reporting a theft in China?

No, and mixing them wastes time. A loss goes to whoever operates the place you lost it, because that is where the item physically is. A theft goes to the police — and if your passport was taken, [the order of the steps decides how long the rest of your trip takes](/guides/theft-and-lost-passport-in-china/).
