An overnight train in China is not one product. It is a set of fare classes that differ more from each other than economy and business differ on a plane, and the choice is made in a dropdown most visitors click past.

## The two conventional classes

Sleepers run on the lettered conventional services rather than on the high-speed G trains — the [prefix system that sorts Chinese trains](/guides/transport-in-china/) explains which letter is which.

**软卧** (*ruǎnwò*, soft sleeper) is a compartment of **four berths with a door that closes**, two lower and two upper. It is the quiet option and the one to take if you are travelling as a pair or a family, because four berths is a bookable unit.

**硬卧** (*yìngwò*, hard sleeper) is an open bay of **six berths**, stacked three high on each side, with no door and a corridor running along the window side. "Hard" is a historical name rather than a description of the mattress; the padding is similar. What differs is privacy and noise.

There is also a high-speed sleeper, **动卧** (*dòngwò*), running on a few long routes as a distinct product with its own compartment layout. Treat it as a third thing rather than as a faster version of the above, and check the specific service rather than any general description of it, including this one.

## The berth level is a fare class

This is the part nobody tells visitors, and it is the whole decision.

**Upper, middle and lower berths are priced differently**, and you choose which one you are buying. The railway's own fare rules treat middle and lower berths as separate fare items and price compartment-style hard sleeper at a further **30% above the middle and lower hard-sleeper fare**. The berth is not an allocation handed to you at the door; it is what you bought.

What each one is actually like:

| Berth | The trade |
| --- | --- |
| **下铺** (*xiàpù*, lower) | Dearest, easiest to get into, most headroom — and it doubles as everyone's sofa in the evening in a hard sleeper |
| **中铺** (*zhōngpù*, middle) | The compromise. Hard sleeper only |
| **上铺** (*shàngpù*, upper) | Cheapest, most private, a climb, and the least headroom |

The lower berth's drawback is the one visitors do not anticipate. In an open hard-sleeper bay there is nowhere else to sit, so the lower bunk is where the compartment gathers until people turn in. If you want to lie down at nine, book upper. If you have a heavy bag or dislike ladders, book lower and accept the company.

## If the sleeper is sold out

Chinese trains sell **无座** (*wúzuò*, no seat) tickets — standing tickets. When seats on a service are gone, the booking system issues one automatically and the seat field on the order reads 无座. It is a valid ticket on that train with nowhere to sit, which on an overnight leg is a genuinely bad night rather than a minor inconvenience.

So a sold-out sleeper is not a wall you bounce off; it is a trap you can walk into by clicking through. Check what you have been sold before you pay. If it is standing, [changing or refunding a Chinese train ticket](/guides/missed-trains-and-refunds-in-china/) is cheap while you are still days out, and gets less cheap as the date approaches.

## Practical points for the night

- **Bring less than you think.** The [railway's baggage allowance and where the racks actually are](/guides/luggage-and-left-luggage-in-china/) matter more on a sleeper, because your bag shares the space you sleep in.
- **Compartments are mixed-gender by default**, which is normal and unremarkable in China — [what that means in practice, and the berth choice that follows from it](/guides/solo-and-female-travel-china/) is worth reading if it is a consideration.
- **Keep valuables in the bed with you**, not on the overhead rack.
- **Arriving at 07:00 is not the same as having a room.** Hotel check-in is hours away, which is why a sleeper saves a hotel night but [does not save you a whole day](/plan/how-many-days-in-china/).

The whole system runs on the **铁路旅客运输规程** (*tiělù lǚkè yùnshū guīchéng*, Railway Passenger Transport Regulations), issued as Ministry of Transport Order No. 37 of 2022 and in force since 1 January 2023. Little of it needs reading, but it is the thing station staff are applying.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between soft sleeper and hard sleeper in China?

Soft sleeper (软卧) is a four-berth compartment with a door that closes; hard sleeper (硬卧) is an open bay of six berths stacked three high, with no door. The mattresses are comparable — the difference is privacy, noise and the number of people sharing your space.

### Which berth should I book on a Chinese sleeper train?

Upper if you want privacy and the lowest fare and do not mind climbing; lower if you have heavy luggage or dislike ladders, accepting that in a hard sleeper the lower bunk is where the bay sits in the evening. Middle exists only in hard sleeper and is the compromise.

### Do upper and lower berths cost the same in China?

No. The berth level is a fare class you select when booking rather than an allocation, and the railway's fare rules price berth levels separately. Upper berths are the cheapest tier of a given sleeper class.

### What happens if the sleeper is sold out?

The system will sell you a standing ticket — 无座, literally "no seat" — automatically, and the seat field on your order will say so. It is valid travel on that train with nowhere to sit. Check what you have bought before paying rather than after boarding.

### Are Chinese sleeper compartments mixed-gender?

Yes, by default, and it is entirely normal and unproblematic. Both soft and hard sleeper are allocated without regard to gender. Travellers who would rather not share a closed compartment often prefer an upper hard-sleeper berth, which is more open and has more people around.

### Are sleeper trains worth it in China?

On long non-high-speed routes, often yes: the journey replaces a hotel night and uses hours you would otherwise spend travelling in daylight. On any route where a high-speed G train exists and takes under about five hours, take the G train instead.
