Two things are true at once in China, and most guidance picks one and drops the other.

A visitor who spends a week in central Beijing or Shanghai may not sit in a smoky room once. A visitor who eats in an older restaurant in a smaller city may find someone lighting up at the next table while a no-smoking sign hangs on the wall. Both are the normal outcome of the same system, and it is worth ten minutes to understand why.

## There is no national indoor smoking ban

This is the fact that reorganises everything else. China has no single nationwide law making indoor public places smoke-free. What it has instead is a patchwork of municipal and provincial regulations, each with its own scope, its own fines and its own enforcement appetite.

The patchwork is large and growing. State media reported in May 2024 that 254 cities and 24 provincial-level regions had smoking-control regulations in place, 44 of them newly introduced or revised during 2023 alone. The national target under the Healthy China programme is to bring 80% of the population under comprehensive smoke-free legislation by 2030 — a target that only makes sense as a statement that the country is not there yet.

Nor is the underlying habit going away quickly. The reported smoking rate among people aged 15 and over was 24.1% in 2022, down 1.7 percentage points on 2020, against a 2030 goal of 20%.

So the practical rule for a visitor is geographic, not national: **the bigger and richer the city, the more likely the indoor rule is real.**

With one qualification worth knowing, because it is the exception that shows the shape of the rule. There is no *general* national ban, but there are **venue-specific** national ones written into the regulations governing particular kinds of premises — [an internet café or games arcade must prohibit smoking and display a no-smoking sign](/culture/internet-cafes-and-gaming-in-china/) under a State Council regulation, in every city, whatever the local smoking law says.

It is worth noticing that this is not how every delegated power in China works. [With fireworks the national instrument fixes the shape of the local decision — time, place and type — and a city that replaces it with a flat ban has gone too far](/guides/fireworks-in-china/). With smoking the national floor is simply low, and a city going further is doing exactly what the system expects.

## The cities you are most likely to be in

**Beijing** has been comprehensively smoke-free indoors since **1 June 2015**, under the Beijing Smoking Control Regulation (北京市控制吸烟条例, *Běijīng Shì Kòngzhì Xīyān Tiáolì*). It covers every indoor public place, every indoor workplace and public transport, and extends to some outdoor settings such as schools, hospitals and stadiums. It was widely described at the time as the country's toughest. The personal penalty is modest — ¥50, rising to ¥200 for someone who refuses to stop — but the norm it created has largely held, and restaurant staff in Beijing will generally enforce it rather than look away.

**Shanghai** followed on **1 March 2017** with a revised version of its own regulation, banning smoking in all indoor public places, indoor workplaces and public transport. Shanghai then did something most Chinese cities have not: on **28 October 2022** its municipal People's Congress amended the regulation so that the definition of smoking includes **e-cigarettes**, which brought vaping indoors under exactly the same prohibition.

**[Shenzhen](/destinations/shenzhen/)** reached a fully smoke-free indoor standard from **1 January 2017**, and later extended its rules to e-cigarettes and to outdoor waiting areas, including station platforms — an unusually broad scope that most cities do not match.

Outside those, assume less. A no-smoking sign in a third-tier city restaurant is a statement of intent more often than a statement of fact, and the older and cheaper the establishment, the truer that is. If smoke matters to you, the reliable moves are the ordinary ones: chain restaurants, hotel restaurants, shopping-mall floors, anywhere with visible corporate management, and outdoor tables.

## Trains: the one rule that bites

Everything above is soft compared with what happens on a train, and this is the part a visitor genuinely needs to know before boarding.

Smoking on a **high-speed train is prohibited absolutely** — no smoking area, no exception, and the toilet and the vestibule are not loopholes. The rule sits in the **Regulations on Railway Safety Management** (铁路安全管理条例, *Tiělù Ānquán Guǎnlǐ Tiáolì*), issued as State Council Order No. 639 on 17 August 2013 and in force since 1 January 2014. Article 77 prohibits smoking on 动车组 (*dòngchēzǔ*, the multiple-unit trains that run the G, D and C services) and in the no-smoking areas of any other train. Article 95 sets the penalty for an individual at **¥500 to ¥2,000**.

The fine is not the serious part. China Railway's own passenger-credit notice lists smoking on a high-speed train as a recorded behaviour, and the consequence is a **180-day restriction on buying rail tickets**, with the record retained for five years. On a trip whose spine is the rail network, that is not a fine — it is the end of the itinerary. [How Chinese rail actually works](/guides/transport-in-china/) explains why the network is the default way to move around the country, which is precisely what makes this penalty disproportionate to the offence.

There is a physical reason the rule is absolute rather than decorative. High-speed carriages carry smoke detectors wired into the braking system, and setting one off slows or stops the train. That is also why **an e-cigarette is not a workaround**: the vapour trips the same sensors, and the offence recorded is the same one.

:::warn Conventional trains are the exception, and only in one specific place
Article 77 bans smoking on multiple-unit trains outright, but on *other* trains it bans it only in the designated no-smoking areas — which is why older, slower **普速** (*pǔsù*, conventional) services still have a tolerated smoking spot at the ends of carriages, and why you may see it and assume the rule is loose. It is not. If your ticket is for a G, D or C train, there is nowhere on board where smoking is permitted.
:::

## Platforms and stations sit in the gap

The railway regulation governs trains, not open-air platforms, and China Railway has said publicly that platforms are largely open environments carrying no national no-smoking rule. Some large stations run enclosed smoking rooms, whose compatibility with indoor smoking bans has itself been litigated. Where a city's own regulation is broad enough — Shenzhen and Shanghai both extend to crowded outdoor transport waiting areas — the local rule governs the platform even though the national one does not.

The practical read: a platform is where people smoke, station buildings vary, and the moment you step onto a high-speed train the rule becomes absolute.

## Vaping, specifically

Vaping in China is legal but tightly standardised, and the standardisation is why visitors find the shelves disappointing.

The State Tobacco Monopoly Administration's **Measures for the Administration of Electronic Cigarettes** (电子烟管理办法, *Diànzǐyān Guǎnlǐ Bànfǎ*) took effect on **1 May 2022**, and the mandatory national standard **GB 41700-2022** followed on **1 October 2022**. Between them they require that a product carry no characterising flavour other than tobacco, and that the aerosol contain nicotine — which removes both flavoured e-liquid and nicotine-free products from legal sale.

Bringing a personal device is a different matter from buying liquid locally. Assume you will not find your flavour, and note that where a city has folded e-cigarettes into its smoking ban, as Shanghai did in 2022, vaping indoors is simply smoking indoors as far as the rule is concerned. One national rule does bind every seller of both: [tobacco and alcohol may not be sold to anyone under eighteen, and the shop must post a sign saying so](/answers/what-is-the-legal-drinking-age-in-china/). The [health and safety guide](/guides/health-and-safety-in-china/) covers where this sits among the other legal lines worth not crossing.

## Cigarettes as a social object

One thing that surprises Western visitors, particularly on business: offering cigarettes is a courtesy ritual, not merely an offer. A packet produced and passed round at a table, a single cigarette held out two-handed to the most senior person present — this is the same grammar as pouring tea for someone else before yourself.

You can decline. A smile, a hand raised, and 我不抽烟 (*wǒ bù chōu yān*, "I don't smoke") closes it without friction, and nobody will press. What does not work is treating the offer as an imposition, because it was meant as the opposite. [Chinese etiquette](/culture/chinese-etiquette/) sets out the wider set of table conventions this belongs to, and it is worth knowing before a hosted dinner rather than during one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you smoke in restaurants in China?

It depends entirely on the city. Beijing, Shanghai and Shenzhen prohibit smoking in all indoor public places and enforcement there is generally real. There is no national indoor smoking ban, so in smaller cities and older establishments a no-smoking sign may not be observed. Chain restaurants, hotel restaurants and mall floors are the reliable choices anywhere.

### Is smoking banned on Chinese trains?

On high-speed trains, absolutely and everywhere on board, including the toilets and vestibules. The Regulations on Railway Safety Management set a fine of ¥500 to ¥2,000, and China Railway additionally imposes a 180-day ban on buying tickets. Conventional slower trains still have a tolerated smoking area at the carriage ends, which is the only exception.

### Can I vape in China?

Yes, but flavoured e-liquid and nicotine-free products cannot legally be sold there — the national standard in force since October 2022 permits only tobacco flavour and requires nicotine in the aerosol. Bring your own supply. Some cities, Shanghai among them, treat vaping indoors as smoking indoors and ban it on the same terms.

### What happens if you smoke on a Chinese high-speed train?

You are fined between ¥500 and ¥2,000, and you are placed on China Railway's passenger credit record, which blocks ticket purchases for 180 days and is retained for five years. Carriages have smoke detectors linked to the braking system, so lighting up can also slow or stop the train — and an e-cigarette trips the same sensors.

### Does China have a national smoking ban?

No. Smoking control in China is set city by city and province by province rather than by one national law. State media reported 254 cities and 24 provincial-level regions with regulations as of May 2024, and the national target is to cover 80% of the population with comprehensive smoke-free legislation by 2030.

### How do I politely refuse a cigarette in China?

Say 我不抽烟 (*wǒ bù chōu yān*) with a smile and a raised hand. Offering cigarettes is a courtesy ritual rather than pressure, so declining is unremarkable and will not be taken badly. Refusing brusquely, as though the offer were rude, is the only version that lands wrong.
