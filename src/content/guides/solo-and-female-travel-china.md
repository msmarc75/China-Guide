## The safety question

Solo travellers, including women, consistently report that Chinese cities feel safer than European or North American ones. Violent crime is rare, street harassment is uncommon, public spaces are busy late into the evening, and the density of cameras and police presence — whatever one thinks of it in general — has a straightforward effect on street crime.

Concretely: walking alone at 11 p.m. through central Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu or Xi'an is normal and unremarkable.

That said, apply the usual habits: use DiDi rather than unlicensed cars at night, watch your drink in nightlife districts in Sanlitun or the Bund, and be alert to the [scams that target foreigners](/guides/health-and-safety-in-china/) — which are non-violent but designed around isolating you.

## Attention, staring and photos

In big cities you are unremarkable. In smaller cities, rural areas and the far west, a foreign traveller — especially one who is visibly non-Chinese — attracts attention: sustained staring, and requests for photographs.

This is curiosity rather than hostility, and it is almost never sexual in nature. Being asked for a selfie by a family, a group of students or a tour group from another province is extremely common. You can say yes; you can also say no with a smile and a hand gesture, and it is accepted without offence.

If someone photographs you without asking and you would rather they did not, a clear "不要" (*bú yào*, "don't") works.

## Eating alone

This is the genuine practical difficulty, and nobody warns people about it.

Chinese restaurant dining is built around shared dishes. A menu is designed for a table of four ordering six plates. A solo diner ordering two dishes will get a strange look and enough food for three people.

Solutions, in ascending order of usefulness:

1. **Order from the noodle, rice-bowl and dumpling sections.** These are single-serving by design and are what Chinese people eat alone.
2. **Eat at a *xiaochi* (小吃) place** — small-eats shops, breakfast stalls, wonton shops. Portions are individual and the food is often the best in the neighbourhood.
3. **Go to hotpot chains with individual pots.** Haidilao famously seats solo diners with a large soft toy across the table; more usefully, chains like Xiaolongkan and many *shuàn* restaurants offer single pots.
4. **Use food courts** in shopping malls: photo menus, individual portions, cheap, and no interaction required.
5. **Accept the over-ordering.** Ask for the leftovers boxed — 打包 (*dǎ bāo*) — which is completely normal.

More on ordering in our [guide to ordering food in China](/food/how-to-order-food-in-china/).

:::tip The single most useful Chinese word for a solo traveller
**一个人** (*yí ge rén*) — "one person". Say it at the door of a restaurant and you will be seated appropriately, often at a small table or the counter.
:::

## Meeting people

**Hostels** are the reliable answer. China's hostel scene is genuinely good, with common rooms, organised day trips and English-speaking staff. The best social hostels are in **Yangshuo, Dali, Lijiang, Chengdu, Pingyao and Yangshuo's countryside**, less so in Beijing and Shanghai, where the scene is more transactional.

**Chinese people will talk to you**, particularly younger people wanting English practice, on trains and in queues. Long-distance train journeys are a social environment: shared snacks, shared curiosity, translation apps passed back and forth.

**WeChat is the social infrastructure.** Adding people you meet is the norm and is how any follow-up happens.

**Structured options:** cooking classes in Chengdu and Yangshuo, tai chi in park mornings, calligraphy workshops, and day tours where the group is the point.

## Practicalities that affect solo travellers specifically

- **Single rooms are rare.** Chinese hotels price by room, not by person, so a solo traveller pays the double rate. Hostels and the budget chains soften this.
- **Book trains and tickets under your own passport.** No issue, but you cannot have someone else hold your ticket.
- **Photography.** You will run out of photos of yourself. Chinese travellers are enthusiastic and skilled photographers and are usually delighted to be asked.
- **Illness alone.** Have insurance with a 24-hour line, keep your hotel's address in Chinese saved, and tell someone at home your route.
- **Hiking alone** in remote areas — Tiger Leaping Gorge, unrestored Great Wall sections such as Jiankou — carries real risk. Jiankou in particular has caused deaths. Go with someone, or take a guided group.

## Notes for women specifically

- **Dress** is unremarkable in cities; shorts, dresses and vests are all normal in Shanghai, Guangzhou and Chengdu. Cover shoulders and knees at working temples, and dress more conservatively in Xinjiang and rural Tibetan areas.
- **Sanitary products:** pads are available everywhere; **tampons are stocked patchily**. Bring your own supply.
- **Public toilets** are ubiquitous and free, frequently squat-style, and frequently without paper or soap. Carry tissues and hand sanitiser.
- **Night trains:** soft-sleeper compartments are mixed-gender by default and this is entirely normal. If it makes you uncomfortable, book the top bunk in a hard sleeper (more open, more people around) or take a day train.
- **Bar and nightlife districts** — Sanlitun in Beijing, the Bund and Found 158 in Shanghai — have the same risks as anywhere: watch your drink, leave with people you came with, take DiDi home.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is China safe for solo female travellers?

Yes, notably so. Street harassment is uncommon, cities are safe to walk in at night, and violent crime against visitors is rare. Normal precautions apply around nightlife districts and with unlicensed taxis, and the tea-house and bar scams target solo travellers specifically.

### Is it awkward to eat alone in China?

Not socially, but restaurant portions are designed for sharing, so a solo diner over-orders easily. Order from the noodle, dumpling and rice-bowl sections, use small-eats shops and mall food courts, or choose hotpot chains that offer individual pots.

### How do I meet people travelling alone in China?

Stay in hostels, especially in Yangshuo, Dali, Lijiang, Chengdu and Pingyao, where the social scene is strongest. Long-distance trains are also naturally sociable, and cooking classes and day tours provide structure. Add people on WeChat — it is how every follow-up happens.

### Do people stare at foreigners in China?

In big cities, rarely. In smaller cities, rural areas and the far west, yes — sustained staring and requests for photographs are common. It is curiosity rather than hostility, and declining politely is accepted without offence.

### Is it safe to take overnight trains alone in China?

Yes. Sleeper compartments are mixed-gender and this is normal and unproblematic. Keep valuables in your bed with you rather than in the luggage rack, and use the chain lock on soft-sleeper doors if there is one.
