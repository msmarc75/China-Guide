The shopping stop is the most predictable feature of a cheap organised tour in China, and travellers generally treat it as an irritation to be endured. It is better understood as the business model — and as something the law names and prohibits.

## Why the stop is on the itinerary at all

A tour sold below what it costs to run has to make the money back somewhere. That somewhere is a commission arrangement with a jade workshop, a tea house, a silk factory or a "traditional medicine" clinic, which pays the operator a share of whatever the group spends.

Once you see that, the rest follows. The stop is not a cultural extra that ran long. It is the part of the day that pays for the coach, and the pressure you feel there is proportional to how cheap your ticket was.

## What the Tourism Law actually says

China's **Tourism Law** — adopted 25 April 2013 and amended in 2016 and again in 2018 — addresses this directly in Article 35.

> 旅行社不得以不合理的低价组织旅游活动，诱骗旅游者，并通过安排购物或者另行付费旅游项目获取回扣等不正当利益。旅行社组织、接待旅游者，不得指定具体购物场所，不得安排另行付费旅游项目。但是，经双方协商一致或者旅游者要求，且不影响其他旅游者行程安排的除外。

Three prohibitions and one exception:

- A travel agency **may not organise tours at an unreasonably low price** to lure travellers and then take kickbacks through shopping or paid extra items.
- It **may not designate specific shopping venues**.
- It **may not arrange paid extra programmes** on top of the tour.
- **Unless** both sides genuinely agree, or you asked for it, and it does not disrupt the other travellers' arrangements.

That last clause is the one operators lean on, and it is worth knowing its shape. An agreed visit to a workshop you asked to see is lawful. A stop that appears on the day, announced by the guide, is not made lawful by nobody objecting.

## The part almost no English guidance mentions

Article 35 does not stop at prohibiting. It gives you a remedy, and an unusually practical one.

Where the agency has broken those rules, **you may require it, within thirty days of the tour ending, to process a return of the goods and advance the refund itself** — or to refund the cost of the paid extra programme.

Read that carefully, because the mechanism is the valuable part. You do not have to find the jade shop again, argue with it, or prove anything about its pricing. The obligation sits on **the travel agency you contracted with**, and the money is meant to come from the agency first, with it then settling up with the shop. The thirty days runs from the end of the trip, not from the purchase.

This is why the receipt matters more than the argument. Keep what you were given, note which day and which stop, and raise it with the agency in writing.

## Get the contract in writing

The same law requires a travel agency to enter into a contract with you, and requires that contract to be in written form. A tour sold on a messaging app with no written itinerary is already outside the framework the rest of this page relies on, which is a reason to decline it rather than a technicality.

A written itinerary is also what makes an unscheduled shopping stop visible as a departure from what you bought.

## A licensed agency is not the same problem as a scam

Keep two situations apart, because the remedies are completely different.

A licensed agency running a legitimate tour with an unlawful shopping stop is a **contractual and regulatory** matter: you have the thirty-day right above, and there is an agency with a licence to lose. That is the situation this page is about.

Someone selling tours on a station forecourt who drives you to a replica exhibition is not that. [The fake tours sold outside Xi'an's railway station](/answers/how-do-i-get-to-the-terracotta-army-from-xian/) have no contract, no licence and nobody to complain to afterwards, which is exactly why that page tells you not to buy them in the first place.

## Practical advice that follows

- **Prefer a private driver and your own plan** over a cheap group tour where the itinerary is vague — the [Great Wall guide](/destinations/great-wall-of-china/) makes the same call for the same reason.
- **Treat an unusually low price as information**, not a bargain. It tells you where the margin is coming from.
- **Do not buy at the stop.** The thirty-day right is a repair, not a plan, and the simplest version of this is owning nothing you need to return.
- **If you did buy jade, read the certificate rather than the sales patter.** The laboratory conclusion has to say whether the stone is natural or treated, and [the trade's "A货" claim is checkable against it](/guides/buying-jade-in-china/) — though it will not tell you anything about what you were charged.
- **If you do buy, keep everything** — receipt, packaging, the itinerary, the agency's name.
- **Buy tea somewhere else entirely.** [Where to buy tea worth carrying home](/culture/tea-culture-in-china/) is a different conversation from a tea ceremony arranged by a coach driver.
- **Anything bought is still subject to export rules**, and [the antiques and wildlife categories customs stops](/guides/shopping-in-china/) are stricter than most published advice suggests.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are shopping stops on Chinese tours legal?

Generally no. Article 35 of China's Tourism Law prohibits travel agencies from designating specific shopping venues or arranging paid extra programmes, and from running below-cost tours funded by kickbacks. The exception is a stop both sides genuinely agreed to, or that you requested, and that does not disrupt other travellers.

### Can I get a refund for something bought on a tour shopping stop in China?

Yes, if the stop breached those rules. The Tourism Law gives you thirty days from the end of the tour to require the travel agency to process a return and advance the refund itself, rather than sending you back to the shop. Keep the receipt and the itinerary.

### Why do cheap Chinese tours always include a jade factory?

Because a tour sold below cost recovers the difference through commission on what the group buys. The shopping stop is the revenue, which is why the pressure there tends to rise as the ticket price falls.

### Does a Chinese tour have to give me a written contract?

Yes. The Tourism Law requires a travel agency to conclude a contract with travellers and requires it to be in writing. A tour arranged with no written itinerary sits outside that framework, which is a reason to avoid it.

### What if the tour was not a licensed agency at all?

Then the thirty-day right has nobody to attach to. Unlicensed sellers outside stations and major sights are a different problem with no realistic remedy afterwards — the defence is not buying, which is why the guidance on fake tours is about avoidance rather than recourse.
